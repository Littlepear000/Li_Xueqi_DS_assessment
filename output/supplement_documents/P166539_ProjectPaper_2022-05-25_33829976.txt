 I. PROJECT STATUS AND RATIONALE FOR RESTRUCTURING

        A. Project Status

1.        More than two and a half years since Board approval, the Ghana Economic Transformation Project (GETP) has
made moderately satisfactory (MS) progress in implementation and achievement of its Project Development Objective
(PDO). The current disbursement rate is 10.86 percent (equivalent to US$21.7 million) of the US$200 million credit, which
is just less than half of the originally estimated disbursement projection by end Q3 FY22. The slower than expected pace is
partly due to the long delay in reaching project effectiveness (August 31, 2019), which took more than one year from Board
approval, compounded with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Very few results have been recorded to-date, except for
the number of Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) and entrepreneurs receiving financial support, which has already
surpassed the end target of 150 (nearly 400ave received financial support through the COVID19 recovery grant scheme
implemented by the Ghana Enterprise Agency (GEA), of which 40 percent are led by women). Three Year 1 Performance
Based Conditions (PBCs), formerly known as Disbursement-Linked Indicators, have been met, totally US$6 million for about
one-third of project disbursements to-date.

2.      Progress across project components and with the Technical Implementing Agencies (TIAs) has been uneven. This
has been compounded by several institutional arrangement challenges, including having ten (10) TIAs, three (3) project
governance committees (Project Oversight Committee (POC), Project Steering Committee (PSC), Technical Committee) and
the Project Coordinating Unit (PCU), all which work closely with the Ministry of Finance (MOF) which has coordination and
fiduciary responsibility for the GETP. Other than Component 4 (Project Management), Component 3 (Accelerating
Entrepreneurship and SME Growth) is the component that has progressed the most in terms of both disbursement and the
achievement of results. In Component 3 there are three TIAs, including NEIP (National Entrepreneurship and Innovation
Program), VCTF (Venture Capital Trust Fund) and GEA. At present, however, only GEA has managed to disburse significant
funds via the COVID19 grants. VCTF has until recently been waiting to have its new Board inaugurated (this is now done) in
order to work closely with its Investment Committee and staff to fund child trust funds (three child trust funds will soon
receive US$11 million in funding; with another US$5 million possible before June 30, 2022), and for NEIP much of the work
was put on hold in the last year given the vacancy of its Chief Executive Officer (CEO), a post which was recently filled.

3.       Much work has progressed under Components 1: Enabling Investments albeit with few results to date. It should
be noted that, under Component 1, a high-level Inter-Ministerial Committee on business regulatory reform (BRR) was
established in 2021 which triggered a US$1 million disbursement in FY21 under a PBC. Unfortunately there has been little
movement on the BRR agenda beyond the creation of the IMC. With the dropping of the Doing Business Reform Report, the
Ministry of Trade and Industry (MOTI) has had to re-think the BRR agenda. Support for the Registrar General’s Department
(RGD) to transform to the Office of Registrar of Companies (ORC) is also part of this project, but much time has been spent
on locating sites and buildings and less time in finalizing work on a new business registration platform. Support from a
complementary Advisory Services and Analytics project (P176172, supported by the European Union’s ACP-IEE trust fund)
has helped the Ghana Standards Authority (GSA) move closer to meeting two DLIs (the enactment of the Ghana Standards
Bill and the adoption of a National Quality Policy; the National Quality Policy will be submitted to the Bank in June 2022 for
reimbursement, while the Ghana Standards Bill is expected to be received in July 2022). Finally it is now recognized that the
GETP is the natural place to support Ghana’s emerging trade agenda, including the Africa Continental Free Trade Agreement
(AfCFTA), trade facilitation and exports, as well as logistics. This work has not been part of the GETP work program to date
and is being added during this restructuring.

4.      Component 2: Crowding-In Investments : promoting spatial and industrial planning and development has also
had much work but few disbursements, therefore, results. For example the two TIAs, MOTI and the Ghana Free Zones
Authority (GFZA), have at times had separate work programs and thus struggled with alignment. This has now changed. The

         Ghana Economic Transformation Project (P166539)


project is now taking a renewed focus on needed policy reforms (including the development of a Special Economic Zone
(SEZ) policy, followed by an SEZ law and enabling regulations). GFZA is now transforming into a single SEZ authority,
something the project can support. Equally important is the alignment within Government of the importance that the GETP
support through a targeted Viability Gap Fund (VGF) to benefit the Greater Kumasi Industrial City Park and SEZ (GKICP &
SEZ), versus using limited VGF resources more widely in Government. The Government is now also asking that a new Project
Developoment Facility (PDF) be created within the GETP to support private participation in infrastructure through public-
private partnerships (PPPs), with the view to fund infrastructure studies and advisory work (legal, technical, financial). In
summary, it is necessary to do a comprehensive restructure of Component 2 at this time.

5.       Project management has also had a mixed track record so far. For example, greater strength is needed in the
Project Coordinator position, including in the work with the Technical Committee and the 10 focal points of the TIAs. While
the POC and the Technical Committee were constituted at the start of the project as laid out in the Project Appraisal
Document (PAD) and the Project Implementation Manual (PIM), in practice they have only marginally contributed to project
implementation. Following Board approval, it was also agreed to set up a Project Steering Committee (PSC), which is
responsible for approving the Annual Work Plans and Budgets (AWPBs) but has not had any other role so far. In this regard,
the client has agreed to reconstitute the work of the PSC with a greater focus to include the heads of the TIAs, with MoF
and MOTI as co-Chairs. As part of the recent restructuring discussions, it has been agreed that, to increase disbursements
and achieve the PDO within the timeframe of the project, the heads of agencies will work closely with MoF to improve
performance and achieve synergies amongst the project’s ten TIAs with strong input from MoF at the center. Overall PCU
performance has been uneven, especially with respect to procurement, coordination, and contract management.


        B. Rationale for Restructuring

6.      There is a need to restructure the GETP to make up for lost time and ensure that the PDO is still achievable
within the project lifetime (the project’s closing date is November 30, 2025). Specifically, there is a need to accelerate
implementation of activities so that results can be achieved within the six-year life of the project. In doing so, the overall
budget needs to be reallocated from slow-moving components to better performing and new components. Within sub-
components, budgets have been streamlined to support more impactful activities which contribute to the PDO and to the
Government of Ghana’s priorities. PBC indicators have been reviewed to focus more on results and to ensure allocated
budgets cover all activities/expenditures needed to meet the PBCs. The results framework has also been revised to make
sure that it accurately and realistically captures results for which the GETP is contributing.

7.       Several developments since project approval have also led to changes that need to be reflected through project
restructuring. First, the recent discontinuation of the World Bank Doing Business Report has a bearing on the project’s
results indicators and PBCs, many of which were linked to the Ease of Doing Business score. Second, emerging priorities
within MOTI, the main TIA for the GETP, affect budget allocations and PBCs under Components 1 and 2. These priorities
remain within the PDO and include the BRR agenda, the trade agenda including the recent Africa Continental Free Trade
Agreement (AfCFTA), the relatively recent creation of the Ghana National Accreditation Service (GhaNAS), and the focus
on GKICP & SEZ. Importantly, there is a recognition in the Government and within the Bank that US$63 million currently
allocated for the VGF under Component 2 is being 'warehoused' and will not soon be used; thus, it has been agreed that
this capitalization is not needed, but the work is still needed to make the VGF operational for future needs, especially at
GKICP & SEZ. With the scaling down of Component 2, it is recognized that monies should be reallocated to SME grants
(including COVID grants) and new trade activities. In Section II below are references to the new trade activities that address
directly the several challenges Ghana faces on the trading across borders front, including how project funds are being
repurposed to support firm resilience, limiting the available budget for SME support as originally designed in the project
and requiring further reallocation to fill the gap. The project has also been restructured to provide greater support to the
         The World Bank
         Ghana Economic Transformation Project (P166539)


PCU and ten TIAs, and the DLIs have been amended to PBCs as per Bank OPCS guidance. Previously all DLI funding was to
support staff salaries. Going forward, this will not be the case as the underlying expenditure will minimize the funding of
salaries, and almost all funding will be for the procurement and non-procurement related activities that, together, support
the achievement of individual PBCs. Finally, the Results Framework (including the Theory of Change) has been updated
and revised to better capture the needed project results and to confirm their attribution.


 II. DESCRIPTION OF PROPOSED CHANGES

        C. Changes to Component 1: Enabling Investments

    (a) Sub-component 1.1: Improving the Business Regulatory Environment – changes in results indicators and PBCs

8.         Given the recent discontinuation of the Doing Business Report, it is urgent to now revise the sub-component’s
results indicator and PBCs related to Doing Business and the Ease of Doing Business Score. This sub-component will also
have a reduced budget because of the restructuring, moving from US$11.5 million to US$10.5 million. Only one of the
PBCs has been achieved, triggering a US$1 million disbursement in FY21 (out of a total of US$5 million in PBCs). The PDO
indicator measuring improvement in the Ease of Doing Business Score is replaced by the “reduction in time to obtain
construction permits” (from a baseline of 87 days to an end target of 40 days; this will also be a PBC) and the “reduction
in time to resolve commercial disputes at district courts” (from a baseline of 204 days to an end target of 106 days; a new
intermediate result indicator will measure the number of district courts fully automated with an end target of 10 from the
current baseline of zero). The new PBCs proposed to replace all current PBCs not yet achieved/due are the following:
     (i)       A new BRR Act is enacted in 2023 establishing MOTI’s BRR Unit as an Agency, with an associated budget of
               US$450,000.
     (ii)      Citizen engagement in rulemaking is improved through the Ghana Business Regulatory Reform Portal (scalable
               PBC measuring the percentage of regulations and policies receiving inputs through the portal, with an end
               target of 30 percent), for a total budget of US$300,000.
     (iii)     The time it takes to obtain construction permits is reduced (scalable PBC using the PDO indicator measuring
               the same result), for a total budget of US$600,000.

    (b) Sub-component 1.2: Investment Policy and Promotion – changes to results indicators, PBCs, and cost

9.       The budget for this sub-component will be scaled down to focus on more targeted support and priority areas;
the intermediate results indicators and PBCs for the Ghana Investment Promotion Centre (GIPC) will also be revised as
a result. While the original project description included supporting legislative reforms to minimize sector-specific
investment barriers, the current focus has shifted, partly due to the pandemic, to supporting the design and
implementation of an investment retention mechanism to address investors’ grievance. Support will also cover an online
investment registration process to reduce the time and cost for investors to register. The overall budget for the sub-
component is reduced from US$12.5 million to US$9 million. The following changes to the PBCs and intermediate results
indicators are proposed with a view to streamline implementation:
    (i) A new PBC will measure growth in investment commitments (target of US$500 million in Foreign Direct
         Investment (FDI) attracted by 2025 and scalable over the life of the project) with an allocation of US$4.5 million.

         US$20 million to US$200 million).
    (ii) A new intermediate result indicator on the number of systems and tools approved and launched will be introduced
         (target of 6 by project-end).


    (c) Sub-component 1.3: Improving Ghana’s Quality Infrastructure (QI) – changes to TIAs, results indicators, and
        PBCs

10.        The main changes under this sub-component are to introduce the relatively new GhaNAS as an additional TIA,
to introduce a PBC and results indicator related to GhaNAS work, and to define the budget allocation between the two
QI TIAs. Based on the proposed changes, GhaNAS will be allocated US$4 million to undertake activities under the project,
thereby reducing the current US$11 million budget for the sub-component to US$10.5 million. The details of the main
changes are as follows:
     (i)      A PBC on the enactment of the National Accreditation Bill and related legislation is added to reflect support
              to GhaNAS under the GETP. The PBC is expected to be met in Year 4 of the project for a total allocation of
              US$1.1 million.
     (ii)     The Year 2 PBC for GSA is deleted, as the strategic plan was recently completed under the project and the PBC
              targets deemed no longer relevant considering the plan’s recommendations.
     (iii)    An intermediate results indicator on the number of Conformity Assessment Bodies certified by GhaNAS is
              introduced, with an end target of 10.

    (d) New sub-component 1.4: Trade

11.     The new trade sub-component, with a total budget of US$10 million, will support (1) the implementation of
multilateral, regional, and bilateral trade frameworks, (2) a AfCFTA market expansion program, (3) trade in services,
and (4) trade facilitation and border management. More specifically, the sub-component will build MOTI’s capacity to
implement trade treaties, prepare negotiation proposals for AfCFTA, and implement commitments under AfCFTA. Under
the market expansion program, the project will provide one-stop support for exporting firms or firms with potential to
export to help increase their market access under AfCFTA and other key agreements. Relating to services, the project will
conduct a regulatory assessment of services trade (also vis-à-vis trade agreements) and revise regulations and investment
frameworks accordingly. Finally, the project seeks to improve the governance for trade facilitation, improve customs and
border management, and support implementation of the World Trade Organization’s Trade Facilitation Agreement.

           D. Changes to Component 2: Crowding-In Investments: promoting spatial and industrial planning and
              development

    (a) Changes to PBCs

12.      Component 2 (spatial development and crowding in investments) existing PBCs ($11m) will be dropped and
replaced by two PBCs ($5m) to streamline and intensify spatial development and crowding in of private investment.
There are currently three PBCs totaling US$11 million, and none have been achieved thus far. The two new PBCs are as
follows:

    (i)       Adoption of a new SEZ Policy in 2022, scaled to a new SEZ Act in 2023 and new SEZ Regulations also in 2023.
              The SEZ agenda is new in Ghana. MOTI has recently recruited a consultant to support the development of the
              SEZ Policy. Given the Bank’s vast experience in this area, the Bank will work closely with the client to ensure
              this work is completed against the target dates (new PBC budget of US$3 million).
    (ii)      Establishment of a new SEZ authority: the Ghana Free Zones Authority (GFZA) was set up under MOTI to
              enable the establishment of free zones in Ghana and to provide for the regulation of activities in free zones.
              Given the shift from free zones to SEZs and to also support private sector industrial parks, GFZA will be
              repurposed as a new authority responsible for establishing and regulating the activities of SEZs, free zones
              and industrial parks. The GETP will facilitate this process (new PBC budget of US$2 million).

    (b) Changes to Component costs

13.      Given MOTI’s focus on developing the GKICP & SEZ, it is critical that the VGF (Viability Gap Fund), which has not
been established, be made operational starting end-2022/early 2023. However, as there is no private sector developer
at Kumasi today, the VGF’s capital will be scaled down to US$0 and its budget reallocated to other pressing activities.
Government will need to review options available to fund the VGF scheme to support last mile infrastructure for private
investment. MOTI has expressed the government’s intent to develop GKICP & SEZ as a top priority. The project has already
financed safeguards and other studies for Kumasi development and will also be financing new technical and feasibility
studies which will give a better indication of the level of VGF funding needed to develop GKICP & SEZ once a developer
has been selected. As a first step, the GETP will support the establishment of the VGF in late 2022 (or early 2023) in line
with the requirements of the 2020 Public-Private Partnership (PPP) Act that provides for a Viability Gap Scheme (VGS)
which too has not yet been operationalized. It has been agreed that, as part of this restructuring, the VGF would be set
starting late 2022 (or early 2023) would only eventually finance the development infrastructure at GKICP & SEZ, when this
project is ready. The VGF will be recapitalized in future at the time when a private sector zone developer at Kumasi has
been identified with a concession contract in place.

14.     Part of the freed-up budget (U$15m) following the scaling down of the VGF will be allocated towards a Project
Development Facility (PDF) to support infrastructure development in SEZs. Under this restructuring, MOTI has agreed to
establish in 2022 a new US$15m revolving (or reimbursable) PDF that pays for (1) advisory work (legal, technical, financial)
and (2) studies (e.g., pre-feasibility studies, feasibility studies, Environmental Impact Assessments, Environmental and
Social Impact Assessments, demand forecasts, etc.) related to infrastructure development to support infrastructure
development at the country’s SEZs and to attract private sector investors to fund the needed follow-on infrastructure,
with the initial development costs borne by the PDF but repaid by developers and investors who compete and successfully
move ahead SEZ infrastructure and pay for the related costs. Given the priority placed by the Government on development
at the GKICP & SEZ, it is expected that advisory and study work will relate to the focus on Kumasi. As each advisory and/or
study cost is repaid by the investors, the PDF is repaid/reimbursed and thus the PDF is recapitalized. This approach also
         The World Bank
         Ghana Economic Transformation Project (P166539)


keeps the Government in charge of SEZ infrastructure development as it decides which zones to develop first, etc. The
US$15 million for which the PDF is capitalized will be utilized by MOTI.

15.      Finally, given the top priority that the Government places to infrastructure development at GKICP & SEZ, the
project will re-allocate US$30 million of Component 2 funds to public infrastructure development at or near Kumasi,
the funds used to attract a zone developer and investors. The Government and Bank recognize the many challenges to
attract private sector developers and investors without the Government first investing in priority infrastructure
investments. The project also recognizes that infrastructure (e.g., roads, power, water, fences) is necessary before the
private sector will show sufficient interest to partner with Government in PPP arrangements such as SEZs. It is expected
that this US$30 million in public infrastructure support will be combined with Government-own resources and possibly
funding from other donors given the tremendous needs at Kumasi.

16.     Finally, US$10 million as well as US$20 million of Component 2 resources will be re-allocated during the
restructuring to Component 1 trade activities and Component 3 support for high-growth SMEs and COVID grants,
respectively. Given the Component 2 spend to date and other procurement commitments, the changes outlined reduce
the overall component budget from US$79 million to US$50 million.

    (c) Changes to results indicators

17.      The intermediate results indicators related to Component 2 will be revised to reflect the new scope of work, as
follows: the indicator on VGF establishment (yes/no) is maintained; the indicator on the number of infrastructure sub-
projects/investments implemented under the VGF is scaled back (the end target of 10 is reduced to 2); a new indicator is
introduced to reflect PDF establishment (yes/no; target is yes by 2022); a new indicator is introduced to track the number
of infrastructure sub-projects/ investments supported by the PDF (with an end target of 10).

        E. Changes to Component 3: Accelerating Entrepreneurship and MSME Growth

    (a) Sub-component 3.1: Reform and Rationalization of Government Entrepreneurship and MSME Support Agencies
        – changes to PBCs

18.     The main change to this sub-component is the deletion of the PBC related to public entrepreneurship funding
totaling US$4 million. During project preparation, it was believed that a new apex agency for MSME support would be
created and would bring together all institutions responsible for entrepreneurship and firm support in Ghana (namely the
National Board for Small Scale Industries (NBSSI) and the NEIP). As such, a PBC was included to incentivize the government
to consolidate entrepreneurship funding within this new apex body. Shortly after Board approval, the GEA was established
by an Act of Parliament and replaced NBSSI while NEIP remained as a separate entity which now sits under the Office of
the President (it was formerly under the Ministry of Business Development which was dissolved after the last Presidential
elections). As such, the PBC on the percentage of public entrepreneurship funding done through GEA is no longer relevant
and is being marked for deletion. Also, as there is less need for rationalization, the overall budget of this sub-component
has reduced from US$7 million to US$6 million.

19.    Limited Partnership Act as PBC for VCTF (target: 2024; budget: PBC US$1m). As part of the restructuring it has
been agreed that a new PBC for US$1 million for the drafting and approval of the new Limited Partnership Act.

    (b) Sub-component 3.2: High-Growth SME and Entrepreneurship Ecosystem Strengthening – changes to Component
        costs and results indicators.

20.     Under this sub-component, the SME support program implemented by GEA will be scaled up by an additional
US$20 million (for a total of US$34 million) to include more beneficiaries (youth in particular) and expand support to
COVID19 recovery. GEA is responsible for implementing an SME Growth Program targeting 400 firms through a
combination of technical and financial assistance (total of US$14 million). At the onset of the COVID19 crisis, the World
Bank and GEA worked together to set aside US$5 million for COVID19 recovery which has been disbursed in the form of
grants to nearly 400 SMEs (of which 40 percent are women-led). While Ghanaian firms started recovering from the crisis,
a recent Business Tracker Survey (implemented by the Ghana Statistical Service with support from the World Bank and
the United Nations Development Program) has shown that recovery has been uneven, and most firms are still facing
challenges in accessing inputs. As such, an additional US$5 million will be made available for the COVID19 recovery grants
to reach a total of 600 firms, and US$15 million will be added to the SME Growth Program to make up for the funding gap
from the COVID-19 grants and to double the reach of the program (including to target youth-led businesses in response
to Ghana’s huge youth unemployment challenge).

21.       The results indicators related to this sub-component will be clarified and revised to reflect the full scope of the
support provided. The PDO indicator on growth in firm sales will remain unchanged, but the definition will clarify that this
does not apply to COVID-19 recovery grant beneficiaries (for which support is to help maintain sales and employment
rather than grow sales). The end target for the intermediate indicator on the number of SMEs benefitting from capacity
upgrading support will double (from 400 to 800); as a result, the end target for the number of SMEs receiving financial
support under the SME Growth scheme will also increase (from 150 to 400). Three new intermediate indicators will be
added to reflect COVID19 support and support to entrepreneurship through NEIP (as specified in the Project Appraisal
Document), as follows: (i) the number of SMEs receiving financial support under the COVID19 window (end target of 600);
(ii) the number of entrepreneurship hubs benefiting from capacity building support (end target of 20); and (iii) the number
of new hubs launched (end target of 12).

    (c) Sub-component 3.3: Venture Financing for Early-Stage Businesses and Strategic Industries – changes to results
        indicators and PBCs

22.     A new intermediate indicator will be introduced to reflect support to VCTF under this sub-component, and the
PBC relating to the number of investments completed under the two new funds is dropped. The number of investments
into general partners completed under the Startup Catalyst Fund (SCF) and the Strategic Industries Fund (SIF) is proposed

as a new indicator to reflect progress under the sub-component, with an end target of 10. The two funds have been
recently established following a long wait period before the new Board was appointed, and the first few investments will
be completed by the end of June 2022. Given the funds are established and investments will be made into child funds,
with three already identified, the PBC relating to the number of investments completed is no longer needed.

        F. Changes to cost of Component 4: Project Management and Evaluation

23.     The overall budget for Component 4 will increase from US$6 million currently to US$11 million. To-date,
Component 4 has spent a little over half of its allocated budget. Given the complexity of the project and the many TIAs
involved, project management requires considerably more effort and, therefore, resources. Ten specialists and assistants
have been recruited into the PCU so far. Much new work is needed between the PCU, MOF and the 10 TIAs. With the
addition of a sub-component on trade under Component 1, and given that Component 1 already involves five TIAs, there
is a need to recruit an additional technical specialist in the PCU. There is also a need to ramp up communication efforts
for the project as a whole and to consolidate them at the PCU level. Procurement and contract management capacity
needs to be substantially scaled up, as well as the capacity to track expenditures and manage accounts; therefore,
additional staff covering procurement, contract management, and financial management will be added. As such, the
budget for the component will be increased to improve the project management, coordination, and M&E.

24.      Equally important is the agreement that the Technical Committee will now meet on a near weekly basis with
the 10 TIA focal points that are involved in project procurement, contract management and overall implementation
activities, in close collaboration with MoF and the Project Coordinator at the Project Coordination Unit (PCU). The MoF
has agreed to add a new position - the Deputy Project Coordinator – who will work closely with MoF and the Project
Coordinator, to strengthen the Project Coordinator position, as well as the three existing Technical Component Specialists
(for components 1, 2 and 3) and the 10 TIA focal points. A new Project Administrator position at MoF will support the
project. The two committees (PSC, TC) will continue to be supported by the PCU. As part of this restructuring the Bank
and MoF are finalizing any needed support for both the procurement and financial management functions. It is
noteworthy that there were several unexpected delays after project approval to set up the PCU at MoF, currently
comprised of a Project Coordinator, a Procurement Specialist, a Safeguards Specialist, a Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E)
Specialist, three Technical Component Specialists, Financial Management Specialists (assigned from the Controller General
and Audit Department at MoF), and a few assistants. The previous interim Project Coordinator is still involved as Project
Administrator, and the Director of the (Multilateral) External Resource Mobilization Division at MoF is the GETP Project
Director and Bank team’s focal point.

        G. Changes to Implementing Agency

25.     The TIAs have been revised to reflect recent changes within the government of Ghana. As mentioned, GhaNAS
has been introduced as a new TIA for sub-component 1.3, adding to the existing TIAs which fall under MOTI (GSA, GFZA,
and GEA). A new focal point within MOTI will be assigned to work with the PCU on implementation of the new sub-
component on trade. Following the 2020 Presidential elections, the Ministry of Business Development was dissolved, and
NEIP, previously under that Ministry, moved to the Office of the President. As such, NEIP has now replaced the Ministry
of Business Development as a TIA.

        H. Changes to Legal Covenants

26.     The legal covenant pertaining to the recruitment of an Independent Verification Agent (IVA) has been revised.
In January 2021 the Bank agreed, on a temporary basis, that MOF would act as the IVA. With this restructuring, however,
the Bank is requiring that an independent (non-MOF) verification agent be put in place no later than October 31, 2022.

totaling US$6m were made. The new IVA will be for all project PBCs except the single PBC (the GSA Bill) which will be met
in early FY23.
