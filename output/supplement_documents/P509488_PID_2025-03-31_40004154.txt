 B. Introduction and Context

 Country Context

1. Central Asia, situated at the historic crossroads of the Silk Route, is on the brink of harnessing its plentiful natural,
human, and cultural resources to fuel sustainable economic growth. With a total population exceeding 70 million, the
aggregate regional GDP stands at USD$329 billion, comparable to economies like Vietnam and Romania. Prior to the
pandemic, the Central Asia (Kazakhstan, Kyrgyz Republic, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan) economy grew on
average by nearly 9 percent a year during 2001-2010 and around 4.5 percent during 2011-2019. Fossil fuels played an
important role in growth in several countries (Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan) as did remittances (Tajikistan, Kyrgyz
Republic, Uzbekistan). However, several countries in the region are accelerating economic and social reform programs,
which offers the prospect of more accelerated, diversified and sustainable growth in the coming years.

2. Growth in Central Asia is projected to reach 3.7 percent in 2022 and 3.9 percent in 2023 — higher than earlier
projections — as higher global commodity prices and strong post-COVID recoveries have helped to buoy economic
activity. Recently concluded Country Climate Development Reports (CCDR) for Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan find that, to
sustain economic growth over the longer term, Central Asian countries face the difficult challenge of reducing their heavy

reliance on fossil fuels, whose prospects for productivity-led growth are low and whose demand is expected to be lower
over time. Accordingly, reforms that strengthen market principles, build integration, and support economic diversification
will be critical. Regional integration can play a catalyst role for the region’s countries to increase participation in regional
and global value chains, offering opportunities for economic transformation.

3. After decades of fragmentation, Central Asian leaders are beginning to forge a regional mindset. Significant political
and geo-economic developments prompted the new approach. New leadership in Uzbekistan led to an historic summit by
Central Asian presidents in March 2018. In June 2018, following their first summit, the presidents submitted a resolution
to the United Nations General Assembly, calling on the international community to support greater cooperation in the
region. Passage of the resolution signifies UN recognition of Central Asia as a subject rather than an object of competing
foreign powers.1 Concerted efforts at economic modernization and regional integration are now imperative to revitalize
and enhance sustainable growth in Central Asia.

    Sectoral and Institutional Context

4. Improvement in the last six years in the geopolitical relations in Central Asia has increased opportunities for
regional energy connectivity and trade. The relaxation of regional tensions following new leadership and reforms in
Uzbekistan is leading to establishing bilateral accords on trade, energy, transport, and investments in the region.
Uzbekistan has resumed its gas supply to Tajikistan and expressed interest in joint development of hydropower in
Tajikistan and Kyrgyz Republic. Kazakhstan has also expressed support in developing of large hydro facilities in the Kyrgyz
Republic to improve the integrated water-energy management in the region. Tajikistan is finalizing resynchronization of
its energy system with Central Asia Power System (CAPS). Turkmenistan is currently the main supplier of energy resources
in the region, especially during winter seasons. Tajikistan electricity exports to Uzbekistan has also resumed in 2018 after
a pause of nine years. Increased interest in electricity trade within Central Asia has made energy connectivity and trade
an important regional agenda for Central Asian countries.

5. With demand expected to grow by at around 40 percent by 2030, and triple by 2050 if carbon neutrality targets
are to be met, substantial scale-up of generation capacity is needed for the regional to cater to its growing needs while
supporting a clean energy transition. CCDRs in the region have shown that the region’s set of complementary energy
sources can help in meeting growing energy demand with a lower cost, accelerating the decarbonization path and realizing
full benefits of regional energy cooperation. Despite a significant regional potential and becoming economically
competitive, the use of renewable energy in Central Asia remains a small fraction of the potential and limited to
hydropower – the share of non-hydro renewable energy in power generation accounted for around 2% in 2021. Scaling-
up trade in Central Asia could support meeting this growing demand and bring regional optimization of renewable energy
development, creating significant economies of scale to attract international financiers. Development of flagship regional
projects such as Rogun Hydropower Project (HPP) in Tajikistan, Kambarata-1 HPP in Kyrgyz Republic as well as large solar
and wind parks in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan will benefit from significant scale-up of electricity exchanges and
strengthening of regional interconnections within the region.

6.     The potential of regional interconnectivity and trade remains however largely unexploited in the region.
Although Central Asia electricity transmission systems are well interconnected, the exchange of power among countries
has been limited to much less than available interconnection capacity. Average utilization of the cross-border capacity has
been only approximately at 2.5 percent of regional demand and about 40 percent of interconnection capacity. While there

7.       There is a common understanding among Central Asian leaders of the need to address such barriers to put the
sectors on financially sustainable path and create an enabling environment for electricity trade and clean energy
transition, but those actions should be accelerated. While the strategies of each country were endogenously chosen in
response to their national sector conditions, Central Asia is seeing significant structural and regulatory reforms in the
energy sector. These strategies included different approaches to enhancing sector governance and transparency,
strengthening the planning framework, and expanding the sector on a systematic least-cost basis. Accelerating such
reforms is a key ingredient to ensure build trust in trade and ripe the benefits of closer regional cooperation.

8.      The proposed Program supports the development and evolution of a fully-fledged regional electricity market in
Central Asia. Regional markets across the world can take different shapes and forms. Multiple market designs could be
implemented. The first Phase of this Program aims to finance the development of a proof-of-concept of a simplified
regional market platform in Central Asia. It will also finance critical infrastructure and digitalization investments needed
to improve the technical and operational performance of the regional grid to get them to a market-ready stage. The project
would also help establish and operationalize regional institutions as well as enhance the capacity of existing regional and
national agencies to be involved in the proposed pilot regional market.

    Relationship to CPF

9.      The proposed Program is consistent with the World Bank Regional Engagement Framework for Central Asia
(REFCA) and Country Partnership Frameworks (CPFs) in the region. The REFCA gives the highest priority to programs that
improve connectivity and sustainability of regional public goods, both of which will be supported by the project. The REFCA
further emphasizes the importance of regional connectivity and trade, and development of potential hydro and renewable
energy projects. Accordingly, REMIT will contribute the Pillar 1 “Removing Obstacles to Trade and Connectivity�? and Pillar
5 “Addressing Climate Change and Environmental Sustainability�? of REFCA. Similarly, the Program is aligned with CPFs in
Kazakhstan (Objective #3, CPF FY20-FY25),2 Uzbekistan (Objectives #1.4 and 3.1, CPF FY22-FY26),3 Kyrgyz Republic
(Objective 2.2 and 2.3, CPF FY24-FY28),4 and Tajikistan (Objectives #4 and #7, CPF FY19-FY23).5

 D. Concept Description

11.      The proposed Project (Phase 1) is framed along the following three components: (i) Component 1: Pilot regional
electricity market; (ii) Component 2: Reinforcing interconnections and system resilience; and (iii) Component 3:
Strengthening the enabling environment and institutional capacity.

12.     Component 1: Pilot regional electricity market. This component aims to support the implementation of a Pilot
Short-term Market platform to promote regional electricity trade in Central Asia. The proposed pilot aims to have strong
demonstrational effect on how a regional market could work in Central Asia, ensuring the satisfaction of the growing
energy demand in the region through efficient deployment of lower-cost energy, enhancing security and reliability of
supply, and thereby also spurring the economic growth and green transition in the region. The pilot market can be
established within a short timeframe (6-12 months) and may have an initial operational period of 2 years. The team
expects that different designs could be piloted during the implementation timeframe of the project. The pilot will allow
the integration of new market participants/countries over time, as well as future coupling with different markets.

13.     Component 2: Reinforcement and digitalization of regional interconnections. REMIT MPA will support the
development of regional market with soft and hard infrastructure. This component will finance critical investments to
enhance regional electricity trade both at the regional and national levels. In parallel, based on proposals from countries
and the countries’ priority investment programs, a potential list of critical investments required to strengthen the regional
systems has also been identified. The investments will be selected based on the following key criteria: (i) contribution to
the PrDO and enhanced energy trade in the region; (ii) technical and economic justification in terms of urgency and
potential impact, and (iii) readiness for implementation, including environmental and social aspects.

14.     Component 3: Strengthening institutional framework and enabling environment. As effective cross-border trade
depends on reliable power sectors at domestic level, this component will support activities aimed at: (i) strengthening the
financial viability and governance of national power sectors (e.g. tariff reform); (ii) reinforcing regulatory authorities –
through training, capacity building, specific activities. This component will also support project supervision, management
and coordination.
