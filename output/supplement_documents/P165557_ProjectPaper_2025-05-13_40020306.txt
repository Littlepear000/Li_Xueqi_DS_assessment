     I.    BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR Restructuring with ADDITIONAL FINANCING
A.    Introduction

1.    This project paper seeks the approval of the Western and Central Africa Regional Vice President to provide
Additional Financing (AF) from the Global Partnership for Education (GPE) as a grant in the amount of US$109.73
million1 and from the Early Learning Partnership Multi-Donor Trust Fund (ELP-MDTF)2 in the amount of US$7.40 million
for the Ghana Accountability for Learning Outcomes Project (GALOP: P165557). The total grant amount of the AF is
US$117.13 million.

2.    The proposed second additional financing (AF2)3 to GALOP will enable the scale-up of successful interventions from
the parent project incorporating lessons learned and the introduction of two activities which would impact the
improvement in learning outcomes namely; the expansion of pre-schooling (including access to childcare) and working
with communities to decrease dropouts in vulnerable regions/districts through Communities of Excellence (CoEx). In
parallel, this will entail: (i) restructuring to extend the project closing dates for the parent GALOP (comprising IDA 64820-
GH and GPE TF0B0846) from December 31, 2024 to December 31, 2025, and AF1 comprising Global Partnership for Results
Based Approaches (GPRBA) TF0B3026 from December 31, 2025 to December 31, 2026; (ii) modifying critical activities from
the parent project, which were not originally Disbursement Linked Indicators (DLIs), into Performance-based Conditions
(PBCs); and (iii) revising the results framework to align with the changes of the proposed AF. The AF2 is proposed to close
on December 29, 2028. Details of the proposed changes are presented in Section II of the project paper.

B.    Country Context

3.     Ghana experienced steady economic growth averaging 3 percent annually over the past two decades, nearly
doubling its Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita. This growth positioned Ghana among the top ten fastest-growing
economies in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and elevated it to lower middle-income status (LMIS) in 2011. However, in recent
years, Ghana's economy has faced cycles of fiscal pressures and heightened vulnerability, exacerbated by pre-existing
macroeconomic imbalances, external shocks, and policy slippages. In 2022, following the Coronavirus Disease 2019
(COVID-19) pandemic, Ghana experienced a macroeconomic crisis characterized by a surge in public debt, loss of access
to international financial markets, declining international reserves, and rising inflation. Inflation soared to over 50 percent,
the balance of payments came under acute pressure, and GDP growth fell from 5.4 percent in 2021 to 2.9 percent in 2023.
The economic crisis disproportionately affected the vulnerable segments of the population. Consequently, it is estimated
that poverty increased by 1.5 percentage points in 2024.4 Despite these setbacks, Ghana’s economy has demonstrated

4.    Ghana’s human capital development outcomes are below its peer income grouping, but above the average for SSA.

harmonized test scores (HTS) which limits the effectiveness of the budget allocations to the sector. In 2023, the
percentage of GDP to the education sector was 3.5 compared to the United Nations (UN) benchmark of 4-6 percent5.

C.     Sectoral Context

5.    Ghana’s education system comprises a 2-6-3-3-4 structure for kindergarten, primary, lower secondary, higher
general/technical/vocation secondary, and tertiary levels, as well as complementary education or non-formal
education. Ghana has made strong political commitments to improve children’s development, including a multisectoral
Early Childhood Care and Development (ECCD) Policy launched in 2004 which covers ages 0 – 8 years.6 The Ministry of
Education (MoE) is guided by its Education Strategic Plan (ESP 2018-2030) which provides medium- and long-term policy
guidance for the sector. The Ghana Education Service (GES) implements policy at pre-tertiary education through its 16
regional and 261 district education offices across Ghana.

6.     Basic7 and higher secondary public education is free for all beneficiaries, while public tertiary education is tuition
free with academic user fees. In 2008, the government established the National Teaching Council (NTC), the National
Schools Inspectorate Authority (NaSIA) and the National Council for Curriculum and Assessment (NaCCA) to deepen
education sector service delivery. The NTC is mandated to license and register teachers, regulate teacher training
institutions, oversee continuing teacher professional development, and ensure compliance with teaching standards. The
NaSIA is responsible for providing independent external evaluation of quality and standards in both public and private
basic and higher secondary schools, while NaCCA has overall responsibility for curriculum development and assessment.
Other relevant agencies under the MoE are the Center for Distance Learning and Open Schooling (CENDLOS), the
Complementary Education Agency (CEA), the National Service Scheme (NSS) and the National Education Leadership
Institute (NELI).

7.    Ghana has made significant progress on improving access to education in the decade between 2000 to 2022. The
gross enrollment rate (GER) has improved significantly at the kindergarten and primary levels from 60 percent to 122
percent and from 83 percent to 107 percent, respectively. The government's flagship Free Secondary Education Policy
rolled out in 2017, is associated with a 28 percent increase in the number of students enrolled at higher secondary level
between 2017 and 2023. Gross secondary enrolment increased from 55.9 percent in 2017 to 60.7 percent in 2023. Ghana
has surpassed its targets for gender parity index (GPI) at lower secondary (1.02) and higher secondary (1.05) levels and is
close to reaching parity at the kindergarten (0.99) and primary levels (0.99).8

Key challenges:
8.    Despite improvements in access over the past two decades, learning outcomes are below proficiency levels with
wide disparities across regions. For children ages 3 to 5, only 43 percent are developmentally on track in literacy and
numeracy (Lim et al., 2023). Only about half of assessed learners in Primary 4 meet the minimum competency benchmarks
(51.3 percent for English and 49 percent for mathematics) according to the National Standardized Test (NST) 2022.

9.     There is evidence that improved teaching approaches introduced under the parent GALOP are beginning to show
improvements in learning progression. Differentiated Learning (DL) in 501 schools with 60,000 learners monitored under
a government pilot (with United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) support) indicates signs of positive learning progression
from one level to the next. The DL approach involves grouping learners by ability levels and working with the various
groups to accelerate literacy and numeracy. 9 In two trimesters, 17 to 25 percent of learners progressed in English
compared to 21 to 27 percent in Mathematics. It is important to note that extending the pilot and tracking the learning
levels of learners through a digital platform, provides much needed information to teachers, school leaders and education
managers on learner progression. Scaling this up will provide the necessary feedback to improve relevant in-service
training (INSET), coaching, and mentoring of teachers in kindergarten and primary schools.

10. There are inefficiencies in the deployment of teachers to basic schools. There is significant district-level and rural-
urban variance in pupil trained teacher ratios (PTTR) with districts mostly in the rural north of Ghana experiencing a
shortage of trained teachers, while districts in the south and urban areas of Ghana have a surplus of teachers (World Bank,
2021). Teacher deployment to remote areas has been challenging, leaving some classrooms in these areas without
teachers. A United States Agency for International Development (USAID, 2018) report10 indicates high teacher turnover,
with 12 percent of teachers transferring to a different school each year.

11. Education expenditure as a percentage of total government expenditure has decreased in recent years, with
spending skewed towards higher secondary and tertiary education. In 2024, the projection of education sector share of
government recurrent budget is 14 percent compared to the international benchmark of 15 to 20 percent. With basic
education receiving about 20 percent of the government education budget in 2023, Ghana’s spending on basic education
is relatively lower than its structural and aspirational peers11. Further, the proportion of total spending on goods and
services allocated to basic education decreased from 10 percent in 2015 to 4 percent in 2021, compared to the share
allocated to secondary education which increased from 43 percent in 2015 to 57 percent in 2021 following the
introduction of the free secondary education policy. Beyond the need to increase the allocations to basic education, there
is the need to ensure full execution of the education non-salary budget. The low execution of non-salary budget has
affected funding to decentralized levels (schools, districts, and regions).

additional efforts are required for gender parity across regions. According to the Education Management Information

improved monitoring of scaled up mobile School Report Card (mSRC) data collected from all schools and utilized by all

D.    Government’s Actions to Address Education Sector Challenges

15. The government’s response to the education sector challenges has been guided by the government’s strategic

Ghana’s access to quality ECE and the learning crises is demonstrated through the implementation of several reforms

strengthen teachers’ capacity, and the NaSIA to strengthen independent periodic external evaluation of quality and
standards in both public and private schools also demonstrate this commitment. Further, the launch of GALOP aimed at
addressing challenges of learning poverty at the school and community levels, is directly aligned to the overarching goal
of the ESP 2018–2030 is to improve the quality of education for all.

in monitoring delivery is essential to get a complete picture of implementation effectiveness.

ENVIRONMENTAL & SOCIAL

Overview
Operation Location
Operation location and salient physical characteristics relevant to the ESS Assessment (geographic, environmental,
social) (if known)
The proposed second AF will be implemented nationwide and will target an additional 6,000 schools throughout the
country. This would allow the project to target all public Kindergarten and primary schools in the country. Beneficiary
schools are generally located within communities, outside of sensitive environments with no Key Biodiversity Areas
(KBAs) within the fringes. The proposed project interventions are therefore not expected to have adverse impacts on
sensitive environments. However, Ghana is vulnerable to climate risks and weather-related disasters such as extreme
temperatures, extreme precipitation, flood, drought etc.
Detailed operation location(s) and salient physical characteristics relevant to the ESS Assessment (geographic,
environmental, social)
Just like the parent project and first AF, the proposed second AF will be implemented nationwide and will target
additional 6,000 basic schools throughout the country. Beneficiary basic schools are generally located within
communities, outside of sensitive environments with no Key Biodiversity Areas (KBAs) within the fringes. The proposed
project interventions are therefore not expected to have adverse impacts on sensitive environments. However, Ghana
is vulnerable to climate risks and weather-related disasters such as extreme temperatures, extreme precipitation,
flood, drought etc. These events could wreak havoc on school facilities, disrupt school sessions, and contribute to
absenteeism. Ghana still has disparities in access to education among girls, boys, and people with disabilities. Key
policies approved to address the disparities include the Inclusive Education Policy (2015), the Early Childhood Education
Policy (2020), the draft Gender in Education policy (2022), and the ongoing Education Sector Plan (ESP, 2018-2030)
which includes key targets for addressing geographic and gender disparities. Regional and district level disparities are
prevalent, for example, the Northern, Ahafo, North-East, Savannah, Oti, and Western-North regions, have Gender

Parity Index (GPI) of 0.90 at the junior secondary level which require extra attention to girls completing basic education
in these regions. Social inclusion remains a challenge in Ghana’s education system, and additional efforts are required
to enroll and retain children, especially those from poor backgrounds, children with disabilities, and adolescent boys
and girls. There are regional variations in Out-of-School- Children (OOSC) with boys (55 percent) out of school than girls
(45 percent) at primary and lower secondary levels. Children from low-income households are more likely to drop out
of school. Despite progress made in expanding access to education for OOSC, there remains much to be done.
Borrower’s Institutional Capacity
The implementation of the parent project and the first AF is mainstreamed in the Ministry of Education (MoE) and its
agencies. The MoE utilizes the secretariats of GALOP and Ghana Education Outcome Project (GEOP) to support a
Project Technical Team (PTT) which coordinates the day-to-day activities of the project. The PTT comprises technical
representatives from Ghana Education Service – GES; National Schools Inspectorate Authority – NaSIA; National Council
for Curriculum Assessment – NaCCA; National Teaching Council – NTC; Centre for Distance Learning and Open
Schooling – CENDLOS; and Complementary Education Agency – CEA). The existing implementation arrangements will
be maintained for the second AF, however, due to the expanded scope of project activities, the technical
representatives of the PTT will be expanded to include new members from the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social
Protection - MoGCSP; Ministry of Local Government, Decentralization, and Rural Development - MoLGDRD; National
Service Secretariat – NSS; and Ghana Health Service - GHS. The project currently has a qualified Environmental
Specialist and a Social Development Specialist, both on secondment from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) of
Ghana. The Environmental Specialist has demonstrated adequate capacity and competence and will be maintained on
fulltime basis to continuously oversee environmental risks and impacts management on the Project. Given the
expanded scope on social issues because of the additional financing, the Borrower will recruit a dedicated Social
Development and Gender Specialist to support social risks and impacts management including gender, disability,
inclusion, SEA/SH prevention. The E&S performance of the Borrower on the parent project and first AF has fluctuated
between Moderately Satisfactory and Satisfactory. Key gaps include delays in operationalizing the Grievance
Mechanism (GM), action plan for Prevention Against Abuse and Sexual Exploitation (PAASE), and delay in completing
the E&S Audit. These gaps have been addressed as the GM has been developed and is operational, the E&S audit has
been conducted, and the MoE has committed funding to implementing the PAASE with technical support from the
Social Development and Gender Specialist to be recruited three months after project effective date to work full-time on
the project. The Bank, under the parent project and first AF, has strengthened the capacity of the Borrower through
training of relevant staff in the World Bank Environmental and Social Framework (ESF), SEA/SH prevention and
mitigation, disability inclusion, child labor, among others. The Bank will continuously support capacity strengthening
under this second AF.

Summary of Assessment of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
A.1 Environmental Risk Rating of Moderate is maintained for the project. However, specifically for the Second
Additional Financing, the environmental risk rating is Low. The classification has considered the nature and magnitude
of the AF 2 activities as they will remain localized, mainly direct, easily identifiable and easily mitigated. No civil work
is expected under this second AF and all the minor civil works under the parent project and first additional financing
have been completed. The potential environmental risks are minimum generation of domestic waste during
workshops and trainings, and inadequate management of end-of-use electronic equipment such as laptops and
tablets supplied by the project for distance learning, training, data collection, report writing, and input into the
Education Management and Information System (EMIS). The Borrower, under the parent project, has prepared an
electronic waste (e-waste) management plan which will be used to guide e-waste management under this AF. The
Borrower has demonstrated adequate capacity with satisfactory management of these risks which were also
anticipated under the parent project. A.2 Social Risk Rating is Moderate The social risk rating remains moderate, same
as the parent project. The AF Project interventions are expected to be limited to existing school compounds and
training centers. None of the proposed activities will lead to permanent or temporary resettlement or livelihood
losses. The toll-free numbers established for the Grievance Mechanism (GM) under the parent project remains active
and will be used for the AF. The MoE and GES have intensified efforts towards better communication, training, and
sensitization on the GM among relevant stakeholders. The project has also considered several opportunities within
the project scope to address school related GBV and disability inclusion. The AF design includes mitigation measures
to address risks of exclusion in basic education with a special focus on gender and disability. The Sub-component 2.2
has been introduced to improve identification of and support to learners with disabilities at the District Level through
health and disability screening. An Action Plan on Prevention Against Abuse and Sexual Exploitation (PAASE) in
beneficiary schools developed under the parent project remains relevant for the AF. The project is taking steps to
intensify communication on the PAASE among relevant stakeholders to facilitate implementation. Due to the

                                           Annex 4: Details on Lessons Learned

1.       Resourcing districts for monitoring implementation of learning interventions is critical and needs to be
incentivized through the project design. Even though the parent GALOP was structured to provide financial
resources to the districts for monitoring and supervision, this happened very late in implementation and
resources were inadequate. District Directors and officials (particularly the School Improvement Support Officers
- SISOs) highlighted the unavailability of resources for their monitoring as a major constraint in effective project
oversight. District Directors could have played a stronger role and ensured that all schools were on-track with
the implementation of the DL and project activities and were accountable for driving learning progress in their
beneficiary schools. The AF has incorporated this lesson into its design by allocating significant resources to go
directly into district monitoring and oversight. A PBC on performance-based district grants is proposed to
incentivize the funds flow to districts.

2.       Ensuring adequate accountability measures are in place at the district level to prioritize learning in
schools is critical. Now that the NST has been implemented across the country, and schools are collecting
information on DL assessments of students, there is the potential to utilize these data to set learning targets for
districts. This would allow District Directors to regularly track the performance of schools in their districts and
identify schools in need of additional support. The AF has incorporated this into its design by ensuring that
district directors will be directly held accountable for their district performance targets (which would include
learning targets and targets on coaching and mentoring of teachers etc.) outlined in their ADEOPs.

3.       Delays in the distribution of TLRs limit the effectiveness of activities aimed at improving teaching
practices. For the DL teaching approach to be successful, teachers and learners must have access to a complete
set of TLRs. The procurement of TLRs and its distribution in the parent project was delayed, as it was not as high
a priority as compared to activities linked to PBCs. The AF has incorporated this lesson by (i) articulating the full
set of TLRs to be made available for kindergarten and primary classes, and (ii) integrating TLRs into PBCs related
to DL and kindergarten scale up.

4.       While online training of teachers can be a useful modality in times of restricted mobility (such as
pandemics) and for delivering refresher training, delivery of INSET with a strong modeling aspect is critical. The
delivery of INSET for the parent GALOP in early years involved both virtual and in-person training sessions. Virtual
training sessions may be considered economical, and useful for delivering refresher training. The feedback from
districts was that in-person trainings cannot be replaced by virtual training due to connectivity issues, and the
fact that virtual training does not have adequate opportunities for modeling concepts. To address this, the AF
has outlined a minimum number of days for which training sessions must be in-person and considerations for
virtual training. The in-person training would have a strong focus on modeling and demonstration by allowing
teachers to observe high-quality teaching in practice. Another challenge relating to ensuring effective teacher
training was the lack of resources to cascade training from the regions and districts to the schools. Processes
were not fully structured to ensure all beneficiary teachers received the required amount of training, consistently
across beneficiary schools and districts. The AF specifies the minimum number of training each teacher must
receive to ensure teachers are well equipped to deliver DL and innovative teaching and learning, and ensures
adequate resources are provided to districts to deliver these trainings. Further, districts will include these training
targets in their ADEOPs, and part of the financing districts receive in subsequent years of the project will be
contingent on achieving these targets.

5.      Using evidence-based criteria to identify and rank schools based on need helps create greater
legitimacy and ownership of the selection process. The parent GALOP relied on criteria-based school selection

to identify and rank the poorest performing public basic schools in the country. This approach was completely
transparent and objective and helped create greater legitimacy amongst stakeholders of the school selection
process. The AF will follow the same approach to select beneficiary districts for accountability grants and this
time more granular data is being used including poverty data on school catchment areas and NST learning
outcome data.

6.      Localized Interventions are essential, particularly in areas where there are service delivery challenges.
Addressing learning can be given an additional boost if the intervention design allows for localized interventions.
The Ministry has developed the CoEx concept and implementation is beginning to yield some positive results
(Bosome Freho district is one such example) as communities are empowered to hold schools accountable to
learning and to support schools in addressing challenges affecting learning. The AF will be supporting the scale-
up of the CoEx interventions in targeted communities.

7.      Ensuring greater civil society engagement in monitoring delivery is essential to get a complete picture
of implementation effectiveness. Supporting Civil society groups to play a formal monitoring function is helpful
to improve accountability with project delivery and help address ongoing bottlenecks during implementation.
Previous education projects provided civil society organizations with a formal oversight role. This was however
not considered in the design of the parent GALOP limiting the benefit of extra oversight from civil society groups
who are in the districts and closer to implementation. Civil society groups during the design consultation
workshops highlighted cases where they had wanted to monitor the progress of the parent GALOP at schools but
were limited as the schools didn’t understand their role in implementation. The AF has been designed to provide
support to Civil Society Organizations to monitor activities such as childcare intervention.

                                            Annex 6: Financial Arrangements

Financial

1.      FM arrangements including budgeting, accounting, internal control, financial reporting, and external
auditing for the proceeds of the AF will follow the existing Government of Ghana public FM policies and
regulations as per the PFM Act, Act 2016 (Act 921) and supplemented by the PIM. The PIM will outline the various
roles and responsibilities including internal approval processes, payment vouchers processing, and authorization
limits.

2.      Under this AF, there will be no major changes in the institutional arrangements for FM arrangements.

3.       Ministry of Education (MoE) – At the MoE, the Director of Finance is responsible for the fiduciary
oversight of the project. However, this function has been delegated to a staff of the rank of Chief Accountant
‘Project Accountant�? with requisite technical skills and experience and assisted by a team of three other schedule
officers who are responsible for the daily transactions processing, recording and financial reporting and supervised
by the Director. The Project Accountant and other staff in the Unit have been involved in implementing earlier
IDA funded projects and understand IDA FM & Disbursement guidelines.

4.       Ghana Education Service (GES) - The Financial Controller with the assistance of the Principal Accountant
will have oversight responsibilities with regards to ensuring compliance with financial covenants such as
submitting unaudited Interim Financial Reports (IFRs), maintaining internal controls over project expenditure,
and engaging external auditors. Under the direct supervision of the Financial Controller, the Principal Accountant
will also be responsible for maintaining and operating the project’s designated account (DA) and make payments
to contractors and service providers, transferring funds to districts and schools (based on Annual Program of
Work) and verifying and authorizing payments. Likewise, the daily transaction processing has been delegated to
a Project Accountant, who is a staff of GES and has worked on other IDA funded projects and part of the current
GALOP implementation.

Compliance to Financial Covenants

5.       In terms of compliance with the financial covenants of submitting acceptable financial reports including
audits, the project, has since inception in December 2020 complied fully with the covenants as stated in the
Financing Agreement. Regarding the submission of periodic financial reports, the project has complied fully in
their submissions of acceptable IFRs, albeit occasional minor delays in meeting the exact deadline date.
Throughout implementation, the quality (content, format, and depth of information) of the IFRs has been
satisfactory and provides relevant information to help management in decision making on the use of funds and
the rate of utilization.

6.       With regards to the submission of audit reports, the project has satisfactorily complied with the
submission of acceptable audit reports and management letters. The most recent audit report was for the year
ending December 2023 and this was submitted on 28th June 2024 and compliant with the audit submission
deadline date of not later than six months after the year end. A review of the audit report and the management
letter indicates the auditor issued an unqualified opinion and that there were no material or significant lapses or
weaknesses in the internal control environment which could adversely have affected the integrity of the financial
statements. Presently there are no outstanding IFRs or audits due under the project.

Summary of key FM arrangements as assessed is as follows:

Budgeting

7.       The MoE and the GES as government agencies and departments follow and adopt the budget
preparation procedures and the annual budget guidelines issued by the MoF as outlined per the Public Financial
Management Act (921) 2016. Specifically, for this project, the budget will be derived from the component
allocations which will serve as the basis for preparing the respective Annual Work Plans (AWPs) and Procurement
Plans. The project specific budgeting process and other requirements will be documented in a revised IDA
approved PIM.

8.       The Director of Budgets (MoE), working in collaboration with the Director of Finance (MoE), the
Financial Controller (GES), the Project Coordinator, and other stakeholders are responsible for initiating the
budgeting process for the project and obtaining inputs (activities, schedules, timelines, and cost) from the
beneficiary agencies. Individual regional, district, and school, budgets, work plans, and procurement plans will
be collated and consolidated at the headquarters and reviewed jointly by key project staff. MoE HQ will coordinate
yearly work plans and budgets and share consolidated copies with stakeholders for review. Once the draft budget
is completed, it will be approved by the Chief Director for onward submission to the World Bank for review and
clearance i.e., ‘No Objection’. Our assessment of the current budgetary control processes and procedures used
mostly for the government’s discretionary budget are capable of monitoring commitments and outstanding
balances, and this helps reduce the risk of multiple payments.

9.     The assessment indicated that the government’s budgeting processes are satisfactory and can be relied
upon to reflect the various components to be implemented.

Accounting

10.      The Heads of Accounts of the respective implementing agencies shall have overall fiduciary
responsibility for all FM aspects of the project, whilst the routine daily transactional processing and reporting will
be assigned to a fully dedicated ‘Project Accountants’ who are staff of the Controller and Accountant General’s
Department (CAGD) of a minimum rank of Chief Accountant. Our assessment indicates that these Project
Accountant has the requisite technical skills and competence capable of managing the project funds. The
responsibility of the Project Accountants is to ensure that throughout implementation there are adequate FM
systems in place which can report adequately on the use of project funds and ensure that project funds are used
for the purposes intended and in line with the PDO.

11.     In addition, and under the supervisory role of the Director of Finance, the Project Accountants will be
tasked with maintaining oversight responsibilities with regards to ensuring compliance with financial covenants
such as submitting acceptable unaudited IFRs, maintaining internal controls over project expenditure, and
engaging external auditors and ensuring compliance to submission of acceptable audit reports. On behalf of the
Chief Director, the Project Accountants will also be responsible for maintaining and operating the project’s DA
and support the processing of payments to contractors and service providers for activities under this project.

12.     Specifically, and in terms of systems for accounting and financial reporting, the AF will use the existing
Government of Ghana accounting and reporting processes and guidelines as provided by the MoF. The
automated Ghana Integrated Financial Management Information System (GIFMIS) using Oracle Financials is the
government’s default accounting system for managing all government funding. As such, the accounting and
reporting on the uses of the project’s funds will be maintained using the GIFMIS platform. Currently, the MoE has

Disbursement arrangements and use of funds

20.    The use of grants proceeds will be based on a hybrid of traditional IPF and IPF with PBCs for all
components. These PBCs are considered significant indicators of performance that will influence behavioral, and
reforms required for achieving visible outcomes in the education sector and the PDO.

21.      Since this is an IPF operation, the underlying principle will be to disburse, after project effectiveness an
initial advance. The initial advance shall be based on a forecast of the funding required to potentially achieve the
set of PBCs in each year, as per the approved Annual Work Plan and Budget. Subsequently, on a half-yearly basis,
the Borrower will provide satisfactory documentary evidence including (i) acceptable IFR and (iii) evidence from
an independent verification agent on the satisfactory achievement of a set of PBCs. These reports will then form
the basis for documenting the advances made into the DA. Further advances will be made on the approval of the
net cash requirements (next six months forecast of expenditure) as reviewed and approved by the World Bank.
Thus, the satisfactory achieving of the defined PBCs as identified in the Financial Agreement will constitute the
primary basis for triggering grant disbursements under the project.

22.      The World Bank will reimburse expenditures according to the assigned values corresponding to the
PBCs achieved during each verification cycle. If the total amount of incurred eligible expenditures at the end of
the cycle is less than the value assigned to the PBCs achieved, the World Bank will reimburse only the amount
of the incurred eligible expenditures. It is important to note that PBCs serve as performance benchmarks rather
than traditional disbursement conditions. Where achievement of a PBC cannot be satisfactorily certified, an
amount equivalent to the PBC value will be withheld or considered as undocumented and an outstanding
obligation on the project. This amount will be documented at any later date, during project life, and at the
discretion of the World Bank’s task team when such an achievement is verified. The task team may consider that
a later achievement of the PBC would not qualify for disbursement against the unmet PBC if it determines that
the timeliness and schedule for achievement of the PBC is critically fundamental to achieving the overall objectives
of the project.

23.      The IFRs will serve as the basis for requesting advances and for documentation. If ineligible expenditures
are found to have been made from the DA, the Recipient will be obligated to refund them, and IDA will have the
right to suspend disbursement of the funds if reporting requirements are not complied with as provided for in the
FA. The World Bank’s FM team will periodically assess the adequacy of FM systems, and this will form the basis of
any change in disbursement methods.

24.      A schedule of the PBC matrix with respective values is included in the Financing Agreement. Additional
details of the verification protocols and the independent verification process shall be documented in the PIM.

                                       Annex 7. Economic Analysis
1.      Cost and Benefit Analysis (CBA) aims to assess the economic justification of the investment by
quantifying the economic gains of the GALOP project interventions including the new activities supported by
the AF. The gains have been calculated in terms of the future earnings of the beneficiary students. The economic
return of the project has been assessed by estimating (a) the NPV which is calculated by deducting costs from
the expected future benefits, and (b) the IRR that equalizes the benefits and costs of the project investments.
2.        The project is designed to have tangible development impacts with a high IRR, currently estimated at
about 26 percent. The project is expected to increase the number of learners in primary school, increase
retention, school completion, and provide the required competencies in literacy and numeracy for improved
work productivity. There are also significant social returns to school, especially for girls,50 that far exceed the
costs of schooling and that result in higher returns for their families, society, and the economy. Improvements in
the quality of teaching and learning and better management of the school system will improve education delivery
and the effectiveness of teachers and will benefit current and future cohorts of learners. Effective deployment
of trained teachers to areas where they are mostly needed will not only improve access to quality education to
all children, especially the disadvantaged, but will also ensure efficient utilization of available teaching resources
given the significantly high proportion of the budget dedicated to teachers’ remuneration at the basic education
level. The additional focus on learners with disabilities will improve equity outcomes which have not been
quantified here.

3.      The CBA has been conducted based on the following assumptions: (a) An increase in the percentage of
students that complete Primary 6 and Primary 9 because of the intervention; (b) efficiency gains in public and
private education spending as a result of improved completion and efficiency of teachers; (c) quality benefits
measured by improved wages for beneficiaries using the household expenditure data. The additional wage for
primary completers, based on the household data, is about 10 percent above the wage for non-primary
graduates. The wage premium, to be derived from better education, for beneficiaries is assumed to be 3 percent
at the beginning, increasing to 5 percent; (d) wage benefits are calculated for 20 years, and they only start after
5 years of project implementation; (e) project costs are incurred only during the project period; and (g) all costs
and benefits accrued are discounted at a 10 percent annual rate. The CBA estimates the NPV of economic benefits
as presented in Table A2 below.
