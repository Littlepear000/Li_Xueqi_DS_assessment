    B. Introduction and Context

 Country Context
Somalia has made significant progress in recent years by consolidating its federal system of governance, strengthening
government institutions, and supporting inclusive private sector-led growth. The country reached the Heavily Indebted
Poor Countries (HIPC) Completion Point on December 13, 2023, securing substantial debt relief that reduced Somalia’s
external debt to 6 percent of the gross domestic product (GDP). Despite this progress, Somalia continues to face significant
governance, security, and development challenges that hinder state performance and slow transition out of Fragility,
Conflict, and Violence (FCV). While there has been incremental gains in financing and institutional capacity to deliver basic
services, overall service delivery remains limited, particularly in conflict-affected areas. Decades of instability have
constrained investments in physical and human capital, preventing many Somalis from reaching their full potential.
Climate change pressures are expected to intensify, with more frequent and erratic droughts and floods.1

Job creation in Somalia has been constrained by recurrent climate and conflict-related shocks, alongside low labor force
participation. Overall vulnerability to shocks has limited economic growth, averaging about 2 percent annually over the
last decade, with particularly adverse effects on employment. Agriculture, employing about 65 percent of the country’s
labor force, is highly vulnerable to climate shocks and insecurity. The 2016-2017 drought caused losses of $3.25 billion,
equivalent to 65 percent of GDP. Together with conflict, climate induced displacement from rural areas has significantly
accelerated urbanization – with urban areas now hosting over 4 million IDPs. With only 28% of the 174 000 urban located
firms classified as formal, the labor market has struggled to absorb the estimated 161,000 new workers entering yearly.

 Sectoral and Institutional Context
Somalia’s economy functions largely as a fragmented set of cities, with unreliable, inefficient, and insecure transport
infrastructure limiting interconnectivity and access to global markets. More than 70 percent of Somalia’s GDP is
generated in urban areas, where agglomeration effects, economic clustering, and relatively improved security offers
opportunities for more inclusive and resilient growth. The road links connecting these urban centers remain
underdeveloped, insecure, and highly vulnerable to climate hazards, limiting the ability to leverage the nodes for sustained
economic expansion. In this context, air transport often serves as the primary, and in some areas the only means of
connectivity, yet it faces safety, security, and climate resilience challenges that raise costs and undermine reliability. The

resulting high transport costs contribute to economic fragmentation.2 Rural producers, particularly in agriculture and
livestock, are further constrained by poor transport and insecurity, which significantly limit their access to urban markets.

The urban nodes themselves struggle to leverage their agglomeration advantage, faced with a set of common
challenges, including poor internal connectivity, deteriorating infrastructure and services, recurrent disasters, and rapid
growth. Rapid urbanization, widespread informality, and settlement on marginal land have driven up the costs of basic
service provision and exacerbated drainage problems. Consequently, recurrent flooding and other disasters continue to
displace hundreds of thousands, creating a vicious cycle. Furthermore, the urban nodes are internally fragmented, limiting
access for workers and job seekers and increasing costs of access. These challenges are compounded by strained municipal
capacities and underdeveloped national and local emergency management systems, which remain heavily reliant on
international support. As a result, cities and towns are hindered from fully functioning as engines of jobs, trade, and
inclusive growth.

Reducing the functional economic distance between these cities through strategic, coordinated and sustained corridor
development can fuel integrated economic regions and unlock critical economies of scale.3 Progressive approaches to
regional transport development corridors4 aim to leverage the contributions of connected catchments, known as “basins
of integration�? by synchronizing multisectoral investments to unlock regional economic development. A study5 on the
potential impact of infrastructure development on the Horn of Africa (HoA) found that coordinated expansion in access
to roads, electricity, and internet, could drive structural change in employment, with an estimated 12 percent increase in
the share of services of employment. The same study also estimated that development of key economic corridors,
combined with trade facilitation measures, could raise real income in Somalia by 6.3 percent. In terms of disaster impacts,
the 2023 floods caused over $29.7 million in transport infrastructure losses and even greater economic disruptions.6

The governance needed to unlock the growth potential of these economic basins of integration remains fragmented,
with responsibilities split across federal and sub-national authorities and limited options for sustainable revenue
generation. In roads, the Ministry of Public Works, Reconstruction, and Housing (MoPWRH) leads national development,
but its mandate overlaps with the Ministry of Transport and Civil Aviation (MoTCA) and Federal Member States (FMS).
City governments have growing responsibilities, including urban road development and emergency response, yet disputes
exist between local, state, and federal levels. For disaster risk management, the Somalia Disaster Management Agency
(SODMA) coordinates policy and response across levels of government. In the aviation, MoTCA oversees the sector, while
the Somali Civil Aviation Authority (SCAA) serves as both regulator and as an Airports Authority. Across all sectors,
operations and maintenance are underfunded, and generated revenues are diverted to general budget needs. For
example, while aviation contributed $34.8 million to domestic revenue in 2024, sector budgets remain insufficient.
Aligning aviation taxes and fees with international standards could potentially triple revenues but will require stronger
fiscal regulation, transparent revenue retention, and improved governance.

The Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for FY24–28 supports Somalia in building a stable and resilient state by
improving basic services, fostering inclusive private sector growth, and enhancing infrastructure. The program
contributes to CPF goals by strengthening sector governance and revenue generation, improving climate-resilient
infrastructure, and expanding connectivity. These efforts promote trade, service delivery, climate resilience, job
creation, and address key growth and trade barriers identified in the Country Private Sector Diagnostic (CPSD).7 A formal
Public-Private Dialogue Platform for the transport sector is under consideration to bring together investors, government
entities, and civil society organizations for structured consultation and pipeline development.8 The proposed program is
Paris-aligned, incorporating adaptation and mitigation measures anchored in key FGS climate and sectoral policies to
enhance infrastructure resilience and logistics efficiency, including the Nationally Determined Contributions, National
Adaptation Plan Framework, National Climate Change Policy, and the NTP. It also responds to key constraints identified
in the Somalia Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) related to climate resilient access and comprehensive
disaster risk management.

    D. Concept Description

The Project Development Objective of MPA Phase 1 (“the project�?) is to improve the safety, climate resilience and
performance of selected infrastructure, and improve the capacity of relevant institutions. This phase will lay the
foundation for subsequent phases through incremental scale-up, creating a national platform for stakeholder
engagement, bundling infrastructure implementation modalities, and ensuring resilient access to facilitate the movement
of people and goods to future project sites, while strengthening urban resilience, ensuring service continuity, and
enhancing emergency preparedness. Activities will be prioritized based on agreed criteria, including criticality in ensuring
safe and resilient connectivity throughout the country, as well as addressing key bottlenecks in the delivery of urban
services in regional hubs, and maximizing sustainable job creation.

Pillar 2 - Institutional Transformation and Capacity Building
To ensure coordinated and efficient infrastructure implementation, this subcomponent will establish mechanisms for
all phases of the MPA while supporting planning for future phases. In particular, it will: i) create a high-level coordination
and technical platforms chaired by the MOF to align phases and resolves, overlaps with sectoral mechanisms (e.g.,
MoPWRH, disaster management agencies, and cities); ii) conduct economic basin level assessments on jobs and growth,
localizing and tailoring national level work underway (i.e., CPSD, CEM, CPRJ, and the Jobs Platform) in covering value
chains, cold chain needs, municipal finance – including performance incentives for local governments, housing and land,
urban planning, risk mapping, and emergency preparedness; and iii) support preparation of any required feasibility or
design studies for future phases. At the FMS level, existing civil servant capacity will be leveraged for quality assurance
with targeted capacity building as needed. The “Reimagining Somali Cities: A Vision for Competitiveness and Job Creation�?,
along with other deliverables under the Somalia Livable Cities PASA, provides an initial foundation.
In addition, it will strengthen institutional and technical capacity in relevant sectors. This includes policy and operational
capacity for air transport, highway, urban, and disaster management stakeholders, with a focus on establishing systems
for training and licensed professionals in key sectors. Building on the success of SURP II,12 it will also take a targeted
approach to skills training in the construction sector. The project will use geospatial analytics, predictive modeling, and
AI-based decision tools where feasible, building on the SURP II MIS in six cities to monitor project and risk data. Embedding
digital technologies in planning improve transparency, speed implementation, and strengthen resilience.

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts



The project is designed as a national program, with Phase 1 establishing implementation structures and critical
connectivity investments across Somalia. Investments span both urban nodes (major cities like Mogadishu, Hargeisa,
Kismayo, Galkayo, Garowe, Baidoa) and rural areas, particularly through upgrades to regional airstrips and connecting
infrastructure. The environment context of the proposed activities is largely hot, semi-arid to arid climate, susceptible to
recurring droughts, floods, and progressive land degradation. Socially, these locations are in areas characterized by rapid
population growth, with large numbers of internally displaced persons fleeing conflict and climate shocks, which strains
the already limited public infrastructure and services. Somalia is highly vulnerable to climate variability, with frequent
droughts and floods. The project integrates climate adaptation and disaster risk reduction measures, including resilient
infrastructure design and early warning systems. Given the FCV context there is ongoing security concerns, fragmented
governance, and recurrent climate disasters. The project’s design incorporates conflict sensitivity, inclusive stakeholder
engagement, and emergency preparedness to address these risks. Most activities are expected to occur within existing
infrastructure footprints, minimizing risks to high-biodiversity areas. However, some rural airstrips and corridors may be
near sensitive ecosystems, requiring careful screening and mitigation and others (e.g., airport-city connector roads,
flood mitigation infrastructure) may have indirect impacts beyond the immediate project footprint.
