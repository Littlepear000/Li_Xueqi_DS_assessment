    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context

1.      Tunisia’s economy in 2024 is set to have a cautious recovery in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, albeit
with challenges and opportunities. The country has one of the most diversified economies in Africa, with a historically
large services sector that has grown over the past decade.1 In 2023, significant export gains came from tourism recovery2
and export-oriented industry.3 On the other hand, severe drought, tight financing conditions and the pace of reform
implementation slowed economic growth in 2023, leaving the economy below its pre-Covid level compared to other
Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region countries. Real GDP growth in the first nine months of 2024 was 1 percent,
which was half the rate of 2022 and lower than 2021.4 This has led to higher unemployment and lower labor force
participation, with unemployment reaching 16 percent in Q3 2024 and labor force participation rate dropping to 46.2
percent, below pre-pandemic levels.5 Further, the growth slowdown amplifies the challenges Tunisia faces in covering its
external financing needs despite an improving external balance. Compounding these challenges, Tunisia has increasingly
relied on recurrent public expenditures to address citizens' immediate needs, leading fiscal and current account deficits
to widen —though these are expected to improve in 2024. This has also contributed to a rising debt burden, which
reached 80 percent of GDP in 2023.6 As of Spring 2024, the budget remains under pressure as the limited growth affects
tax revenues.7

2.      Tunisia’s health system continues to face the impact of an ongoing demographic and epidemiological shift. The
share of the population above 65 is high in Tunisia (9 percent), almost twice the average for MENA countries (5 percent),
and well above peers such as Algeria, Egypt, and Morocco. Non-communicable diseases (NCD) continue to be the main
source of the disease burden, causing 86 percent of deaths, with particularly high and increasing rates of hypertension
and diabetes. In 2016, population-level prevalence of hypertension was at 29 percent, and of diabetes 16 percent, placing
Tunisia amongst the countries with the highest rates in the MENA region. The control rates for these diseases are
particularly low, with 14 percent of cases for diabetes and 11 percent of cases for hypertension controlled.8 As a result of
these low control rates, the prevalence for chronic kidney disease amongst diabetics is 39 percent, with only 17 percent
of diabetic patients screened for the condition.9 There are over 100,000 new cases of strokes and acute cardiovascular
incidents, which require urgent intervention. Tunisia has a high share of road traffic accidents, with over 2,200 deaths and
10,000 injuries each year; most of this burden is for younger than 50, which creates an economic burden. These low
control rates not only increase morbidity and mortality, but also impose inefficiencies presenting an upwards pressure on
health expenditures.

3.      Climate change is anticipated to increase health risks faced by Tunisia's most vulnerable communities,
exacerbating environmental health concerns. Tunisia’s Mediterranean location makes it highly vulnerable to climate
change, with extreme weather events such as heat waves, heavy rainfall, and drought becoming more frequent. Rising
sea levels could impact nearly 78,700 people by 2100.10 Four years of drought, have significantly reduced agricultural
production, increasing food insecurity and malnutrition.11 Climate-related hazards pose high risks to 26 percent of the
population (18.9 percent exposed to drought, 7.3 percent to heatwaves, and 1.3 percent to floods).12 Under a high
emission scenario, the mean annual temperature is projected to rise by 5.3°C in 2100, causing as many as 56 deaths per
100,000 in the elderly (65+ years) by 2080 compared to the estimated baseline of 4 deaths per 100,000 annually between
1961 and 1990.13 Other negative health impacts associated with climate change in Tunisia include the recurrence and
emergence of vector-borne diseases (malaria, leishmaniasis, dengue and West Nile fever), an increase in water-borne and
food-borne diseases, and the aggravation of diseases related to air pollution.14 Rural poor populations, as well as outside
workers, women, children, the elderly are disproportionately affected.15 Environmental factors such as poor air quality,
water contamination, and exposure to hazardous chemicals further exacerbate the burden of NCDs.

4.      Tunisian health system faces organizational constraints in responding to these changes. While there are over
2,000 primary health centers (centre de santé de base – CSB), only 419 are open daily, and many operate once a week,
resulting in exceptionally low utilization rates at this level. Consequently, most patients seek services at hospitals, which
drives up operating costs and reduces the ability to serve populations in harder-to-reach areas. Regional and university
hospitals are unevenly distributed; and patients in disadvantaged areas need to travel over 300 kilometers to reach
essential and emergency health services. Recruitment and retention challenges of health professionals persist, with
substantial gaps in the nursing and specialist cadres; against the backdrop of an increased outward migration of health

5.      The Government has developed a National Health Policy (PNS) in response to lagging health outcomes and
fragmented governance. Developed through national dialogue, the PNS focuses on three core principles: (i) strengthening
governance, (ii) improving transparency, and (iii) deepening citizen engagement. The key pillars include (1) scaling up
family medicine to strengthen primary care (2) introducing an essential benefits package for all citizens, (3) merging
financial risk protection schemes for universal coverage, (4) enhancing public service delivery to reduce inequalities, and
(5) promoting multisectoral prevention approaches. These reforms aim to boost patient trust, health worker motivation,
and system efficiency. By 2030, Tunisia aims to reduce maternal mortality from 45 to 19 per 100,000, obesity rates from
26 percent to 20 percent, and OOP from 38 percent to 25 percent, and expand basic health insurance coverage to 100
percent of the population and increase government health spending to 6 percent of GDP.


 Relationship to CPF
6.      The Country Partnership Framework (CPF) Report No. 180304-TN lays out the World Bank Group’s strategy in
Tunisia for FY23–27, building on Tunisia’s 2023-25 development plan, the Tunisia Vision 2035 plan, the World Bank’s
Systematic Country Diagnostic as well as the Tunisia Country Climate and Development Report. The overarching
objective is to support the Tunisian government’s development plan to expand the economy to benefit all, create quality
jobs, integrate environmental sustainability and boost resilience to climate change. The proposed Project would
contribute to two CPF high-level objectives (HLO): (a) HLO 2: strengthened human capital, as it would work towards
improving universal health coverage by improving quality and coverage of primary healthcare services; and (b) HLO 3:
Improved Climate Resilience by improving Tunisia pandemic preparedness and response capabilities. The Project will also
address environmental resilience by integrating energy efficiency and sustainable healthcare practices into Tunisia's
health sector, ensuring that health facilities are better equipped to manage climate-related risks and reduce their carbon
footprint. The proposed Project would also aim to boost the cross-cutting objectives of gender, by empowering women
through increased labor force participation, especially among female higher education graduates.

7.      The Project will also contribute to the World Bank’s ambitious new goal to reach 1.5 billion people with quality,
affordable health services by 2030. The Project expects to reach 2.4 million people by strengthening the accessibility and
quality of health care services, increasing pandemic preparedness and improving emergency response systems. In
addition, the Project will prioritize environmentally sustainable healthcare practices, integrating energy efficiency in
hospitals, implementing sustainable waste management practices, and ensuring that health facilities are climate resilient.
The Project preparation and implementation will benefit from close cross-GP and WBG collaboration, notably with
Agriculture (on One Health and early warning systems for zoonotic diseases), Energy (on energy efficiency for hospitals),
Environment (on climate resilience and sustainable waste management in healthcare facilities), Digital (on expanding
connectivity and health information systems to the primary care level). This close collaboration will continue throughout
preparation and implementation. The Project will respond to the Global Challenge Program on Enhanced Health
Emergency Prevention, Preparedness and Response. With financing from the Pandemic Fund, component 1 will enhance
the health sector’s capacity to prevent, detect, and respond to health emergencies by strengthening health systems and
supporting a One Health approach.

C. Proposed Development Objective(s)
To improve access to quality healthcare services and enhance the resilience and capacity for pandemic prevention,
preparedness, and response of Tunisia’s health system



Key Results (From PCN)

PDO achievement will be measured by a subset of the preliminary PDO indicators proposed below.

    •        Percentage of the population with access to optimized emergency services
    •        Percentage of patients that are screened, diagnosed and controlled for hypertension and diabetes at the
             primary care level
    •        Number of beneficiaries with enhanced resilience to climate and environmental risks, including improved air
             quality, access to clean water, and waste management systems in healthcare facilities
    •        Number of patients managed via the national management system for disease surveillance and response



D. Concept Description

        8.       The components are financed with US$108 million of IBRD financing and US$17 million RETF grant
        from the Pandemic Fund. This covers part of the investments needed to finance the ambition and scope of
        health reforms envisioned by the government. There is room to increase the scope of the Project and to scale
        up its financing during the design phase. The total scope and financing amount will be determined during
        appraisal.


        9.         The PDO will be achieved through a set of activities across four components.
        10.   Component 1: Strengthening Health System Resilience (US$25M, including US$17M RETF from the
        Pandemic Fund)
        (i)      Component 1 focuses on strengthening surveillance systems, climate change adaptation, and
        laboratory capacity for pandemic preparedness. It aims to enhance Tunisia's ability to effectively detect,
        assess, and respond to public health threats through improved surveillance systems, a skilled workforce, and
        robust laboratory networks. Specific activities will include: 1) strengthening early warning systems including
        against climate-driven health risks, and improving risk assessment capabilities through integrated One Health
        approaches; 2) supporting workforce development and training for health professionals; 3) enhancing
        laboratory capacity and quality management for effective diagnostic networks and improved biosafety
        standards; and 4) strengthening the coordination of the preparation, response, and recovery for public health
        emergencies.
        Component 2: Supporting reorganization of preventive and primary health services (US$50M)
        (ii)    Component 2 seeks to transform primary health care services to improve the efficiency, distribution
        and quality of services. It will do so through four subcomponents: i) strengthening the organizational structure

       governance framework, and strengthening human resources for health at the PHC level; ii) upgrading the
       capacity of CSBs based on the PHC service reorganization plan; and iii) supporting the deployment of a
       comprehensive PHC information system.
       Component 3: Strengthening emergency medical services ($49M)


       (iii)   Component 3 aims to strengthen Tunisia’s emergency medical services. It will enhance the pre-
       hospitalization phase by improving emergency call management and ambulance dispatch systems. It will also
       focus on the hospitalization phase by enhancing triage systems and ensuring efficient patient transfers and
       address the post-hospitalization phase by establishing a referral network to connect discharged patients with
       primary care and rehabilitation services. This component will also promote environmental sustainability in
       emergency services by adopting green technologies and reducing energy consumption in emergency
       management systems.
       Component 4: Implementation Support, Training and Project Management (US$1M)

       (iv)     This component will support technical assistance and training for the Ministry of Health. It will finance
       Project management, fiduciary oversight, procurement management, and the implementation of
       environmental and social measures to ensure compliance with ESF. Training will focus on integrating E&S risk
       management into healthcare operations, building capacity for identifying, mitigating, and monitoring
       environmental and social impacts throughout the Project lifecycle. This will include enhancing the Ministry's
       ability to address key environmental issues such as healthcare waste management, climate resilience, and
       energy efficiency in healthcare facilities. Eligible expenditures under Component 4 include goods (IT and office
       equipment), services (consultancies) and non-salary operating costs.
