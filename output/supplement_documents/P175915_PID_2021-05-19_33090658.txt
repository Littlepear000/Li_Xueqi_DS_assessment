     B. Introduction and Context

     Country Context
     1.     Pre-Covid-19 strong economic performance in Senegal did not translate into substantive improvements of living
     standards or reduced inequality. Over the 2016-19 period, Senegal’s average real GDP growth reached 6.1 percent,
     outperforming the West African Economic and Monetary Union (UEMOA) by 0.7 percentage points (p.p.) and the Sub-
     Saharan Africa (SSA) average by 3.3 p.p. Despite strong growth, poverty reduction remained modest, and the share of
     consumption of the bottom 40 percent of the population contracted during the same period (the Gini coefficient
     remained stable at 0.35). New comparable estimates indicate that poverty declined from 43 percent in 2011 to
     37.8 percent in 2018-20191. With an average 1 p.p. reduction per year, Senegal’s track record falls short of the one
     achieved by the 15 best performing SSA countries, which saw their poverty rates decline by 1 to 3.4 p.p. per year2.

     2.     This modest poverty reduction is threatened to be reversed by the Covid-19 pandemic, which has considerable
     economic, fiscal, and social consequences for Senegal. As a result of this sanitary crisis, real GDP growth is estimated to
     have contracted, from 4.4 percent in 2019 to -0.7 percent in 2020, accompanied by rising external current account (from
     7.8 percent of GDP in 2019 to 8.9 percent in 2020), fiscal deficit (from 3.9 percent of GDP in 2019 to 6.5 percent in 2020),
     and public debt (from 64.5 percent of GDP in 2019 to 67.6 percent of GDP in 2020). Services and export growth have
     been declining, as tourism and transport continue to suffer a standstill and key markets have shut down. Lower
     remittances and labor income loss subdue private consumption. Due to these shocks, the poverty headcount may have
     increased to approximately 39 percent in 2020 (equivalent to 300,000 additional poor), up from 38 percent in 2019,
     undoing one third of the reduction achieved since 2011.

     3.     The Government of Senegal (GoS) has taken decisive measures to contain the pandemic, mitigate its socio-
     economic impact, and return to the growth trajectory, including by leveraging natural resources. On top of containment
     measures, the GoS has put in place a comprehensive support and recovery plan, the Economic and Social Resilience
     Program (Programme de résilience économique et sociale - PRES), underpinned by a dedicated solidarity fund at the
     Central Bank of Western African States - FORCE-COVID-19. The PRES’ main objectives are to upgrade the health system
     and mitigate the economic fallout, while providing targeted support to vulnerable households and firms. In addition, the
     GoS issued on September 5, 2020 a new version of its Priority Action Plan 2 - Adjusted and Accelerated (PAP2-AA). The
     PAP2-AA, spanning 2021-23, seeks to catch up with the initial path of the National Development Plan (Plan Sénégal
     Emergent – PSE) while considering the new challenges from the pandemic crisis and fostering an inward-driven

  Sectoral and Institutional Context
  4.     Natural resources in Senegal are under pressure, putting growth on an unsustainable path. Over the last 25
  years, Growth as well as Produced and Human Capitals have grown on par, but Natural Capital did not follow the same
  pattern and decreased in per capita terms3. Ecological modeling confirms this worrying trend, whereby Senegal has
  become a net borrower of natural resources since 20054. Natural resources are facing growing anthropogenic pressure,
  be it overexploitation, or pollution and climate change risks. The annual cost of coastal zone degradation alone was
  estimated at 7.6 percent of GDP in 2017 (much higher than in Benin, Cote d’Ivoire, and Togo)5. Coastal flooding and
  erosion are the main drivers (61% combined) and are expected to worsen with unabated climate. Senegal's natural capital
  is gradually eroding, this compromise both economic growth and people's livelihoods.

  The Fishery Sector
  5.      The fisheries sector is vital for Senegal’s economic and social development. It provides nutrition and food
  security (providing more than 70% of animal protein), improves livelihoods and employment (at least 15% of jobs, both
  direct and indirect, including a large share of women involved in post capture processing and commercialization) and is
  a source of economic growth (3.2% of the GDP and 10% of all exports)6.
  6.    Despite important transformation, including with support from the West Africa Regional Fisheries Project
  (WARFP, implemented over 2009-16), the fisheries sector faces many challenges, which can be grouped in three main
  categories:
      a. Over-fishing: more than half the fish stocks in Senegal are over-fished (out of 18 fish stocks that are monitored -
         both pelagic and demersal fish7, 12 are considered overexploited, 5 fully exploited, and only 3 underexploited).
         There is also an increase in the degradation of fish habitats due to bad fishing practices, pollution, and plastics. In
         addition, climate change is posing a significant threat to the availability of the resource (see Annex 1). Over-fishing
         reduces the fish stock available and can completely disrupt the food chain.
      b. Low added-value: there is low value addition to the products landed and exported from Senegal often without
         any transformation. As a result, a very high economic potential is totally unrealized. Overall, the post-harvest to
         transformation segment still registers high losses (without proper handling and storage) and does not always
         operate within modern standards and practices for security (with increasing accidents at sea) and food safety
         (e.g., low level of compliance with sanitary norms required for exporting). As for aquaculture, which offers the
         potential to re-balance development inland with economic activity, jobs and food security in the hinterland, its

     7.      The Government’s Fisheries and Aquaculture Strategy9 duly acknowledges the sector’s challenges and draws
     the path towards a sector that contributes to food security, economic growth, and local development (in line with the
     PSE). The proposed Project, formulated in response to a Government’s request for a successor to the WARFP, will
     support the achievement of the sector’s strategy. In particular, the Project will seek to improve the management of
     fisheries by supporting a larger involvement of communities, which can help increase access control, maintenance of
     infrastructure, and the sustainability of interventions. The proposed Project will also improve the fisheries information
     system for better-informed management and greater transparency. In addition, the Project will promote the valorization
     of the halieutic production, by strengthening value chains and their formalization, and the accelerated development of
     aquaculture, by setting up the enabling framework for the attractiveness of the sector, strengthening technical skills, and
     financing key infrastructure.
     The Forestry Sector
     8.     Senegalese forests play a key role in providing economic opportunities and contributing to rural livelihoods.
     Forests are a major source of timber and charcoal with about equal production value for each. In terms of usage,
     fuelwood (which encompasses all woody energy forms, including charcoal) provides up to 80 percent of households’ final
     energy consumption. Forests also provide a variety of non-timber products, such as honey, nuts and fruits, natural gums,
     or medicinal plants, which are necessary for the subsistence of rural populations, particularly as a safety net during the
     lean season. Forests and forested landscapes (and related protected areas) are also touristic destinations. While less
     important than seaside tourism, nature-based tourism does inject resources for natural resources management and
     sustainability and create jobs and an outlet for local economic activities. While a quantification of the contribution of
     forests to economic activities and livelihood in Senegal is not available, recent estimates from Burkina Faso (also in the
     Sudano-sahelian zone) indicate that the forestry sector contributes around 9.6 percent of Burkina Faso’s GDP and 40
     percent of households derive income from forest-based economic activities.10 In addition, forests provide a range of
     ecosystem services, such as regulating watersheds, improving air quality, providing habitats for animals, preventing soil
     erosion and helping slowdown desertification, and storing a large amount of carbon. Forests could thus contribute to half
     the emission reduction effort in Senegal’s recent NDC (December 2020).
     9.      Despite some progress in managing forests, forest degradation in Senegal is still alarming. Senegal has engaged
     in a set of reforms and programs for sustainable management of its forests, including via the support from the Sustainable
     Management of Traditional and Alternative Sources of Energy Project II (PROGEDE II, implemented over 2010-18).
     Despite these efforts, forest loss and degradation is progressing at an alarming rate: the reduction in forest cover is
     estimated at 9.4 percent between 2001 and 2019 and accentuated in the southern and south-eastern regions (which are
     hubs for charcoal production). Loss of forested areas between 2010 and 2017 is estimated to be four times greater than

  10.     Community-based approaches, pioneered under PROGEDE, have proven transformative in promoting forest
  integrated management and bringing concrete gains in income, living conditions, and sustainability. Experience with
  PROGEDE has shown that wood energy can be a successful entry point into sustainable forest management, enticing
  participation of communities, addressing several drivers of forest and ecosystems degradation and fragmentation, and
  enhancing income and livelihood. Wood energy provides a strong incentive for communities to engage in sustainable
  forest management as it is in their own economic interest. It can involve a mix of forest management activities, including
  management and fight against illicit exploitation; reforestation, fencing, and assisted regeneration; control of bush fires;
  as well as wood energy production (possibly mixed with agroforestry). It can also promote emerging business and value
  chains (i.e., charcoal and improved stoves production). While significantly improving the living conditions of the
  populations in its intervention area, PROGEDE also achieved major technical and institutional progress, notably in terms
  of associating local governments and communities to the management of natural resources. This experience has also led
  to changes in the Forest Code (better regulation on timber, local forest management structures, benefit sharing, and
  local development funds financed by forest revenue). In response to a request from the Government for a follow-up
  project, the proposed Project seeks to consolidate and scale-up the success and achievements from PROGEDE, in terms
  of community-based approaches for sustainable forest management.

 Environmental and Social Management
  11.     Since the adoption of the Environmental Code in 2001, Senegal has developed a national environmental and
  social (E&S) management system coordinated by the Department of Environment and Classified Establishments (DEEC).
  It includes: a system to handle urgent environmental issues by the Environmental Emergency Response Center, the
  establishment of a National Technical Committee for Environmental Assessment with representation from different
  sectoral ministries and stakeholders, as well as a decentralized system to monitor environmental performance at the
  regional level through Regional Environmental and Social Monitoring Committees (CRSEs) and the Regional Divisions of
  Environment and Classified Establishments (DREECs). As the country is engaging in the development of large-scale
  investments in various strategic sectors (e.g., extractive industries, major infrastructures, land reform), it is necessary to
  further strengthen the national E&S management system and improve technical and operational E&S management
  capacities to meet development ambitions. Several areas need strengthening, such as in terms of legislation, to provide
  guidelines for taking into account the current environmental challenges (e.g., climate change, various types of pollution,
  biodiversity management, etc.), as well as for preparing operational environmental and social management instruments,
  and in terms of institutions, low technical capacity to prepare required instruments, lack of sustainable financing
  mechanisms to support the supervision of the implementation of these instruments, and need to explore new
  technologies for supervision.

  Key Results (From PCN)

  16.    The proposed key indicators of the project are as follows:
     a. To improve sustainable management of fisheries and forests:
         i. Fisheries management plans implemented;
        ii. Forest area brought under management plans;
     b. To promote selected value chains:
          i. Share of fish landing at project sites following improved practices;
         ii. Number of households with access to sustainable woodfuel sources and more efficient woodfuel technology
             or alternative energy;
        iii. Improved aquaculture production capacity (increase in production);
     c. To strengthen E&S risk management:
         i. Proportion of development projects with a validated E&S impact assessment, a monitoring plan and annual
            inspections;
        ii. Percentage of citizens’ enquiries to the environmental emergencies response center that are addressed
            satisfactorily, as per due process.

  D. Concept Description
  17.    Component 1 - Institutional framework for managing environmental and social risks and intersectoral strategic
  collaborations (fisheries, environment, and forests) (US$ 16M IDA). This component seeks to strengthen intersectoral
  coordination for the management of shared or complementary natural resources as well as institutional capacity for
  managing the environmental and social (E&S) risks of projects and programs. Key activities include : (i) Capacity building
  and provision of equipment for the management of E&S impacts (E&S regulatory and coordination framework and
  Building the technical and operational capacity on E&S management); (ii) Streamlining management of Marine & Coastal
  and forest natural resources (Strategic analysis of community-led fisheries initiatives, marine & coastal natural resources
  management / zoning, and Strengthening resilience and sustainability of the smoked seafood value chain); and (iii) Citizen
  engagement and communication (educate on the value of natural marine and forest resources, raise awareness to
  mounting environmental risks (pollution, climate change), and promote eco-friendly behaviors and solutions).
  18.     Component 2 - Resilience and productivity of fisheries and aquaculture (US$ 42M IDA) . This component will
  consolidate the achievement of the West Africa Regional Fisheries Program in Senegal (WARFP), with support to: (i)
  fisheries management and community-led initiatives (fisheries management plans, fishing permits and licenses

  Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
  .

 The Project is rated substantial both on environment and social. While the Project is expected to have positive impacts on
 the environment and people through the straightening of the management frameworks of natural resources in forestry
 and fishery, negative impacts will be related to work conditions, OHS, community health and safety, land acquisition and
 involuntary resettlement, resource efficiency and pollution, mainly due to disposal and management of construction and
 waste, nuisances related to air and noise emissions, use of water, biodiversity, etc. Adequate mitigation measures will be
 put in place, including capacity building to address all the potential adverse impacts.
