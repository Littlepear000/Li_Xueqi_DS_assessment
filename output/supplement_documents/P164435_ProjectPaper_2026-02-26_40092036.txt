 I.   BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING
A. Introduction
1. This Project Paper seeks the Regional Vice President’s approval to (i) provide additional financing (AF) in the amount
of Eur1.5 million (US$1.73 million equivalent) through a Grant from the Deutsche Gesellschaft fur Internationale
Zusammerarbeit (GIZ) to the Republic of Burundi for the Solar Energy in Local Communities Project (“SOLEIL-Nyakiriza”,
P164435). In addition, the project will be restructured to extend the closing date from March 1, 2026, to March 1, 2028,
and to revise the results framework. The AF aims to enhance the Project's scope and sustainability by supporting digital-
energy-health-education-nexus via improved connectivity allowing remote monitoring of public-facility solar systems,
expansion of digitalization initiatives in health and education, and extending digital access to surrounding communities.
The original financing of the Project was approved by the World Bank Board of Directors on February 28, 2020 and became
effective on September 3, 2020. The initial total project cost of US$100 million was reduced to US$63 million in June 2023
through a first restructuring, which included the cancellation of the mini-grid activity. The proposed closing date extension
will allow the Project to complete all on-going activities and meet the Project Development Objective (PDO). The PDO will
remain unchanged. However, the results framework will be adjusted with the PDO indicators revised to reflect the focus
on new connections through the M300 initiative and the new scorecard.
2. The Project (“SOLEIL Nyakiriza”) is an important contributor to the Mission 300 (M300)1 target. As part of this effort,
there will also be a transition towards cleaner sources of energy. It is estimated that about half of the M300 target will
be most cost-effectively achieved via grid connections and half via mostly private sector-led distributed renewable energy
(DRE). In addition to residential connections, the WBG and the AfDB aim to extend electricity access to Micro, Small and
Medium Enterprises (MSMEs), commercial and industrial sectors, social infrastructure such as schools and health facilities,
and productive users of energy, which are crucial for economic growth. The Project complements the ongoing ASCENT
Burundi project (P184491), approved in June 2024, which provides new or improved electricity access which is expected
to benefit approximately1.2 million people in Burundi.
3. The AF is expected to significantly increase the electricity access rate in Burundi, in line with the M300 initiative. As
part of the M300 initiative, the Government of Burundi (GoB) has embarked on developing a National Energy Compact
which charts a pathway towards the achievement of universal access to energy in a reliable, affordable and sustainable
manner, outlining critical reforms, and measures and actions to achieve energy access targets.
4. The Project is rated Moderately Satisfactory both for achievement of the PDO and Implementation Progress. The
Project initially suffered implementation delays due to Covid-19 crisis and low Government capacity. However, the
implementation pace is currently at its peak, with 75 percent of physical progress conducted and US$40.18 million
disbursed, representing a total disbursement of 62 percent. The PDO is still highly relevant and aligned with the
Government of Burundi’s (GoB) vision to reach SDG7 and provide clean cooking solutions to 40 percent of the population
by 2030.


B. Country and Sector Context
5. Burundi, a landlocked country with a population of 12.9 million, faces significant challenges in delivering quality
and reliable services and creating jobs for its rapidly growing population (annual growth rate of 2.7 percent). The
country has one of the lowest electricity access rates globally. 25.9 percent of the population has access to power,
compared to the Sub-Saharan Africa average of 52 percent. Urban areas are relatively better served: 72.5 percent of
residents in the economic capital, Bujumbura, and 42.6 percent in other urban centers have access to electricity. In stark

6. The GoB is expanding energy access through the Energy Compact launched in September 2025 and the M300
initiative, which targets 70 percent electricity access and 40 percent clean cooking access by 2030. Under the 2024 least-
cost electrification strategy, access will come from the main grid (60%), stand-alone solar (28%), and mini-grids (12%).
Achieving universal electricity access and clean cooking under SDG7 will require an estimated US$3.49 billion, making
private sector financing essential. The World Bank is supporting the GoB through policy dialogue, donor coordination,
technical assistance, and financing to help reach universal access by 2030.
7. Achieving these ambitious national targets will require addressing the stark disparities in energy access across the
country. Lessons from the energy–digital–health nexus show that sustainable development requires both reliable
electricity and digital connectivity. Low rural electrification, combined with weak digital infrastructure, constrains public
service delivery and socio-economic opportunities, leaving health centers and schools without the power and internet
needed for digital learning, healthcare services, emergency communication, and remote infrastructure monitoring.


C. Project Description and Status

8. Project Description. The SOLEIL-Nyakiriza Project is designed to provide energy services to promote human capital
development, enhance productivity of rural enterprises, and reach poor and vulnerable populations, including women.
The Project focuses on: (i) electrifying up to 354 schools and 362 health centers with modular solar PV systems and
implementing a multi-layered maintenance approach; (ii) providing 65,000 off-grid solar products to poor and vulnerable
households; (iii) distributing 300,000 clean and efficient cookstoves to households and schools; and (iv) strengthening the
enabling environment through improved policies, regulations, private sector participation, and capacity building for off-
grid solar energy and clean cooking solutions.

9. The PDO is to expand access to energy services for households, enterprises, schools, and health centers in rural
areas of Burundi and will be achieved through three components: (i) Energy Services for Schools and Health Service
Centers (Original cost US$27 million, Updated cost US$28.23 million); (ii) Energy Services for Households (Original cost
US$17 million, Updated cost US$17.5 million); and (iii) Technical Assistance, Capacity Building and Project Implementation
Support (Original cost US$19 million, no change). The PDO remains highly relevant to the country and is in line with the
World Bank’s mission, the M300 initiative and SDG7 to achieve universal access to electricity by 2030.
10. The original financing includes US$100 million IDA equivalent and US$2 million counterpart funding from
Government of Burundi. The first restructuring of the Project was approved in June 2023 consisting of clarification of
some eligible expenses in the Financing Agreement (FA), cancellation of the former component 2 on “Energy Services for
Rural Communities” and adjustment of project implementation arrangements and results framework.
11. The Project is implemented by a Project Implementation Unit within the Ministry of Mining Resources, Energy,
Industry and Trade (Ministère des Ressources Minières, Energetiques, de l’Industrie et du Commerce or “MRMEICT”2),
on behalf of the Republic of Burundi. The line Ministry’s main role is to lead national energy policy design, oversee project
implementation, and monitor sector results.
12. Current progress. Under Component 1, seventy percent of the targeted health centers (261 out of 362) have been
electrified with solar energy, with the remainder to be completed by March 2026. School electrification (354) is expected
to be commissioned by April 2026, with works expected to be completed by March 2026. The technical design of the solar
PV systems prioritizes the use of innovative, modular, and scalable solar PV systems that can be expanded as demand

13. Under Component 2 (formerly component 3 before first Project restructuring), the Project established the Solar
Energy and Cooking Fund managed by an independent fund manager Bamboo Capital Partners. The fund which provides
start-up, growth, and Results-Based Financing (RBF) grants to private companies to supply off-grid solar PV systems and
clean cookstoves committed 53 percent of the total US$16.5 million allocation and disbursed US$4 million. To promote
sustainability and local ownership, targeted support has been provided to women entrepreneurs. Currently, the fund has
supported 23 solar companies reaching 27,690 households and selling 78,445 products. In the clean cooking sector, 28
contracts have been signed, and start-up and growth grants have been disbursed. A total of 54,186 improved cookstoves
were sold. In addition, the Private Sector Technical Assistance Facility was created to provide technical assistance to
beneficiaries and other stakeholders, further strengthening the enabling environment. Recent learnings also highlight the
critical role of consumer financing in overcoming affordability barriers for rural households, with beneficiaries leveraging
Village Savings and Loan Associations, cooperatives, and microfinance institutions to expand access to energy solutions in
rural Burundi.
14. Component 3 (formerly component 4 before Project first restructuring) has been supporting long term sustainability
of decentralized solutions, and of the energy sector overall. Under this component, the Project has been strengthening
technical assistance and capacity-building in Burundi’s energy sector. It has been supporting key activities to advance the
World Bank’s engagement and help the Government achieve its M300 target through the National Energy Compact such
as: (i) least-cost power system planning and strategy development for universal electricity access; (ii) development of
quality standards for off grid products, (iii) definition of a national e-waste management strategy; (iv) sector reform and
institutional strengthening, such as the definition of a performance contract between the utility REGIDESO and the GoB,
and the initiation of leadership recruitment for REGIDESO; and (v) the design of a Renewable Energy Master’s Program
being launched in March 2026..
15. Current Closing Date. The current closing date of March 1, 2026, does not provide sufficient time to fully complete
ongoing contracted activities, establish long-term maintenance mechanisms, or ensure sustained efforts to develop the
off-grid and clean cooking private-led market. In detail, a 2-year extension would allow, (i) the effective completion the
supply & installation contracts for the PV systems in the 362 Health Centers (CDS) and 354 schools by April 2026; (ii)
execution of the subsequent 2-year operation and maintenance contracts of the installed systems to ensure their
sustainability and durability by March 2028; (iii) the implementation and operationalization of the maintenance financing
system via Performance Based Financing in the health sector; (iv) the full utilization of the Solar and Clean Cooking Energy
Fund (FESEC) subsidy fund which would significantly increase the chances of an accelerated and sustainable growth of the
market for clean individual energy systems through RBF; and (v) the rollout of the Connectivity and Remote Performance
Tracking of Public Facility Solar Assets via a Unified Monitoring Platform under Component 1. This would also allow enough
time to try out innovative solutions such as Productive Use of Energy (PUE).
16. Progress towards achievement of the PDO and overall implementation are rated ‘Moderately Satisfactory’. The
Project implementation regained momentum due to strategic actions, including the restructuring in June 2023, the
appointment of a new Project coordinator, and the signing of key contracts including the RBF. Progress toward achieving
the PDO is advancing, with electrification of health facilities nearing completion and school installations underway,

18. Lessons learned from the Project so far will be used to improve performance ratings during the implementation of
this AF. To address the implementation challenges, the World Bank is closely monitoring the effective implementation
of an action plan that aims to increase disbursement by June 2026 through regular weekly check-ins with a keen focus on:
(i) milestones, (ii) associated risks, and (iii) mitigation measures. Actions have already been taken, as documented in the
last Implementation Support Mission held in September 2025.


   II. DESCRIPTION OF PROPOSED CHANGES AND ADDITIONAL FINANCING

B. Description of activities under the AF
23. The AF is fostering Smart Connectivity and Remote Performance Tracking of Public Facility Solar Assets via a Unified
Monitoring Platform under Component 1. Current electrification efforts reveal a major gap: only about 15 percent of
electrified public facilities in Burundi have reliable internet, while 35 percent have none or only intermittent 2G access,
limiting digital health, education systems, and remote solar PV monitoring. To address this and meet the PDO, the project
will provide high-speed satellite internet to about 400 rural health and education facilities, enabling real-time monitoring
and essential digital services. Low-orbit satellite solutions are proposed as a cost-effective option, alongside hardware and
subscription support. A World Bank digital specialist will support implementation, which will use direct contracting with
existing solar suppliers to meet the September 2026 completion deadline.
24. The AF is also Expanding Solar Access through Results-Based Financing under Component 2. Building on the success
of the private sector vehicles under the Project, this component aims to scale access to solar energy in underserved
communities where up to 716 public facilities are electrified (component 1) through results-based financing. Performance-
based incentives will be used to support private sector-led market expansion into those targeted communities, reduce
upfront costs, and stimulate demand for quality solar solutions. This is expected to have a catalytic effect on the entire
community at the nexus energy-health-education-digitalization. It will also reduce the risk of abuse of the solar system at
each health facility (component 1). The Project will liaise with private sectors to encourage them to open sales points near
the health and education facilities. If there is a community-facing internet connection at the public facility, sales agents
can use available internet at the public facility to upload their sales data and enable easier access for PAYGO payments.
Mobile money payments by the communities will be possible and incentivized. The RBF scheme under FESEC includes, in
addition to the base subsidy per verified product, a set of modular performance bonuses (primes) designed to incentivize
specific market behaviors. These include a top up to encourage companies to offer installment payment solutions or
partner with microfinance institutions and VSLAs; bonuses for serving poor and vulnerable households; and incentives to
create synergies with electrified public facilities by expanding access in surrounding communities. Together, these
performance-based bonuses reduce commercial risk for companies while promoting inclusive, community-level energy
access.

 III. KEY RISKS
26. The overall risk of the Project remains High. The overall implementation risk of the Project remains High, reflecting
Burundi’s fragile macroeconomic conditions, limited institutional capacity, and systemic governance constraints that
affect implementation efficiency and sustainability. While the operating environment has improved since effectiveness,
key risks persist related to long-term maintenance of public-facility solar systems, coordination among implementing
entities, and sustainability of private-sector participation. The additional GIZ financing introduces important opportunities
for digital monitoring and community access but also adds technical and coordination complexity that must be closely
managed.
27. Political and Governance risk remains High. Although the political situation has stabilized since the 2020 elections,
new elections are coming up in 2026, the governance risks remain high due to limited institutional capacity, political
interference, coordination challenges across sectors, and potential policy or leadership changes. To mitigate these risks,
the Project builds on strong government commitment and continued dialogue with development partners and champions
across sectors, supported by the inter-ministerial cross-sectoral Steering Committee to ensure transparency, coordination,
and policy continuity.
28. Macroeconomic risk remains High. Macroeconomic conditions remain fragile, with persistent inflation, limited fiscal
space, and constraints of accessing forex that could affect project implementation, procurement, and payment flows. The
Project mitigates these risks through careful financial planning, use of local-currency RBF disbursements, and close
coordination with the Recipient’s Ministry of Finance within the broader macro-fiscal dialogue supported by the World
Bank and partners. Sustained reforms in exchange rate management and fiscal discipline remain critical to maintaining
stability.
29. Sector Strategies and Polices risk remains Substantial. The enabling environment for off-grid expansion has improved
but remains constrained by evolving regulatory and institutional frameworks. Since approval, the GoB has joined the M300
initiative and adopted the National Energy Compact (September 2025) to accelerate universal access through
decentralized, private-sector-led solutions and expanded solar distribution. An energy policy update (by March 2026) will
further clarify priorities. The Project supports these objectives by strengthening planning capacity, promoting integrated
community electrification, and advancing a transparent, light-touch regulatory framework that attracts private investment
while protecting consumers.
30. Technical Design of Project risk remains Substantial. The project builds on proven approaches with no major technical
risks anticipated, but ensuring sustainable maintenance of solar systems in public facilities remains a key challenge. Digital
monitoring will improve oversight but requires strong coordination and careful management of connectivity costs through
least-cost designs and alignment with health and education digitalization efforts. Targeted technical assistance,
standardized maintenance frameworks, and securing recurrent budgets through the PBF mechanism are essential for long-
term sustainability.
31. Fiduciary risk remains Substantial. Fiduciary responsibilities are now centralized within the Project Implementation
Unit (PIU) at MRMEICT. Although the PIU has gained experience managing World Bank–financed activities, its capacity
remains limited, particularly on contract management. To mitigate this risk, the PIU is strengthening its team with a Senior
Procurement Specialist, with particular attention paid to monitoring and optimizing the most critical contracts. Continued

                                           Official Use Only                                                         Page 7
           The World Bank
           Solar Energy in Local Communities (P164435)


training, close supervision, and timely technical assistance will be essential to ensure compliance with World Bank
procedures and efficient contract management.
32. Stakeholders risk is Substantial. The project engages multiple public institutions, private solar and clean cooking
companies, financial intermediaries, and beneficiary communities, requiring sustained coordination and clear
communication to manage expectations and maintain trust. Although structured engagement mechanisms are in place—
including the inter-ministerial Steering Committee, private sector coordination platforms, and the project-level grievance
redress mechanism—risks remain related to affordability constraints, private sector liquidity challenges, and potential
resistance to new digital monitoring and maintenance arrangements, which will require continued outreach and
structured dialogue.
33. Other risks are substantial. Private sector engagement is critical to achieving project results. FESEC and ATSP have
strengthened the enabling environment by supporting over 40 off-grid firms with grants and technical assistance, but
many remain financially fragile due to limited capital, foreign exchange constraints, and market volatility. The project will
continue to mitigate these risks by improving quality assurance with BBN, ensuring predictable RBF support, and
promoting a clear regulatory framework to sustain private sector growth and resilience.


   IV. APPRAISAL SUMMARY
  A. Technical, Economic and Financial
34. Project investments (under Components 1 through 3) use well-proven technologies and present no unusual
installation, commissioning or operational challenges. As described in the risk section, the main challenge will be to
ensure that sound maintenance mechanisms are in place throughout the lifecycle of the investments.
35. Under Component 1, solar systems and CECs for health centers and schools meet rigorous technical performance
specifications. For solar systems, recognized International Electrotechnical Commission standards apply, and best practice
design using standard component building blocks and remote monitoring capability, while the procurements have
confirmed these as reliable cost-effective and least O&M cost solutions. This design ensures that O&M requirements are
kept to a minimum, thus increasing the likelihood of system uptime over the longer term. The remote monitoring platform
design was initially piloted under the Project with ESMAP support, while in turn the Project has supported ESMAP in
further development and testing of a performance monitoring Energy-as-a-Service (EaaS) platform on 150 operational
solar sites systems. This innovative best-practice platform will be rolled out at scale to all 700 public facility solar sites
under the AF, to track O&M contracts. For stoves installed in schools, the methodology related to testing of household
stoves (required under Component 3 before distributors receive financing) is applied to assess fuel savings in school
settings.
36. The value-for-money case for AF of US$0.9 million for improved connectivity at public facilities is supported by the
expected performance gains and long-term O&M cost reductions associated with more efficient operational
management of the national fleet of 716 solar installations. The total solar investment of US$16.5 million (averaging
US$23,791 per site) would increase by only 1.8 percent through the inclusion of connectivity investments at 300 sites. The
amortized annual O&M cost is estimated at US$1,243 per site, covering all preventive and corrective maintenance visits,
remote monitoring services, and component replacement over a 15-year period (US$870,000 annually for all 700 sites).
These O&M costs could increase by up to 21 percent if dedicated satellite internet connections are provided to 300 sites
(US$1,053,000 annually), but the increase could be limited to 8.3 percent if satellite-enabled cellular connections are
shared across multiple sites via other commercially and technically viable options. It is thus imperative that a technology
neutral approach to connectivity expansion is taken. The affordability of these digital subscription costs is assessed against
the incremental benefits.
37. Realizing a positive cost–benefit case for dedicated satellite connectivity requires activation of priority sector

                                           Official Use Only                                                         Page 8

digitalization initiatives in health and education. Digital platforms in these sectors support asset, operations, and budget
management; facility-level communication and emergency response; and access to online health, education, and digital
services. The Ministry of Health’s Digitalization Division is advancing HMIS, DHIS, CMMS (GMAO), and other digital health
initiatives, currently covering 90 percent of public hospitals and around 120 health centers with satellite connectivity from
development partners. The Division aims to expand access to more facilities, and the sector is expected to assume
connectivity costs after project closure.
38. Under Component 2, all solar systems and stoves deployed directly or indirectly through the Project need to meet
minimum performance and reliability standards. For solar, strict Lighting Global standards ensure the project raises the
bar in Burundi’s off-grid solar market. For clean energy cooking (CECs), eligibility is based on minimum fuel savings, aligned
with ISO/TR19867 voluntary performance targets. A stove testing lab at the University of Bujumbura will use ISO-standard
instruments for thermal efficiency and emissions testing. The project provides technical support to the BBN in developing
and adopting internationally recognized solar norms; the BBN has actively contributed to ISO/19867 stove work. This
activity is expected to create a quality-verified market segment in the country.
39. The original economic analysis used a cost-benefit framework to determine the development impact of the Project,
the rationale for public-sector involvement, and the value added of the World Bank’s support. The economic returns of
the Project were derived from the avoided cost of substituting SHSs and CECs for traditional sources of lighting and cook
stoves for household, institutional (health and education), and business beneficiaries. The estimated economic returns of
the Project did not account for other likely benefits such as increased level of service and local environmental and health
costs associated with deforestation and dry cell disposal. The same methodology has been used in the revised analysis of
the Project for the proposed additional financing.
40. The revised analysis finds the Project to be economically viable with an economic internal rate of return (EIRR) of
40 percent and a net present value (NPV) of US$33.3 million at 6 percent discount rate. This is slightly reduced compared
to the original analysis (EIRR of 44 percent) which took into account a mini grid component which was removed at the
beginning of Project implementation. Additionally, it is estimated that a total of 57,790 tons of CO2 emissions will be
avoided through the Project, with the substitution of traditional sources of lighting and cooking fuel with CECs and solar
products. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted and indicates that these results remain sufficiently robust under
significantly higher-cost and lower-benefit scenarios.
41. Paris Agreement: The AF aligns with the Paris Agreement and Burundi’s 2025 Nationally Determined Contributions
(NDC), targeting a 3.04 percent emissions reduction by 2030 (12.61 percent with international support). It supports clean,
renewable energy in rural communities, consistent with low-GHG energy solutions. On adaptation, it aligns with Burundi’s
2023 National Adaptation Plan by promoting off-grid solar electrification of public buildings and clean cooking, enhancing
climate resilience for critical infrastructure and vulnerable populations.

procurement function has made notable progress in understanding World Bank Procurement Procedures, challenges

issues: (a) Strengthen overall contract management by focusing on thorough contract planning, regular monitoring, and

contract manager and a contract management plan for each contract, with systematic mechanisms to track execution,
monitor performance, and ensure amendments remain relevant and aligned with operational needs; (c) Implement
contract-specific plans, conduct regular performance reviews, and coordinate with OEs to ensure timely, well-aligned, and
efficient implementation and; (d) Establish a risk-based oversight approach, prioritizing high-value or high-risk contracts
for closer monitoring and early corrective actions.

Consultant) with a keen focus on contract management and monitoring on 6 high-value contracts.

Burundians, is designed to provide energy services to promote human capital development, enhance productivity of rural
enterprises, and reach poor and vulnerable populations, including women. The project focuses on: (i) electrifying up to
400 schools and 400 health centers with modular solar PV systems and implementing a multi-layered maintenance
approach; (ii) providing 65,000 off-grid solar products to poor and vulnerable households; (iii) distributing 300,000 clean
and efficient cookstoves to households and schools; and (iv) strengthening the enabling environment through improved
policies, regulations, private sector participation, and capacity building for off-grid solar energy and clean cooking
solutions.

These selected sites should supply energy to the community, including public education and health facilities, small
enterprises including agriculture, and households. Considering the pilot nature of the program and the involvement of
private sector in a nascent mini-grid market, larger sized sites (of 400-500 structures) will be selected and implemented.
The project should also address economic constraints to accelerate growth of off-grid solar market and leverage
mechanisms that shall be put in place to provide access to foreign currency for private sector, access to finance for
households and businesses, improve affordability of solar energy services, and encourage productive use of renewable
energy. The project will also support energy and cooking needs of the most vulnerable households, as identified in the
safety nets project (Merankabandi project). It is expected to pilot a voucher program to reach 40,000 extremely poor
households targeted in the Merankabandi project in the provinces of Kirundo, Gitega, Ruyigi, and Karuzi where it is being
implemented. With the support from this proposed project, the program shall help vulnerable households acquire solar
home systems and improve cookstoves to the households – thereby avoiding market distortion. The vouchers will be
provided either to the companies or end-users, which will be further identified during the project preparation. The
proposed project is structured into four components: (i) Stand-alone solar systems for health and education facilities (ii)
Mini-grids for communities (iii) Stand-alone solar systems and efficient cookstoves for households (iv) Technical
assistance and capacity building.

Project activities may cause impacts and risks on environment and natural resources which will occur during the
construction and the maintenance of solar systems, and construction of mini grids. In addition, environmental health
and safety guidelines of the workers on construction sites should be ensured in accordance with ESS1 requirements. As
the project shall involve many workers on multi subprojects across the country, it is important to regularly guide the
private sectors and monitor their activities based on elaborated ESS’s instruments. During the implementation phase, e-
waste could be generated in a neighborhood which is not familiar with it. For instance, these solar PV systems will likely
need maintenance that involves disposing of old batteries and installing new batteries, and the entire system will need
to be disposed at the end of the project life. This should be well addressed in sub-project-specific environmental and
social assessment (ESA) instruments expected to be developed in accordance with ESS1 requirements during project
implementation before any site can be developed.

Social risks are downgraded moderated following the Mid-Term Review Mission, the environmental risk rating was also
revised downward to Moderate for two main reasons: (i) the Recipient's capacity to manage E&S risks and impacts has
improved, evidenced by the current staffing of four E&S specialists; and (ii) certain activities previously identified as
potentially generating substantial risks, specifically the construction and installation of mini-grids, are no longer financed
under the project. The project continues to be implemented in accordance with the Environmental and Social

The key energy institutional stakeholders for this project are: the Ministry of Hydraulic, Energy and Mines (MHEM) and
l’Agence Burundaise de l’Électrification Rurale (ABER). The project will be implemented by the Direction Générale de
l’Energie (under MHEM) and ABER. The regulator (’Autorité de Régulation des Secteurs de l'Eau Potable et de l’Energie
(AREEN)) will benefit from technical assistance support under the project. The project activities shall comply with national
environmental laws, regulations and procedures. The Burundian office for the protection of environment (Office
Burundi's pour la protection de l ’environment (OBPE) is a Government agency that operates under the Ministry of
Environment which regulates environmental impacts assessment. Also, it is expected that private sector entities will be
involved in the implementation of the subprojects under components 1, 2 and 3.

The ESA and ESIA review process is under the responsibility of the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock and Environment
through the Burundian Office for the Protection of Environment (Office Burundais pour la Protection de l’Environnement,
OBPE). The OBPE has the mandate to manage and regulate environmental impacts and risks of any project prepared and
implemented across the country. The office has officers in provinces who always monitor and enforce regulations to
ensure that compliance with project environmental risk/impact of ESIA studies reports, particularly the proposed
mitigation measures to address impacts and risks for a proposed project. OBPE regularly reviews and approves
Environmental Impact Assessment documents for projects and gives an Environmental Compliance Certificate. The
Decree n ° 100/22 of October 07, 2010 implementing the Environmental Code in relation to the Environmental and Social
Impact Assessment procedures in Burundi needs to be updated, because it and has gaps when compared to the WBG’s
ESSs (ESF). OBPE implements its duties through four main phases: (i) validation of the terms of reference in accordance
with OBPE’s Guidelines; (ii) the receipt of the ESIA draft reports submitted to the Ministry of the Environment; (iii) review
and organization of field visits for validating the environmental studies of the project by OBPE; and (iv) environmental
monitoring and evaluation of the project by its Provincial Officer of OBPE.

During the project preparation, the Implementation agencies, involved institutions and the private sector have no prior
experience with the new Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) of the World Bank that guides the preparation and
implementation of the project in accordance with ESSs requirements. However, according to the actions and measures
agreed in the ESCP, the Recipient has set up a Project Implementation Unit (PIU) with qualified staff which includes 4
E&S specialists. These specialists ensure that the project activities are implemented in accordance with the ESCP. In
addition, the E&S staff are also in charge of providing capacity building sessions to the project stakeholders in the ESF
and relevant ESSs.

Summary of Assessment of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
  Environmental and Social risk is currently Moderate. Initially, the environmental risk classification was deemed
  Substantial, whereas the social risk was considered Moderate. The Substantial environmental risk was primarily
  attributed to the Recipient's limited capacity to manage environmental risks and impacts, as well as the nature of
  project activities involving hybrid systems under Component 2. Following the Mid-Term Review Mission, the
  environmental risk rating was revised downward to Moderate for two main reasons: (i) the Recipient's capacity to
  manage E&S risks and impacts has improved, evidenced by the current staffing of four E&S specialists; and (ii) certain
  activities previously identified as potentially generating substantial risks, specifically the construction and installation
  of mini-grids, are no longer financed under the project. The project continues to be implemented in accordance with
  the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP), and no major issues have been reported. The E&S compliance
  performance for the Project is currently ‘Moderately Satisfactory’. All required E&S instruments have been prepared,
  adopted and disclosed in-country and on the WB website. These include Environmental and Social Management
  Framework, Stakeholder Engagement Plan, Labor Management Procedures, Environmental and Social Commitment
  Plan and site-specific Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs). The current Additional Financing (AF) will
  contribute to increase the electricity access rate in Burundi, in line with the M300 initiative and in a sustainable manner.
  The additional financing focuses on leveraging Digitalization at the Digital-Energy Nexus to Strengthen Connectivity,
  Remotely Track Solar Asset Performance in Public Facilities, and Expand Access via targeted RBF in surrounding
  communities. It also includes a restructuring, consisting of: (i) extension of the project closing date, (ii) reallocation of
  budget between categories and (iii) reflecting the adjustment of results framework. The AF project will build on the
  existing Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF), which guides subproject screening, risk
  categorization, and ESMP preparation, review, and disclosure. As the AF is financing project scale up, the existing
  environmental and social instruments would not require any modification. Importantly, the AF is not expected to raise
  the environmental and social risk level of the project or to make relevant a new Environmental and Social Standard
  (ESS).
