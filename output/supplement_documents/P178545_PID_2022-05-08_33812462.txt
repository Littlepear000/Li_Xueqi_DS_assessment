     B. Introduction and Context

     Country Context
 1.      Strong GDP growth rates in Lao People’s Democratic Republic (PDR) have not led to comparable poverty
 reduction gains. The national poverty rate declined by 6.3 percentage points between 2012 and 2018, from 24.6 percent
 to 18.3 percent primarily due to rising farm incomes. Growth has been fueled by the exploitation of natural resources and
 has not been accompanied by significant job creation. Importantly, labor force participation declined from 84 percent to
 72 percent (between 2012-2018) with youth unemployment quadrupling to 21.8 percent during that same period. Female
 labor-force participation dropped by 15.8 percentage points from 81.8 percent in 2012 to 66 percent in 2018.1 Overall,
 growth has been more favorable to the non-poor, with a rise in inequality involving a widening consumption gap within
 regions2.
 2.      The regional distribution of poverty in the country has shifted3. Poverty declined primarily in southern and
 northern regions. By contrast poverty in the central region – previously the wealthiest – declined only slightly. Between
 2012 and 2018 monetary poverty rates in the central region decreased from 23.5 to 21.5 percent. During the same period,
 poverty in the south decreased from 29.9 percent in 2012 to 17.7 percent in 2018 (while the northern region also saw a
 substantial reduction from 31 percent to 20.7 percent.
 3.      The effects of the COVID 19 pandemic are expected to be severe with poverty impacts accelerating and
 unemployment expected to rise further particularly among young people and women. Based on the latest available
 data on the impacts of the COVID-19 lockdowns, increased unemployment among youth is expected to continue. Women
 had been particularly hard hit by unemployment and loss of income. In addition, as of October 2021, over 200,000 migrant
 workers had returned to Laos. Loss of remittances will be severely felt particularly for the poor and vulnerable.
 4.      The profile of the poor in Laos4 highlights the need for dedicated poverty reduction interventions targeting
 priority populations as noted in the World Bank 2020 Poverty Assessment for Laos PDR. Targeted area-based
 interventions are particularly relevant for ethnic minority groups in midland and upland areas in Northern and Central
 Provinces.
 5.      As an autonomous implementing unit, the Poverty Reduction Fund (PRF) has established itself as one of the
 Government of Lao’s main institutions working to reduce rural poverty and deliver infrastructure services in remote
 rural areas. The PRF was established in May 2002 in accordance with the Prime Minister’s Decree (73/PM, 2002).

 6.       The World Bank financed a series of projects in support of the PRF. The first project from 2003–11 aimed to
 deliver basic services using community-based participatory methods with a total commitment of US$35 million (P077320,
 Credit 3675-LA and Grant H4180-LA). The Government’s strong commitment to the approach was confirmed by its
 decision to continue poverty reduction efforts under two more PRF operations. The second PRF project (P123480) was
 implemented from 2011-2016 with a US$53.5 million commitment (US$16.9 million in Trust Fund resources and US$36.6
 million IDA).5 And the third, (PRF III - P157963) is currently under implementation with a closing date of June 2024. Overall
 funding under PRF III totals US$52.5 million. The three World Bank supported PRF projects’ improved access to
 infrastructure for well over a million rural people through implementing more than 6,000 subprojects.
 7.       Building on lessons-learned from the Poverty Reduction Fund (PRF) III, activities under the proposed Community
 Livelihoods Enhancement and Resilience project (CLEAR) will focus on improving connectivity and access to markets to
 improve livelihoods among these groups. This will be key to provide dedicated support to ethnic minority households
 engaged in low-productivity farming in remote areas. With only 64 percent of households in these areas living in villages
 with all-weather roads limited access to services and inadequate access remain key constraints. Poor ethnic minority
 households rely mostly on agricultural income with some groups in northern region having some of the worst nutrition
 outcomes.

 8.       Food insecurity remains a critical issue in Lao PDR, with moderate to severe food insecurity experienced by
 nearly 20 percent of the population in 2018. The prevalence of moderate to severe food insecurity was highest in the
 central region at 25 percent compared to about 20 percent in the northern and southern regions. It is households in the
 northern region, however, that have the lowest nutrient intake and are at highest risk of nutritional deficiencies. This has
 significant repercussions in terms of chronic malnutrition among children. Stunting in Lao PDR remains highest in the
 northern region and among ethnic minorities. Furthermore, food insecurity is predominantly a rural issue with 25 percent
 of rural households being food insecure compared to 11 percent of the urban population in 2018.
 9.      In addition, Lao PDR has experienced increased vulnerability to climate change, environmental degradation,
 and shocks. In particular, the country has significant exposure to flooding (riverine and flash flooding), the frequency
 of which is expected to increase with climate change. Lao PDR’s generally mountainous geography results in a high risk
 of natural disasters and serious impacts on communities and infrastructure from climate change. Increasing rainfall and
 temperatures are altering the Mekong hydrological system, with floods, droughts, storms, and landslides increasing in
 frequency.6 Over the past 10 years, more disaster events have occurred annually and with increased intensity.12 The
 impacts of climate change and natural disasters affect the poor disproportionately and are likely to exacerbate existing
 inequalities. This is likely to be true for: (a) rural areas where three-quarters of the labor force rely on agricultural activities
 as the primary source of livelihood; and (b) urban areas where the urban poor are more vulnerable to natural disasters
 given the precarious housing and infrastructure conditions in urban poor settlements.

     Sectoral and Institutional Context
 Key Government of Lao PDR Policy Instruments
 10.     The Government of Lao PDR (GoL) is addressing these poverty reduction challenges by promoting rural
 development policies and programs that aim to create employment from green jobs and livelihoods that can contribute
 to climate resilience. These are important areas of focus under the 9th National Social and Economic Development Plan
 for 2021-2025 (NSEDP), the 2030 National Green Growth Strategy (NGGS) as well as the Agriculture Development Strategy
 to 2025 and Vision to 2030 (ADS).
 11.      The 9th NSEDP is in line with the Party’s Development Strategy 2025 and Vision 2030 and
 identified six priority outcomes: (a) continuous quality, stable and sustainable economic growth; (b) Improved quality of
 human resources to meet development, research capacity, science and technology needs, and create value-added
 production and services; (c) enhanced well-being of the people; (d) enhanced environmental protection
 and reduced disaster risks; (e) engagement in regional and international cooperation and integration through robust
 infrastructure and effective utilization of national potentials and geographical advantages; and (f) improved public
 governance and administration, and an equal, fair society protected by effective rule of law.
 12.     The 9th NSEDP defines directions for rural development and poverty alleviation, including “smart farming�?, with
 an emphasis on promoting sustainable and climate resilient food production systems; food quality, safety, and
 nutrition; and agricultural commercialization. Complementary measures to achieving improved agricultural productivity
 include upgrading and modernizing technical infrastructure (including agricultural machinery, mills, dryer domes,
 wholesale-retail markets, cold storage among others) to meet international standards. In addition, GoL aims to invest in
 improving the effectiveness of rural advisory services based on the lessons learned from implementation of activities by
 the Poverty Reduction Fund (PRF) and emphasizes the use of Self-Help Groups (SHG) to disseminate agricultural
 knowledge and provide training in entrepreneurship skills for young farmers, especially to women, and to family- and
 community-based enterprises. Critically, poverty alleviation and development efforts in GoL policies will be carried out in
 accordance with the Sam Sang Directive. This directive focuses on the creation of jobs and on the more inclusive

 13.       In addition, the National Green Growth Strategy highlights the role played by the PRF in implementing programs
 focusing on improving rural infrastructure and on the delivery of basic services.7 The Green Growth Strategy emphasizes
 the role of PRF in: (a) developing rural transport and communication infrastructure resilient to natural disasters. The

 is also highlighted by the National Strategy on Disaster Risk Reduction 2021-2030 (NSDRR)8.12
 14.      Finally, the National Action Plan on Nutrition (NPAN) 2021-2025 stresses that segments of the Lao PDR
 population continue to suffer from food insecurity, undernourishment, and limited access to nutritious foods.
 Interventions outlined under the strategy focus on production of diversified foodstuffs, processing and storage, and retail
 marketing of nutrition food. The NPAN also highlights the role played by natural disasters in exacerbating food
 insecurity.910 The impacts of natural disasters affecting rice yields and the loss of livestock are cited by villagers as
 significant causes of poverty.11
 Decentralized service delivery is an important mechanism for poverty reduction
 15.      The “Sam Sang�? directive envisions greater decentralization and local area development by delegating
 responsibilities, functions, and resources to local governments, particularly at the district level . The 2000 Sam Sang
 policy was enhanced in 2008 with measures put in place to develop model development villages and village clusters. A
 2012 Central Party Politburo resolution further specified the roles and responsibilities of Provinces, Districts, and villages
 under a more decentralized administration. Three new laws passed by the National Assembly in 2016, based on lessons
 learned from the piloting of the Sam Sang policy, provide the legal framework that further reinforces decentralization.
 The 2016 Law on Local Administration officially extended implementation of the Sam Sang policy nationwide to all 148
 districts, with local administrations assigned more responsibilities and authority to be more proactive and self-reliant in
 local development and poverty reduction. Evidence suggests, however, that while responsibilities have been delegated to
 lower levels of government, funding decisions remain centralized.
 16.      To guide decentralized planning processes, the Ministry of Planning and Investment (MPI) developed the
 Participatory Planning Manual (PPM) which provides processes and procedures to empower district authorities to
 develop the five year District Socioeconomic Development Plan (DSEDP) based on the Village Development Plans (VDP)
 and the Kum Ban Development Plans (KDP).The District Planning Office, under the supervision of the District Governor, is
 tasked with developing the annual implementation plan which provides investments to address priority issues identified
 in the five year DSEDP. The PPM provides an institutional platform to integrate bottom-up processes with the GoL’s
 planning and budget processes. While significant efforts have been made through the PRF series financed by the WB and
 the Swiss Development Cooperation (SDC) to build the capacity of district level authorities, gaps remain. Further
 investments are required to ensure that villagers are effectively able to present their priorities in terms of basic
 infrastructure and service provision to district authorities.

 17.       An important source of resilience in communities in Lao PDR is the strength of village level institutions. The
 2016 Local Administration Law establishes three levels of local administration: provincial, district, and village. Village
 administration is led by a locally elected village chief, who is assisted by deputy chiefs, and heads of village hamlets.
 National level GoL mass organizations represent Lao civil society in villages through f the Lao Women’s Union, the Lao
 Front for National Development, and the Revolutionary Youth Union. These organizations operate through local citizen
 committees and networks to support and enhance the implementation of priority GoL policies and rural development
 activities. The community platform and village institutions strengthened by the PRF series are seen by the GoL and
 Development Partners as an effective mechanism to deliver inclusive local investments and rural development programs.
 Traditionally, however, participation in local institutions tends to be dominated by older men, with limited avenues for
 youth and women to engage in local decision making. Furthermore, ethnic minority groups in Lao PDR face several barriers
 in engaging with the local administration and face a significant risk of being excluded from rural development programs.
 This exclusion contributes to a welfare gap between ethnic groups and the majority that can be attributed to differences
 in education levels, land ownership, income sources, access to electricity and economic opportunities. Many development
 plans, including infrastructure, are not planned, or implemented considering the specific vulnerability of ethnic minority
 groups. Consequently, development impacts can contribute to the marginalization of isolated ethnic groups unless
 sufficient mitigation measures are adopted, and potential beneficiaries are consulted in project planning and
 implementation. Importantly, the PRF has created and trained a pool of community volunteers and mobilizers that are a
 critical resource in supporting villagers to identify community needs in an inclusive manner. The community mobilization
 and prioritization process put in place by PRF has been effective in increasing the opportunities for ethnic minorities,
 women, and youth to benefit from local poverty reduction initiatives.

  Relationship to CPF
 18.      The proposed operation is in line with the approach outlined in the forthcoming Country Partnership
 Framework (CPF; FY2022-2026) of concentrating efforts in areas of demonstrated government commitment. In
 particular, the proposed operation is aligned with the CPF’s second and third High-Level Outcomes (HLO): (a) To improve
 labor incomes of vulnerable households; and (b) To strengthen sustainable livelihoods through the better management
 of natural resources. Under this overall umbrella, the proposed operation would contribute directly to Objective 5 –
 Expanded use among women and children of practices and services beneficial to nutrition and Objective 9 – More
 productive and climate adapted agriculture. Considering the persistent development challenges and governance and
 institutional weaknesses, the proposed operation: (a) builds on a program with a long track record of successful
 implementing, (b) limits complex inter-agency coordination efforts; and (c) draws on impact evaluation data collected in
 earlier stages of implementation to help adjust/strengthen approaches.

      Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
  .
 20.     Overall, the project is expected to have positive environmental and social impacts by improving the livelihood
 opportunities and nutritional outcomes for poor communities in selected Provinces to enhance their resilience to shocks.
 The activities would build on the current ongoing PRFIII-AF and focus the poorest areas in the northern and central regions
 of Lao PDR based on the recent poverty assessment and migration including those most impacted by COVID-19, and those
 most likely to be affected by climate change. The project would channel its block grants in a subproject approach to
 targeted villages to improve access to productive infrastructure and income generating opportunities. The project will
 support construction or rehabilitation of basic and climate resilient infrastructure and support to livelihood activities.
 Implementation of the CLEAR subprojects will be directly managed at the village level and the project will therefore build
 the capacity of local communities to plan, implement, and sustain these sub-projects.
 21.      Experience from similar set of activities and implementation arrangement financed under PRFIII suggested that
 these type of small-scale infrastructure works, and livelihood support activities can induce negative environmental and
 social risks and impacts. The project recognizes that 8 out of 10 Environmental and Social Standards (ESS 1, ESS 2, ESS 3,
 ESS 4, ESS 5, ESS 6, ESS 7 and ESS 10)13 are assessed to be relevant to the project.
 22.      The social risk is classified as Moderate. Whilst the project aims to deliver a range of benefits, project activities
 have the potential to generate moderate, predictable, mitigatable social risks and impacts, that are low in magnitude.
 Social risks and impacts anticipated for this project include: (a) lack of stakeholder engagement; (b) risk of exclusion and
 discrimination particularly of ethnic minorities and vulnerable groups if not adequately represented in project activities;
 (c) risk of elite capture, (d) risks related to the labor and working conditions of project workers; (e) small scale land
 acquisition and/or access restriction, (f) risk of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse and Sexual Harassment (SEA/SH) and (g)
 child labor. These risks and impacts are for the most part predicable and possible to mitigate during the lifetime of the
 project.

 23. Environmental risk is classified as Moderate. The direct environmental risk and impact are expected from (1)
 physical construction and renovation civil works (noise, vibration, dust, generation and management of construction
 wastes); (2) occupational health and safety issue of project personnel who are implementing project’s activities (injured
 from falling subject and other construction activities, road safety, and transmitted and infected with COVID-19); (3)
 potential involvement of project staff in consuming and trading wildlife and other NTFP while working and staying in
 remote village areas; (4) Community Health and Safety (CHS) of those involve in project activities and accident caused by
 project vehicle; (5) exposure to an unexploded ordinance (UXO) risk. In addition, indirect risk and impact are expected
 from (1) generation and management of solid waste during the operation of office facilities (including minor electronic
 waste from the used IT equipment procured by the project), (2) access to and extraction of natural resources by road users
 while using tertiary road supported by the project (including expansion of forestland, involving with illegal hunting and
 logging), (3) improper/unsafe use of pesticides, fertilizers for agricultural intensification and irrigation related subproject,
 (4) no protection of water sources for future water supply, (5) unsafe used of water tap built by the project which may
 lead the bad environment and illness in the village, and (6) potential fire at storage or processing facilities built for
 horticultural products and animal husbandry, solar driers supported by the project and other nearby facilities. However,
 these potential environmental impacts will be minor, localized, temporary and can be mitigated through the design and
 implementation of subprojects and environmental and social mitigation measures in compliance with ESF requirements.

 24. Prior to appraisal, the client with the support from consultants, will develop, consult, and disclose an Environmental
 and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP), ESMF which will include guideline and principle for development and implementation
 of E&S mitigation measures to ensure that project activities are compliance with ESF requirements. This will include (a)
 Resettlement Policy Framework (RPF), (b) Labor Management Procedures (LMP) and (c) a negative list of activities not
 eligible for financing, The project will have to ensure adequate budget and staff for project E&S risk management,
 monitoring and reporting include a training which will be informed by the capacity needs assessment. All ESF related
 activities to be funded by the Client as part of the project budget.

 25. The project is expected to involve different groups of stakeholders from national to village levels, including local
 communities, government line agencies, mass organizations and the private sector. The project’s stakeholders and the level
 of their engagement will be identified and analyzed by the PRF during project preparation and a Stakeholder Engagement
 Plan (SEP), including a Grievance Redress Mechanism (GRM) will be prepared. Requirements of ESS7 on meaningfully
 involving ethnic groups in project activities will be included in the SEP and not in a standalone document
 An Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP), drawn and agreed between the Bank and the Borrower, will set out
 the important measures and actions that will be required for the project to meet environmental and social requirements
 over the project's lifetime. These measures will be implemented within a specified timeframe and the status of
 implementation will be reviewed as part of project monitoring and reporting.

 26. During the implementation stage, site specific E&S screening will be conducted following the screening procedure
 outlined which will be developed in the ESMF to identify environmental and social risk and impact, and mitigation measures
 for each proposed sub-project.

 27. For the preparation of the ESF instruments, the client will engage external consultants to support the PRF with the
 preparation of the required ESF instruments during project preparation. These will be financed from the Project Preparation
 Advance which is being prepared.
