    B. Introduction and Context

 Country Context
1. Angola is characterized by limited economic diversification and declining labor productivity. As the fifth-largest
   economy in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and the second-largest oil producer, Angola's growth was sustained by oil
   revenue until recent years, resulting in volatile growth and limited economic diversification.1 From 2009-2019, over
   350,000 net jobs were created annually, driven by an expansion in the working population, rather than by productivity
   (output per capita) increases.2 Since 2002, Angola's economy has shifted towards services, consumption, and
   construction sectors. However, most jobs remain in agriculture (about 46 percent of employed), followed by services,
   and the public sector. Limited structural transformation and low-productivity jobs limit Angola’s prospects for
   increasing earnings and job quality, thereby reducing its opportunities for sustained and inclusive economic growth.

2. Although Angola is a lower middle-income country (LMIC), its poverty and human capital indicators resemble those
   of low-income countries (LICs). In 2018, 31.1 percent of Angolans were "extremely poor," (i.e., below the international
   poverty line US$2.15/day) compared to 11.3 percent in LMICs and 43.9 percent in LICs.3 Poor households are often
   larger, more likely headed by females, and have more young members than wealthier households. Angola’s has a Gini
   coefficient of 0.51, one of the highest globally.4 In addition, Angola’s Human Capital Index (HCI) of 0.36 is among the
   lowest in SSA (below SSA, and LMIC averages of 0.40 and 0.48). Girls HCI is lower than boys (0.36 vs 0.37) driven by
   poor education outcomes.5 This hinders Angola’s future productivity and growth, as a stronger human capital is crucial
   for sustainable and inclusive development.

3. Angola’s high vulnerability to climate change also poses a risk to its sustainable growth and human capital
   development. Angola is ranked as the 38th most vulnerable country to climate change with poor readiness to adapt.6
   Alternating floods and droughts significantly impact Angola’s economy and human capital development.7 Rising
   temperatures and more frequent extreme floods and droughts are expected, especially in Angola’s largest cities,8

4. Global trends and regional investments, such as in Angola’s Lobito corridor,11 could enhance growth, employment,
   and human capital development, but remain unexplored. Adoption of digital technologies and transition to low
   carbon (green) economy are transforming the world of work. Online gig jobs,12 engaging up to 12 percent of the global
   labor force, offer income and digital skills development opportunities for vulnerable youth, especially in developing
   countries like Angola.13 With Angola’s mobile penetration rising from 49 percent in 2020 to 67 percent by 202214,
   urban areas with higher mobile and internet access stand to benefit, enabling youth to explore new work
   opportunities. Angola, rich in mineral resources, expects increased demand for green minerals and jobs due to global
   energy transitions,15 potentially boosting economic growth and employment if managed well. Investments in
   extractives and the Lobito corridor, critical for transporting strategic minerals, are expected to bring economic and
   social benefits through upstream linkages and demand from local and international industries. Environmental
   management and waste treatment also offer sustainable youth employment opportunities that enhance climate
   resilience and mitigation efforts.
     Sectoral and Institutional Context

5. Angola saw modest job creation over the last decade, with 3.5 million new jobs created between 2009 and 2019,
   although most were of low quality, offering minimal contribution to economic growth and worker welfare. More
   than 72 percent of jobs were created in the non-wage sector, mainly by low-productivity micro-businesses.16 Most
   new jobs were unskilled positions in agriculture and commerce, offering low wages, poor learning opportunities, and
   no social benefits, with women overrepresented in these sectors. Despite high participation and employment rates
   (76 and 85 percent respectively in 2018)17, most workers are in low productivity jobs, with over half in subsistence
   agriculture or self-employment. The extractives sector, despite being highly productive, contributed minimally to
   employment creation, representing only 0.8 percent of those employed.

6. Youth employment is a pressing challenge, driven by a rapidly growing young population not adequately absorbed
   in productive employment Youth, aged 15-34,18 constitute 30 percent of the total population19 and comprise over
   half of the labor force. About 600,000 youth enter the labor force annually, many leaving school early in search for
   economic opportunities. The youth unemployment rate is 50 percent higher than the national average (22 vs. 15
   percent), with youth representing 83 percent of the total unemployed in Angola. Those employed are more likely to
   work in precarious jobs, with only 9 percent in high-quality jobs compared to 15 percent of adults.20

7. Angolan youth face multiple constraints, with most classified as vulnerable and not prepared for the labor market.
   Youth can be classified based on their school-to-work transition and labor market barriers. Only four percent of the
   youth are "low vulnerable" and have followed the expected steps in education and employment (they are full time
   students or have good quality jobs). On the other hand, 20 percent are "highly vulnerable" and are mostly “Not in
   Education, Employment, or Training (NEETs)�?, mainly in rural areas. The remaining 76 percent are "vulnerable" and
   have poor quality jobs or are too old for their grade level, mostly living in urban areas.21 Demand-side constraints,
   such as macroeconomic conditions, firm-specific constraints, and entrepreneurship challenges, mostly affect urban
   youth depending on formal markets. All youth face supply-side factors, such as lack of relevant skills, work experience,
   social barriers, information gaps, and spatial mismatches, that prevent them from being ready for quality jobs.

8. Young women and persons with disabilities face more challenges in the labor market. Despite progress, only 51
   percent of young females have some secondary education, compared to 64 percent of young males. 22 Female youth
   participate less in the labor force than males (67 vs. 71 percent) and are more likely to work in low-quality jobs than
   males (92 vs. 78 percent), and in less profitable sectors.23 Only 26 percent of women hold management positions in
   private firms.24 Restrictive social norms and traditional gender roles discourage women from pursuing male-
   dominated fields of study, and occupations.25 Early marriage and early childbearing limit young women's access to
   education and work, increasing their household responsibilities and disempowering them.26 Persons with disabilities
   face barriers such as information gaps, negative attitudes, stigma, inaccessible physical environment, low education
   levels, and ineffective legislation. Those who find work, often work part-time, are paid less and rarely promoted.27 In
   2014, 2.5 percent of the population had disabilities, mainly due to prolonged armed conflicts,28 highlighting the urgent
   need for disability-sensitive training and explore digital entrepreneurship to improve their opportunities. 29

9. Youth employment is a top priority for the government of Angola (GoA), emphasized in its Long-Term Development
   Strategy (LTDS) 2025, the National Development Plan (NDP) (2023-2027), and the National Agenda for
   Employment.30 These strategies aim to ensure productive employment for all Angolans and promote job creation
   across key sectors like transportation, agriculture, and manufacturing. The NDP also prioritizes digitization to create
   new work opportunities, fostering the development of youth skills and learning methods.

    training centers, promoting employment programs, and intermediation services. MAPTSS, along with the Ministry of
    Economy and Planning (MEP), implements job creation programs. Vocational and non-formal professional training is

11. Despite government prioritization of youth employment, its policies are inadequately designed for its large,
    vulnerable youth population. While spending on active labor market programs (ALMPs) is significant, ALMPs in Angola
    have restrictive eligibility criteria, limited scale, and overlapping objectives, resulting in inefficient fund allocation and
    limited reach among vulnerable youth.32 Angola also lacks robust information and monitoring and evaluation systems
    for labor programs, limiting its ability to assess their effectiveness. The NDP 2023-2027 prioritizes the Employment
    Market Observatory, under MAPTSS, to produce and analyze labor market, employment, and training statistics, which
    is yet to be operationalized.

12. In this context, the GoA has requested World Bank support to improve youth’s access to employment opportunities
    and productivity. Addressing the youth employment challenge in Angola requires a multi-dimensional approach
    combining policies with impacts in the short- term and those with impacts materializing in the long-term.
    Complementing government’s longer term strategic investments, the proposed project will focus on interventions to
    promote opportunities for vulnerable youth already in the labor market to increase their productivity, income, and
    experience. This will provide a basis for accessing better opportunities over time through innovative approaches.
    Strengthening the institutional capacity will also be critical to ensure impact at scale and sustainability.


 Relationship to CPF

    and several Global Challenge Programs (GCPs). Specifically, it aligns with the new CPF under preparation and Angola’s
    NDP 2023-2027, both focusing on human capital and economic diversification, by building pathways to productivity

    boost shared prosperity on a livable planet, and the institution’s evolution roadmap, contributing to several GCPs:
    Food and Nutrition Security and (through livelihood opportunities) and Accelerating Digitization (building digital skills
    for jobs).

 D. Concept Description

14. The Project will support vulnerable youth through a multi-faceted approach addressing both supply and demand
    sides constraints, alongside strengthening the institutional capacity to support youth employment. The Project will
    incorporate digital approaches to foster innovation, enhance productivity, promote green jobs and entrepreneurship,
    and provide youth with remote work opportunities. While the project has national scope, regional interventions will
    complement and leverage government and World Bank efforts for economic transformation, diversification and
    digitalization, especially in the Lobito economic corridor, to maximize impact.

15. The project is structured around three main components:

16. Component 1: Improving the productivity of vulnerable young entrepreneurs. This component aims to provide
    better economic opportunities through entrepreneurship development and address key constraints and market
    failures that limit the participation of vulnerable youth in productive entrepreneurship. It will support the adoption of
    digital tools and digital skills to enhance entrepreneurship. In rural areas, it will explore ways of integrating youth into
    agricultural value chains by improving their capacity and productivity to respond to the requirements of end markets.
    It will seek to complement interventions offered by “Diversifica Mais�?: youth benefiting from this component and
    grow into small and medium-sized enterprises (SME) may qualify for business support, and for young entrepreneurs
    supported by this component who may be able to participate in upstream and consumption linkages.

17. Component 2: Supporting transition of vulnerable youth to earning opportunities. This component targets
    vulnerable youth who come out of schools and training centers frequently lacking the relevant work experience and
    competencies, including behavioral skills, needed for employment. It aims to enhance employability and support
    access to first work experience, including in digital jobs. It will provide technical and soft skills training, job search
    assistance, and mentoring. Based on the digital skills strategy that will be developed by the Institute for Administrative
    Modernization (IMA), (supported by PADA) this component will support the implementation of a new digital skills
    program that meets market needs and empower youth for the digital economy. It will also support apprenticeships
    and work experience, with a focus on young women and youth with disabilities. In the Lobito corridor, the project will
    identify youth who need training, work experience, or certification to access opportunities generated by investments
    in the area.

18. Component 3: Strengthening Institutional and Implementation Capacity, Monitoring and Evaluation. This
    component will support the modernization of INEFOP's systems, standardization of processes, provision of training
    and technical assistance, establishment of a women's leadership academy, and the operationalization of the
    Employment Market Observatory, alongside the creation of a research and innovation laboratory. This component
    aims to support MAPTSS, INEFOP in the design, implementation, and evaluation of employment-related policies and
    programs, including through an impact evaluation of selected project intervention(s), as well as effective project
    management. INEFOP’s capacity will be strengthened through a technical support team to enhance their operational
