 B. Introduction and Context

 Country Context
1. Brazil’s economy is recovering from a decade of sluggish growth and a recent economic recession deepened by the
   COVID-19 pandemic. Poverty and inequality remain significant challenges. In 2023, growth is expected to have
   reached 2.9 percent, after having achieved the rates of 4.8 percent in 2021 and 3.0 percent in 2022, driven by
   agriculture and consumption, and supported by fiscal stimulus, increased public spending, and transfers to low-income
   households. A large share of the population continues to live in poverty, at 21.3 percent or 43 million people1i
   (US$6.85 per day), linked to improvements in economic conditions, labor market – with unemployment down to 7.4
   percent – and social protection policies. Brazil ranks ninth globally in terms of inequality level2, and access to services
   and opportunities vary sharply across subregions and population segments. On average, rural areas are twice as poor
   as urban areas. Women and minorities, especially Afro-descendants and indigenous populations, have the lowest
   rates of access to job opportunities and lower wages on average, and poverty rates among them are the highest.
   According to IBGE, in Santa Catarina the overall unemployment rate is 50% higher for women in relation to the
   percentage of unemployed men, being the majority of the former afro-descendants.

2. Brazil faces major climate change challenges, exacerbated by deforestation and land degradation. Climate change
   is altering temperature and rainfall patterns, potentially pushing 800,000 to 3 million Brazilians into extreme poverty
   by 2030. Deforestation in the Amazon and Cerrado biomes increases land-use emissions, the main source of Brazil's
   greenhouse gas emissions. Regarding Santa Catarina State (SC) more particularly, the effects of climate change are
   exacerbated. SC is one of the main areas negatively affected by Climate Change, with devastating consequences for
   the road sector..

3. Santa Catarina, located in the southernmost part of the Mata Atlantic Forest, is predicted to face significant climatic
   changes by 2040. Average summer temperatures are expected to increase by up to one degree Celsius, and summer
   precipitation is expected to increase by 10 percent. This change will likely exacerbate natural phenomena in the region,
   which have already increased in frequency. In 2022 alone, the state experienced 874 natural phenomena, resulting in
   13 fatalities, impacting 25 percent of the population, and causing around 3.5 billion reais in damages, predominantly
   from increasingly frequent flash floods.

4. The state's vulnerability to extreme weather events has manifested in numerous disasters over the years, leading
   to substantial economic and human costs. From 2002 to 2012, there were 1,108 incidents of flash floods, floods, and
   landslides, accounting for 11 percent of the nation's similar occurrences in the same period. The costs of these
   disasters are staggering, with material losses amounting to billions of reais. A detailed breakdown shows that flash
   floods, droughts, windstorms, and floods have collectively affected millions, emphasizing the urgent need for
   enhanced preventive measures and infrastructure resilience.
5. According to the Atlas Digital de Desastres no Brasil, natural disasters have caused damages amounting to R$ 2.6
   billion in Santa Catarina's industry over the past 32 years. This figure represents 24 percent of the total impact on
   the national sector, making Santa Catarina the most affected state by natural disasters in Brazil. Furthermore, over
   the past 32 years, Santa Catarina recorded 6,560 incidents, resulting in 311 deaths and 1.1 million displaced people.
   Notably, 46 percent of these incidents were reported in the last decade. Beyond industrial impacts, the state incurred
   additional losses amounting to R$ 32.5 billion, ranking it as the sixth state with the highest recorded losses, behind
   Rio Grande do Sul (R$ 92 billion), Minas Gerais (R$ 52 billion), Bahia (R$ 42 billion), Paraná (R$ 40 billion), and
   Pernambuco (R$ 33 billion)

 Sectoral and Institutional Context

6. The transport services and logistics performance in Brazil are hindering the country’s ability to compete globally
   (Custo Brasil). Brazil's overwhelming reliance on road transport, which accounts for over 65 percent of logistics,
   combined with inadequate management of road networks, leads to poor road conditions and high vehicle operation
   costs. Consequently, Brazil performs below its peers in logistics competitiveness, ranking 51st in the 2023 Logistics
   Performance Index (LPI) and 56th in 2018, compared to China’s 19th and India’s 38th. According to the Infrastructure
   Department of Santa Catarina (SC), the road system is the primary mode of transportation in Santa Catarina, similar
   to the rest of the country, accounting for approximately 80 percent of all cargo transported within the state.

7. Investment in infrastructure and maintenance has been neglected by successive governments for years. While the
   country has developed sound concession programs to expand and manage part of its network, these have been limited
   to roads with the highest traffic warranting the implementation of tolls. The maintenance of roads with lower traffic
   levels has remained subject to unsustainable financing, insufficient to cover the costs of maintaining existing assets,
   let alone the annual depreciation of new investments needed to bridge the infrastructure gap. To achieve the
   Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), Brazil will need to invest USD 778 billion by 2030, with 53 percent allocated
   to transport. In recent years, erratic policies and financing for infrastructure maintenance have led to the overall poor
   condition of road networks. According to the Brazilian National Transportation Confederation (Confederação Nacional

8. Transport is one of the main sources of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the energy sector in Brazil and is the
   fastest growing. According to the 5th National Communication on Climate Change in December 2020, the transport

   The limited share of paved network (12.4 percent3 in 2016) is a compounding factor: traffic on inappropriate roads
   wastes an average of 4 to 5 percent of total diesel volume, according to studies4.

B.1. Sectorial and Institutional Context of Santa Catarina:

9. The road network in Santa Catarina consists of 6,289.2 kilometers of state highways, of which 80 percent are single-
   lane paved roads, primarily asphalted, and 11.8 percent are unpaved roads. The municipal road system adds another
   66,607 kilometers, with only a small portion paved. Despite the vital role of these highways in freight transport,
   approximately 74 percent of the paved roads are in poor to mediocre condition. Historically, various methodologies
   have been used to address the deterioration of state road assets, including direct administration, outsourced
   maintenance in separate contracts, and outsourced maintenance in integrated contracts, each with its pros and cons.
   Despite recent state investments for the expansion and improvement of various road segments under state
   jurisdiction, using both state resources and funding from CAF, Banco do Brasil, BNDES, and others, the current
   condition in 2023 remains poor, with only about 26 percent in excellent or good condition and 74 percent in regular,
   poor, or very poor condition.

10. The diagnosis clearly shows that road conservation policies have not achieved the desired efficiency and urgently
    need revision due to the lack of continuous financial resources. There is a need for an institutional change within the
    policies of investment in road infrastructure to break away from the reactive paradigm, particularly given the impact
    of climate changes. This includes the use of public-private partnerships, which have never been utilized by the state.
    Despite SC's institutional capacity and fiscal space, the state has not engaged in any road PPPs to date, making it an
    outlier in the country compared with peer states from the south and southeast regions, as well as with the federal
    government. The only existing concession in the state is for a federal road. In this context, the MPA will also help build
    capacity in the state regarding private sector participation in infrastructure and promote pivotal and innovative
    changes in this regard.

11. Santa Catarina is the most affected state by climate change in the country, with increasing frequency and severity
    of rain and windstorms. The western part of the state, notably susceptible to tornadoes due to its geographical and
    topographical features, has recorded over 30 tornado incidents in two decades, affecting over 70,000 residents,
    mainly around Chapecó. Furthermore, floods in 2008 due to heavy rainfall resulted in 128 deaths and 78,700 displaced
    individuals, and the occurrence of a 'cyclone bomb' in 2020 wreaked havoc across 204 cities, highlighting the

12. Due to the drastic impacts of climate change in Santa Catarina (SC) and its effect on road infrastructure, the state

    and incorporates climate risks. The Geological Service of Brazil is actively developing Susceptibility Maps for these

    prioritize investments in drainage maintenance, containment systems, and road improvements, leveraging ongoing
    data collection and analysis to mitigate the escalating risks associated with climate change, ensuring safer and more
    reliable transportation networks.

15. Lack of access to opportunities exacerbates social exclusion and poverty rates in Brazil. Although considerable
    progress has been made in closing gender gaps in critical sectors such as human capital, significant employment
    discrepancies remain, particularly in the construction sector. The Brazilian labor market is marked by major gender
    disparities, with males constituting a larger proportion of the labor force—on average, 20 percentage points more in
    recent years—and working more often than women. This disparity is especially pronounced in construction, where
    men are overwhelmingly present compared to women, further promoting discrimination and keeping women
    concentrated in lower-paying industries and occupations, resulting in occupational segregation.

16. Women and minorities in Santa Catarina have the lowest rates of access to job opportunities and the highest rates
    of poverty. In the state of Santa Catarina, women earn 29.4% less than men, according to the first Gender Pay
    Transparency Report published by the Ministries of Labor and Employment (MTE) and Women's Affairs in March 2024.
    The report also highlights racial disparities, noting that the number of black women is significantly lower than that of
    non-black women in the surveyed companies, with 68,100 and 322,100 respectively. Additionally, black women earn,
    on average, 23.1% less than non-black women. Among men, the wage gap is even larger, with black men earning
    24.8% less than non-black men.

17. Creating job alternatives through the project in partnership with Santa Catarina’s Department of Women may
    have a significant impact on reducing gender gaps in the transport sector in the state. By promoting equal
    employment opportunities and addressing wage disparities, such initiatives can help mitigate social exclusion and
    poverty rates among women and minorities, fostering a more inclusive and equitable labor market.



 Relationship to CPF

18. The Project will be fully consistent with the current Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Brazil (FY18 –FY23)
    (Report No. 113259-BR) and the upcoming CPF (FY2023-FY2028). It will be aligned to CPF’s three focus areas: i.
    Promoting fiscal sustainability and enhancing the investment climate – by promoting fiscal gains of more efficient
    long-term CREMA contracts, including the expansion of the Government-pays PPP model to roads; ii. Enhancing
    productivity and innovation for competitiveness – by ensuring both private and public sector lead the way in the
    change of paradigm to delegate the management of road management to construction/concession companies; iii.
    Building resilience to environmental and social (E&S) shocks and improving social inclusion – by enhancing road
    resilience and safety for users and preventing unforeseen shocks such as road closures that create large economic
    disruptions and saving thousands of lives along the project timeline.

19. The Project is aligned with the Paris Agreement. On mitigation, the proposed activities are either Universally
    Aligned (UA) or considered of low or significant risk: road upgrading with longer-periods of maintenance with minor
    enhancement of capacity for resilience, road safety, and accessibility purposes are considered UA. On adaptation,

 D. Concept Description

20. The third phase of the MPA consists of a proposed project in the State of Santa Catarina for a total of $375
    million, from which $300 million is IBRD financing and $75 million counterparty financing .

21. The Project Development Objective (PDO) of the third operation is to “improve sustainable, safe, and resilient
    transport access to socio-economic opportunities for the targeted population of selected areas in the State of
    Santa Catarina�?. The third phase has four components:

    21.1.        Component 1: Proactive maintenance based on a long-term (8–25 years) CREMA model using PBCs:.
        21.1.1. Subcomponent 1.1: Proactive maintenance CREMA-DBM: Long-term performance-based safe and
              resilient road management contracts for state road corridors, (estimated cost US$121.5 million; US$82.75
              million in IBRD financing, US$26.25 million in counterpart financing, and US$12.5 million from PCM).
        1.1.1.Subcomponent 1.2: CREMA-PPP: Structuring of long-term (over 20 years) safe and resilient CREMAs using
              the PPP regulation for selected state roads, (estimated cost US$5 million, covered by counterpart
              financing).

    21.2.       Component 2: institutional strengthening for sustainability of road asset management, road safety,
         climate resilience, and decarbonization:
        21.2.1. Road Network Sustainability: This will involve both counterpart funding (survey/registration, basic
              design) and financing (multi-year maintenance management). Activities include road network survey and
              multi-year management.
        21.2.2. Road Safety: Support will be provided to the institution responsible for road safety in the state, which
              will be identified (and/or proposed in the Institutional Capacity Review).

22. During the preparation mission in May 2024, it was decided that the program will adopt a network approach
    towards the selection of roads that will benefit from the program. This approach will allow for better management
    of the state road network in Santa Catarina and enable phased implementation, presenting advantages over
    independent corridor selection. The program anticipates a progressive extension to the entire state road network.
    The State Infrastructure Secretariat (SIE) is committed to defining the regions that will benefit from the program and
    will strive, as much as possible, to align the selection of regions with the criteria previously chosen for corridor
    selection, namely: (i) Human Development Index (HDI); (ii) climate resilience; (iii) road safety; (iv) road quality; and (v)
    Annual Average Daily Traffic (AADT). Please refer to Annex 2 for the map of the roads selected.

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts


Environmental risk is moderate. The environmental moderate risk ratting accounts for the potential impacts associated
with road rehabilitation activities (CREMA rehabilitation Component 1), which may include: production and disposal of
construction waste; water contamination due to construction runoff, wastewater from construction sites, and soil or
groundwater contamination from asphalt mill waste; soil and air contamination from asphalt plants; noise pollution, dust
emission and soil manipulation from quarries, construction work sites and traffic risks to workers and communities; and
occupational health and safety in construction workplaces. Road rehabilitation works will be restricted to the ROWs,
therefore no loss of habitats is expected for this project. On the other hand, the Borrower's ability to manage the possible
risks and environmental impacts generated by the planned works seems satisfactory. Within SIE, the socio-environmental
management is conducted by the Environmental Advisory (ASMAM/SIE), from the design phase to road operations.
Social risk is moderate. The Project is expected to have an overall positive impact on the local community, improving
sustainable, safe, and resilient road transport connectivity. Minimum negative impact is expected under Component 1 as
it will involve road maintenance works. Although unlikely, some maintenance works may require a limited taking of land,
occurring mainly on land classified as land not intended for construction. These small parcels of land (easement areas,
strip of land) may be needed to improve road safety and build new accesses. The designs will be prepared following
mitigation hierarchy criteria to avoid alternatives with significant adverse impacts, such as physical displacement and
losses of livelihood. Indigenous peoples will not be affected by the project as they are not present within the direct areas
Project’s interventions. Construction activities may also induce potential concerns such as safety, accidents, and road
safety, especially during the operation of the roads. To mitigate these risks, specific road safety studies will be carried out
and implementing a traffic management plan to minimize potential impacts to road users and communities located in
surrounding areas. The works are likely to be implemented by local firms and local labor, with low likelihood of labor
influx. There is also a potential for excluding vulnerable groups from benefits offered by the project, (vulnerable to
extreme weather events and natural disasters, particularly family farmers). To address these risks, the project will carry
out mapping of relevant stakeholders, including government entities and NGOs, to support the engagement process. The
impacts that the Project may have on women will also be considered and activities to promote gender equality and address
GBV will be included under component 2.
