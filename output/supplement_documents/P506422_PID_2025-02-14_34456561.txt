B. Introduction and Context

Country Context

 1. The Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) and the Federated States of Micronesia (FSM) are defined by their
    extreme remoteness, small size and geographic dispersion. RMI consists of 29 coral atolls and five isolated islands
    in the Western and Central Pacific Ocean (WCPO), with a total land area of 181 square kilometers (km²) and an
    Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) of just under 2 million km², making it the 19th largest EEZ in the world. FSM is an even
    more widely dispersed, and overall larger, archipelagic nation in the WCPO, comprising over 600 islands stretching
    2,700 km² from West to East, and organized into four semi-autonomous states: Pohnpei, Chuuk, Yap, and Kosrae.
    FSM's total land area is 702 km² and its EEZ is the 14th largest in the world, covering approximately 3 million km²,
    including some of the most productive tuna fishing grounds in the WCPO. The geographic dispersion and remoteness
    results in the high costs of public administration and the uneven availability of quality public services.

 2. Both countries are classified as fragile countries due to limited state capacity, and exposure to natural disasters
    and climate change. The fragility in RMI and FSM arise from their extreme geography and limited economic viability,
    thin institutional capacity, urbanization and youth unemployment, poor land governance, and gender-based
    violence. RMI also has high levels of Gender-Based Violence (GBV), straining social stability. FSM faces additional
    challenges relating to its particularly thin institutional capacity. There is also high exposure to frequent and
    damaging natural hazards however both islands have low capacity to manage the ensuing risks. Vulnerability is
    exacerbated by the lack of technical capacity for adequate disaster preparedness and mitigation, and by climate
    change, which is predicted to amplify the magnitude of cyclones, droughts, and flooding. Addressing these
    challenges is therefore essential for building resilience and supporting sustainable development in both countries.

 3. RMI and FSM populations are declining because of relatively high rates of migration to the USA. Both countries
    have a Compact of Free Association with the USA that provides annual financial transfers. The Compact has also,
    since independence in 1986, offered RMI and FSM open migration to the USA as well as financial support and access
    to various US federal services and programs. This open access to the USA contributes to relatively high migration
    and depopulation. RMI's population is estimated at 41,996 as of 2023, concentrated mainly in the capital, Majuro,
    and the island of Ebeye. This reflects a notable decline from 51,352 in 2013. The population of FSM is estimated at
    about 115,224 in 2023 and is expected to have declined based on the latest Census. The declining population in
    both countries reflect an important demographic trend, which has exacerbated the capacity constraints.

     4. RMI and FSM face significant economic challenges characterized by low growth rates, external vulnerabilities,
        and dependence on foreign aid. FSM is a lower middle-income country with a Gross National Income (GNI) of
        US$4,250 per capita in 2023 and RMI is an upper middle-income country with a GNI of US$7,860 per capita in 2023.
        Over the past 15 years, RMI's real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) has grown at an average rate of 1.8 percent per
        year, a modest pace by global standards and marked by significant volatility. FSM, however, faces even more
        pronounced economic challenges, with an average annual growth rate of just 0.6 percent over the same period.
        FSM’s economy stagnated during 2022 and 2023, with no recorded growth, but is expected to recover modestly to
        1.1 percent in 2024, driven by an increase in public wages and the resumption of infrastructure projects partly
        benefitting from new Compact agreements with the USA (see further below). Both Pacific Island Countries (PICs)
        remain highly vulnerable to external shocks, such as global commodity price fluctuations, labor shortages due to
        outmigration, and climate-related disasters.


    Sectoral and Institutional Context

     5. Public sector capacity in PICs is stretched thin. RMI and FSM score below 3.0 on the overall Country Policy and
        Institutional Assessment (CPIA) index and are consistently ranked below the other PICs. They also score below 3.0
        on average for indicators measuring the quality of public sector management and institutions. Scores are particularly
        low in RMI and FSM for dimensions of particular relevance for countries’ ability to prepare and implement projects,
        namely: (i) Quality of Public Administration, which measures the extent to which the structure of civilian central
        government staff supports the effective design and implementation of government policy and delivery of services;
        and (ii) Quality of Budgetary and Financial Management, which measures, in part, the extent to which there are
        effective financial management systems in place and timely and accurate accounting and fiscal reporting, including
        timely and audited public accounts.

     6. The public sector faces competition for human resources from the local private sector, development partners, as
        well as opportunities for outward migrants. There is a scarcity of professional project managers in the Pacific which
        makes it very difficult to fill core positions and retain expertise in the project teams. Capacity-building efforts are
        often undermined, as staff with newly acquired skills and qualifications move to new roles in or outside the public
        service. This includes working with other development partners such as the Asian Development Bank (ADB), United
        Nations, Australia and New Zealand thereby retaining local capacity to support development projects. In addition,

     7. COVID-19 further weakened capacity in the PICs. The pandemic created a greater need for transparency and
        accountability over public spending in an increasingly constrained fiscal space. Concurrently, the pandemic
        exacerbated PICs’ already serious shortage of domestic technical experts and project managers and diverted public
        institutions’ attention and resources from other development priorities. These impacts were evident in the small
        nations of RMI and FSM, where they experienced a particularly high turnover of qualified staff during the period
        due to limited domestic economic opportunities, with many skilled international consultants leaving their posts.

     8. There are often too few people to complete all the functions required in a particular ministry or department,
        meaning that available staff are stretched across a wide range of functions. Limited technical expertise and high
        turnover of employees in public institutions are among the most frequent problems encountered across RMI and
        FSM, often offset by the inclusion of international experts in donor funded projects. The cost of international
        consultants is also a relatively high expense in project budgets given the remoteness and fragility of the countries.
        Remote islands have more challenges hiring skilled technical international consultants, and many of whom do not
        complete their full contract term. In addition, experience has shown that the reliance on international expertise has
        not built national capacity which is required for sustainable implementation of development projects.

     9. The World Bank (WB) has implemented innovations to address capacity constraints and improve operational
        efficiency and effectiveness in PICs. The IDA18 scale-up and the compressed IDA19 period led to a faster than
        expected expansion of the World Bank-financed portfolio, especially in RMI, both in the number of projects and
        value of commitments. To address the implementation support and capacity challenges, the WB financed Central
        Implementation Units (CIUs) were implemented in 2019 in the Ministry of Finance, Banking, and Postal Services
        (MOFBPS) of RMI, and Department of Finance and Administration (DOFA) in FSM2. Hands-on Expanded
        Implementation Support (HEIS) for procurement was implemented in 2023 resulting in an average increase of 129%
        in the number of contracts, 132% in the value of contracts and over 60% reduction in procurement delays across
        both countries. The WB also implemented expanded financial management (FM) support which has resulted in a
        moderately satisfactory FM Performance rating across the portfolios in RMI and FSM.

     10. The concept of shared centralized services is still relevant and offers an important solution to improving PIC
         government capacities. The CIUs current objectives are to (i) to strengthen institutional capacity to build systems
         and develop a cadre of local staff to operate these systems and implement the policies / processes for the longer
         term; and (ii) to enhance quality and timeliness of preparation and implementation WB-financed projects. They
         comprise of local and international staff, under individual consultant contracts. These staff provide support in
         common function areas including Procurement, Financial Management (FM), and Environmental and Social (E&S),
         and Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) coordinated by a Project / Program Manager. The PIUs are comprised of
         technical Project Manager, Project Officer, administrative staff and technical consultants.

     11. The recent Joint Country Portfolio Performance Reviews (CPPRs) for RMI (December 5, 2024) and FSM
         (December 12, 2024) highlighted the multiple challenges facing the CIUs. The existing CIUs provide support and
         capacity building for pipeline delivery and the implementation of active projects in the RMI and FSM portfolios.
         However, there are challenges which undermine the CIUs’ effectiveness, these include (i) a lack of clear
         performance metrics to incentivize capacity building (performance and compensation are not linked to capacity

12. The performance of the CIUs has been affected by unclear mandates and absence of standard operating
    procedures. There is a lack of clarity in roles and responsibilities of PIUs, CIUs and Implementing Agencies. This has
    led to significant challenges in implementation and increased the risk of implementation delays and mismanaged
    funds in relation to donor-funded projects. Example: Overlapping division of responsibilities (Project Officer and CIU
    doing the same function). At times the division of responsibility between the finance and line ministries with respect
    to the CIUs has also been unclear, as well as the line between the strategic role required of the government and the
    hands-on implementation more appropriately required of the CIUs. The absence of a clear standard operating
    procedures (SOPs) exacerbates the confusion related to roles and responsibilities of all implementation
    stakeholders. A comprehensive review of the CIUs’ structure, composition, processes and systems will be key to
    improving performance.

13. Changes in the operations of the CIU are needed to build an effective framework for sustainable local capacity.
    The establishment of CIU served as pilots to build local capacity. However, there is persistent high staff turn-over
    and experience has proven that the brain drain from both countries cannot be fully mitigated. Factors contributing
    to the high staff turnover, include the (i) lack of a formal mechanism for workforce planning (ii) inefficient contract
    renewal and recruitment processes (iii) lack of staff performance reviews and (iv) lack of compensation reviews.
    Innovative solutions such as a formal knowledge transfer program (international consultants to local staff),
    implementation of SOPs and systems to improve operational efficiency, and the creation of a pipeline of local talent
    (by using various strategies including internships; targeting scholarships recipients; capacity building, career
    development and competitive renumeration) are required to attract, train and retain local staff.

14. CIU international specialists have de-prioritized capacity building activities for local staff in favor of preparation
    and implementation support activities, which are often perceived as more urgent. The CIUs have not proven
    effective in building considerable local capacity to manage government funds, in part due to the lack of a clear
    directive for international experts to work with national staff to transfer knowledge has prohibited capacity building.
    For example, while E&S consultants in RMI have developed a detailed capacity building plan, this was only partially
    implemented due to local staff turnover and other demands on the E&S specialists' time. There is an urgent need
    to identify national candidates invested in national development outcomes including strategies for engagement
    with high school and college students to provide pathways to career development within PICs. In addition, SOPs are
    required to ensure that international experts build the capacity to effectively manage all government and public
    sector funds. A key action arising from the joint CPPRs is that the RMI and FSM will implement formal knowledge
    transfer programs / capacity building in the short term (6 months).

15. Need for greater accountability and transitioning of the CIU into a Central Support Unit (CSU) . The current CIUs
    are embedded as components in investment projects. There are no indicators in the investment project’s Results
    Framework to measure the CIU’s performance. There is also a lack of periodic reviews of the CIUs’ performance by
    the MOFBPS in RMI and DOFA in FSM. This has contributed to the failure of the CIUs to deliver the desired level of
    results and impact in both countries. There is an urgent need to develop a stand-alone project with a Results
    Framework that allows for increased accountability for the performance of the CIUs. This will require an incremental
    transition of the CIU into a CSU to resolve the ambiguity in the mandate, formalize increased hands-on support to

16. The World Bank has a coordinating partnership with other development partners to share knowledge, harmonize
    approaches and to agree on the ‘division of labor’ Close collaboration with development partners is facilitated by
    formal and informal coordination mechanisms at the regional and individual country level. The WB works closely
    with Australia, New Zealand, ADB, and other key development partners on macroeconomic policy and public FM
    issues in the region. Providers of budget support to the Pacific take a coordinated approach and agree on joint policy
    matrices to minimize the burden of required policy actions on clients. The Alternative Procurement Arrangements
    specified under the WB Procurement Regulations allow the World Bank to rely on and apply ADB procurement rules
    and procedures, and for ADB to take the lead in providing implementation support and monitoring of procurement
    activities for these projects. There is also on-going “division of labor�? in key sectors such as digital and
    transportation.


Relationship to CPF

17. The Project aligns with the World Bank Group’s Nine Pacific Island Countries (PIC9) Regional Partnership
    Framework (PIC9 RPF) FY17–21, report #100997-EAP. The RPF was extended to FY23 by the Performance and
    Learning Review (PLR) of The Regional Partnership Framework (report #145750-EAP) on February 6, 2022. A new
    RPF is under preparation, which will include a stronger focus on selectivity, partnerships, and building client
    capacities to drive results and increase development impact. The PIC9 RPF FY17-21 outlines four major focus areas,
    which are relevant to RMI, FSM and the Project in that capacity of national governments to manage large-scale
    projects, and development partner and donor financing will be enhanced. The focus areas addressed are (i) Focus
    Area 1: Fully exploiting the available economic opportunities; (ii) Focus Area 2: Enhancing access to economic
    opportunities and (iii) Focus Area 3: Protecting incomes and livelihoods; and (iv) Focus Area 4: Strengthening the
    enablers of growth opportunities. In particular, the Project will focus on improving implementation support for
    projects using government funds by building capacity and streamlining ministry processes, thereby improving public
    expenditure management.

18. The PLR notes that project delivery units (CIU’s) have proven to be a cost-effective innovation to the complexities
    of delivering in the Pacific. The PLR states that the PIC9 can benefit from economies of scale through the centralized
    implementation arrangements. It highlights that the WBG program will put special emphasis on building institutional
    capacity and sustainable local capacity. The Project is thus aligned with Focus Area 2: Enhancing access to
    employment opportunities and is fundamental to better delivery across the four Focus Areas.

D. Concept Description

21. The Project will strengthen the institutional and human resource capacity of the implementing agencies within the
    Governments of the RMI (GoRMI) and the FSM (GoFSM) to enhance operational efficiency and portfolio quality.
    This will be achieved by amplifying innovative solutions to improve fiduciary, E&S, contract management and M&E
    capacity and develop sustainable human resources. The Project consists of three components: (i) strengthening
    institutional capacity; (ii) enhancing human capacity; and (iii) project management.

   Component 1. Strengthening Institutional Capacity (Approximately US$ 9.0 million)

   Sub-component 1.1: Institutional review

22. This sub-component will conduct a comprehensive review of the institutional capacity of the implementation
    agencies. Activities supported under this component include: (i) mapping the roles and responsibilities of MOFBPS/
    DOFA in relation to CIU and other ministries implementing investment projects; (ii) reviewing the current structure
    for CIUs and identifying gaps/overlaps in responsibilities with key government ministries for project management as
    well as fiduciary, E&S and M&E processes; (iii) implementation of recommendations from the institutional review
    including the transition from CIU to CSU structure, development of job descriptions, renumeration framework and a
    performance framework to attract and retain talent; (iv) review country systems against the international best
    practice including the environmental and social framework (ESF) for RMI and FSM to identify critical gaps and provide
    recommendations; and (v) implement recommendations from the country system review (i.e. support for legislative
    revisions, development of Codes of Practice). Independent consultants, excluding current CIU and PIU consultants,
    will be hired to conduct these activities. Advanced procurement will be done during project preparation to ensure
    that the institutional review is completed by Year 1.

   Sub-component 1.2. Strengthening government capacity for project support functions.

23. This sub-component will strengthen and improve the efficiency and capacity of key ministries to provide effective
    project support by improving the operating environment as identified in sub-component 1.1 reviews. Activities
    supported this sub-component that include: (i) consultancy to develop a revised set of streamlined processes to
    increase efficiency (i.e. development of SOPs, guidance notes for project documents such as Project Operations
    Manuals and procurement plans); (ii) investing in emerging technologies for innovative solutions to improve

    operational efficiency and monitoring (Geo-Enabling initiative for Monitoring and Supervision (GEMS) and other
    systems ); and (iii) financing the operations of the CIU and subsequent CSU.

24. The transition from CIUs to CSUs will be guided by the findings and recommendations from the institutional review
    under sub-component 1.1. It is anticipated that the two CSUs will be established and functioning by Year 2.
    Consideration will be given to ensuring that PIU capacity is in place to manage the transition period and technical
    surge support may be required to ensure continued project implementation. The project will complement the PFM
    II Projects in RMI and FSM that will support improvements in financial management and procurement. Independent
    consultants will be hired to implement these activities.


   Component 2: Enhancing Human Capacity (Approximately US$ 5 million)

25. This component will enhance technical skills for project support functions in RMI and FSM by attracting, training
    and retaining talent. A consulting firm will be hired to conduct activities for both countries to facilitate economies of
    scale and sharing of knowledge. The key activities of this component include; (i) development and implementation
    of training plans by identifying skill gaps between CSU staff and job description requirements for upskilling; (ii)
    development and implementation of a formal knowledge transfer program for international consultants to train
    CSUs, local staff and strengthen the PIUs; (iii) attendance at courses, conferences and relevant trainings identified
    through training plan; (iv) implementation of human resource management inclusive tools and strategies to recruit
    and retain high calibre local staff; (v) support for graduate and vocational education for candidates identified through
    a robust selection process to be documented in the Project Operations Manual, prioritizing staff with intent to remain
    in / return to country; (vi) support for internships with the College of Micronesia, College of Marshall Islands,
    government departments, PIUs and CSUs to build transferable skills; (vii) conducting awareness campaigns in high
    schools to support and promote career pathways within country as well as providing support for fiduciary, E&S,
    contract management and M&E training and (viii) identifying functions that can be conducted remotely and utilize
    social media to attract potential candidates in the diaspora / or in remote islands who may be interested in remote
    work or internships. These activities will be managed by the designated CIUs / CSUs focal points.

   Component 3: Project Management (Approximately US$ 1.0 million)

26. This component will cover the activities related to project management, including procurement, financial
    management, environment and social safeguards, contract management and monitoring and evaluation. Project
    management and supervision of the Project components will be done by the designated focal points in the CIUs /
    CSUs. This component will also finance audits, administrative costs, technology support (ICT and M&E system),
    operational costs and coordination between countries and activities. Support for a mid-term review (MTR) and the
    Implementation Completion and Results Report will also be provided under this component.

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts



27. The project is assessed to have low environmental and social risks. The objective of the project is to support the
    institutional and capacity building of selected ministries of the government of RMI and government of FSM to improve
    management of public funds and expenditure realized by improved project support functions, which is to be achieved
    by improving the performance of fiduciary, E&S and M&E management capabilities, and developing sustainable
    human resources. The project activities are all institutional strengthening and capacity building activities and do not
    support any civil works, nor any feasibility studies/technical design for future physical infrastructure. Therefore, the
    project is not expected to have any direct impact on natural habitats or cultural heritage sites, nor support any activity
    that leads to involuntary resettlement. Key risks relate to labor management of government staff and interns and
    stakeholder engagement which will be managed via the inclusion of requirements in the Environmental and Social
    Commitment Plan (ESCP). The ESCP will require that environmental and social considerations are integrated into
    Terms of Reference for all project activities.
