  B. Introduction and Context

  Country Context

 1.      The COVID-19 outbreak hit Argentina at a time when its economy faced significant macroeconomic imbalances.
 Following a two-year recession, high inflation, and lack of access to capital markets, the strict lockdown imposed to contain
 the spread of the pandemic triggered a sharp GDP contraction of -10 percent in 2020. The recovery in economic activity
 started in the fourth quarter of 2020, when the Government of Argentina (GoA) gradually eased confinement measures.
 In May 2021, a second COVID wave hit Argentina, which prompted the Government to tighten again social distancing
 measures to prevent overburdening the already strained health system and the slow pace of vaccination.

 2.       The recovery has been varied and remains uncertain. While economic activity is above pre-pandemic levels in
 agriculture, construction, financial activities and manufacturing, other sectors such as hotels and restaurants, and
 transports and logistics are still contracting. The recovery shows signs of slowing down, as the most dynamic sectors that
 kick-started the recovery in 2020 Q4 are showing signs of stagnation. Labor markets are still subdued and have not been
 responding to the cyclical recovery of the economy. Although labor market participation and employment have increased
 since the end of the strict lockdown in June 2020, the observed dynamics is driven by the informal sector, as private sector
 employment has stagnated at very low levels since March 2020, and public sector employment, already at high levels, has
 increased modestly in the past months.

 3.      The implementation of a fiscal stimulus package to support families and firms – equivalent to 3.9 percent of
 GDP, coupled with an abrupt decline in revenues, resulted in a central government (primary) deficit of 6.5 percent of
 GDP in 2020. Financing the response to the COVID-19 shock required an important monetization of the deficit. This has
 exacerbated macroeconomic imbalances, notably by exerting pressures on inflation, foreign reserves and on the
 persistent large gap between the official and parallel exchange rates, despite tight capital and price controls. However,
 the spike in commodities prices since late-2020 contributed significantly to restoring stability in the FX market and
 enabling the Central Bank to partially rebuild its reserves.

 4.      Notwithstanding a strong 2020 Q4 carry-over effect and ample idle capacity, growth performance will be
 subject to a number of challenges. Uncertainty, as well as price and capital controls, will limit strong investment growth,
 which is needed to raise low-productivity levels and generate formal job creation, after many consecutive years of anemic
 growth. Real GDP is projected to rebound by 6.4 percent in 2021, but the economy is not projected to reach 2019 GDP
 levels before 2023. High commodity prices are supporting the fiscal and external accounts, as well as foreign reserve
 accumulation. The Government has announced additional support measures to households, which are expected to be

 completely financed by high commodity prices windfall revenues. There are important downside risks to this baseline
 scenario, notably the evolution of the pandemic and the pace of vaccination. Prolonging social distancing measures risks
 could dent the incipient recovery, and the fiscal balance, via lower fiscal revenues and a renewal of temporary support
 measures, risking accelerating inflation.

 5.      Even prior the pandemic, Argentina’s economy has been crisis-prone and overly dependent on commodity
 exports, underscoring the need to implement a long-term growth strategy. Before the pandemic, Argentina has spent
 one third of the last 60 years in recession, 14 episodes in total, each one of which lasted close to 2 years on average and
 resulted in negative growth. Despite challenges, the agri-food sector has grown and innovated remarkably in the last three
 decades providing a key silver lining to the economy over the years, driven by technological change and, over much of the
 period, by high international agricultural prices. However, unsophisticated products, primarily in the agricultural sector,
 continue to make up more than 60 percent of the Argentine export basket, leaving the country vulnerable to fluctuations
 in these resources. Going forward, it is important to view and focus on innovation and its contribution to growth, and
 thereby increased domestic value-add, with a longer term, stable vision even at times of crisis.

 6.        Significant regional and social disparities related to poverty and access to basic services persist. Urban poverty
 in Argentina remains high, reaching 42% of population in the second semester of 2020, with a 10.5% of extreme poverty
 and 57.7% of children poverty. Inequality, as measured by the Gini index, increased from 41.2 to 42.9 points during 2017-
 2019 (WB, 2021). While at the national level 13.8 percent of the population has unsatisfied basic needs, 23.5 percent of
 indigenous households exhibit unsatisfied basic needs; and this number increases notably to one third of the population
 living in the northern regions of the country (INDEC, 2010). Disparities exist among and within provinces in terms of levels
 of economic and social development and access to high-quality basic services, where rural areas have traditionally had
 lower levels of access to water and other basic services, thus affecting rural inhabitants economic and social development
 (WBG, 2015).The “digital divide�? accentuates existing inequalities; while 60 per cent of the higher income population can
 do their jobs remotely, the rate is less than 10 per cent for the lower-income population (ILO, 2020).

 7.       Past and potential impacts of climate shocks call for urgent actions. Argentina’s diverse landscapes and climates
 as well as its geographic location results in the country being at high risk to national hazards. An estimated 60% of natural
 disasters in Argentina are floods, which have resulted in 95% of economic losses for affected populations. The majority of
 regional flooding has been linked with warm phases of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) phenomenon. Floods are
 expected to cause at least US$700 million in economic losses every year and one-in-three Argentinians live in a flood-
 prone area; approximately 14.2 million people. Argentina has also been adversely affected by increased aridity and
 drought events. During the last 20 years, Argentina experienced several extreme and widespread droughts in many
 different regions, including the core cropland areas. The most devastating recent events were recorded in the years 2006,
 2009 and 2011,when reported impacts of the main events induced losses of more than US$ 4 billion and more than 1
 million persons were reported to be directly or indirectly affected1.

 8.      The confluence of health, macro-fiscal and environmental challenges requires Argentina to adopt policies that
 promote smart and green private sector growth that can achieve the most with limited fiscal space . Argentina has
 committed to not exceed more than 359 million tons of net emissions of carbon dioxide equivalent (MtCO2e) by 2030.
 This new goal is ambitious, since it is equivalent to a total decrease in emissions of 19% by 2030, compared to the historical
 peak reached in 2007, and a reduction of 25,7% compared to the previous NDC submitted in 20162. This commitment as
 well as other policy initiatives, such as the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and the Partnership for Action on

     Sectoral and Institutional Context

 9.      Argentina’s abundant natural capital supports the livelihoods of a large rural population, as well as the
 development of the agricultural, livestock, forestry and fishing sectors. The country is one of the largest food producers
 in the world. With more than 32 million hectares of agricultural land, 53.6 million hectares of native forests and 1.2 million
 hectares of forest plantations, agriculture, forestry and fishing represented 7.2 percent of the GDP in 2019 (WB Data Atlas,
 2020), generating approximately 12 percent of direct employment (and more than 20 percent, if indirect employment is
 considered).

 10.      Argentina lags behind in global infrastructure competitiveness rankings. In the 2018 World Economic Forum's
 World Infrastructure Ranking, Argentina ranks 68th out of 140 countries, behind peer countries in Latin America, such as
 Chile (41), Mexico (49) and Uruguay (62). Investment in infrastructure represents 2.5percent of GDP and the quality of the
 infrastructure stock is lower than expected. Given its GDP per capita as a reference, Argentina is below the expected level
 of development in the transport sector, and the water and sanitation sectors show a significant gap at the national level
 and it is significantly below the norm in several regions, among which is the Greater Buenos Aires area. To close the
 infrastructure gap, the country should double the level of investment as a percentage of GDP over the next 15 years and,
 in addition, it would require a combination of policy measures providing an adequate system for attracting investment for
 promoting key interventions in subsectors with high growth potential3.

 11.      Decline of family farming, land concentration in the hands of fewer larger commercial farms4, and lack of
 income generation opportunities generate poverty pockets. In the Pampas region, the sector is comprised of
 commercialized grain and oilseed production, where extensive farming and the use of mechanization, innovation, and
 technology dominate agricultural production. Production of higher-value crops, such as fruits, vegetables, and legumes,
 is less commercialized and predominantly takes place outside the Pampas region where contemporary technologies and
 decision-support tools are needed to maintain and improve yields. Distribution of farmland in Argentina is highly unequal
 across different types of farms. Seventy-five percent of Argentina’s farms are “family farms�?, accounting for 18 percent of
 the country’s agricultural land and producing 27 percent of total agricultural output, all of which face problems and
 challenges that hamper their development and competitiveness. On the other hand, the farm units grouped under “non-
 family farms�? (excluding special cases) make up 23 percent of all farms accounting for 79 percent of Argentina’s
 agricultural land (more than 25 million ha) and generating about 72 percent of the country’s agricultural production in
 value terms5.

 12.     Climate Change and other weather-related events present critical risks for agriculture and the welfare of
 farmers. Rising temperatures and precipitation variability are expected to lead to more frequent and intense natural
 disasters, such as heat waves, floods and droughts, impacting people’s health, water resources and food security. It is
 estimated that productivity losses associated with climate change can reduce agricultural GDP between 3 percent and 17
 percent in some countries of the region 6. Small-scale farmers and rural populations are particularly vulnerable to the

 13.      Irrigated agriculture in Argentina is the main destination of water resources and agriculture is responsible for
 70 percent of the annual water consumption7. The irrigated production area reaches around 2.1 million ha,
 corresponding to 5 percent of the total cultivated area. This irrigated area, despite its low relative efficiency, generates
 around 13 percent of the value of the country's agricultural production. However, irrigated agriculture presents an
 interesting potential for expansion, given that certain areas of the country present favorable agroecological conditions
 together with availability of water resources, which would make it possible to generate important production benefits
 from investments in irrigation. Increasing the irrigated agricultural area in Argentina (there is an estimated area of about
 6.2 million ha with irrigation potential) will produce an enormous positive impact, not only on production but also on its
 quality and reducing vulnerability to climate variations, together with the creation of employment and generation of
 better conditions for the development of rural areas.

 14.     INTA (Instituto Nacional de Tecnología Agropecuaria) is the leading institution in Argentina, focused on
 research, extension and innovation in agricultural technology. Among the essential elements of this strategy described
 above is the need to strengthen INTA’s capacity to respond to regional economies’ needs in terms of innovation, extension
 services, food safety, and climate change adaptation/mitigation 8. INTA is a decentralized government organization
 (created in 1956) with operational and financial autonomy under the Ministry of Agriculture, Livestock and Fishery
 (Ministerio de Agricultura, Ganadería y Pesca, MAGyP). The objective established in its Institutional Strategic Plan for the
 period 2015-2030 is to promote innovation and contribute to the sustainable development of a competitive, inclusive,
 equitable and environmentally friendly agri-food system, focusing on innovation, knowledge development and technology
 sharing through its systems of extension, information and communication.

 15.      Digital agriculture presents a unique opportunity to promote sustainable economic growth, but challenges still
 persist 9. As part of the World Bank study “What’s Cooking: Digital Transformation of the Agri-Food System,�? this Digital
 Agriculture Profile for Argentina leverages the expertise of stakeholders to evaluate the current landscape of digital
 agriculture in Argentina, considering its key actors across value chains, the main challenges they face, and the potential to
 overcome these barriers through the adoption of innovative digital technologies. Mainstreaming digital agriculture will
 require further analysis of enabling factors, but identifying and prioritizing digital technologies allows donors,
 governments, investors, and other stakeholders to focus on the technologies with the highest potential in order to
 maximally improve food production in Argentina.

 16.      Despite the efforts made towards the reduction of gender gaps, gender inequality in the rural sector remains
 high. Although 45 percent of the registered family farmers in Argentina are women, only 10 percent of the family farming
 units self-identify as headed by women. Available data suggests that less than 30 percent of women have had access to
 communal property and only 16 percent have benefited from the allocation of public lands (Ferro, 2013). The lack of
 employment opportunities for rural women in highly mechanized production systems has concentrated the greatest
 female participation in agriculture in peasant and indigenous family farming. Women receive a tiny part of the monetary
 benefits, by being linked to little or no commercialized products; and there is a perception that their salaries are

  C. Proposed Development Objective(s)

 The objective of the project is to (i) improve economic10 and climate11 resilience of targeted beneficiaries in the agri-
 food system; and (ii) in case of an eligible crisis or emergency, to respond effectively to it.

  D. Concept Description

 17.     The proposed Project is an Investment Project Financing (IPF) with a total estimated cost of US$ 425 million,
 comprising an IBRD loan of US$ 300 million that is expected to leverage US$ 75 million of counterpart funding from the
 Borrower, and US$ 50 million in contributions from local beneficiaries. The total project cost is estimated to be US$ 425
 million to be implemented over a 5-year period at a national level., although priority will be given to key areas of
 investment aiming at solving main constraints and closing territorial development gaps.

 18.     The project aims to support a more environmentally sustainable, job-rich and inclusive development pathway
 for project beneficiaries, leveraging relevant technologies including climate-intelligent technologies and practices,
 improving investments in agro-logistics and strengthening capacities and institutions. The proposed project concept is
 based on other operations recently implemented in Argentina, aiming at promoting an agro-industrial and climate-smart

 19.      Beneficiaries. The proposed Project’s main target group consists of agro-industrial MSMEs and rural producers’
 associations (including small and medium family farmers and will promote the inclusion of women, youth, and
 indigenous people). The Project would also indirectly benefit other agri-businesses, rural enterprises and local population
 through the expansion of public infrastructure and connectivity services, as well as through the strengthening of key public
 sector institutions, especially INTA, which are providing support to the agri-food systems12. In terms of inclusion, it is
 expected that young people, women, and members self-identifying as Indigenous People will receive a certain percentage
 (at least 25 percent) of the subproject's investments.

 Project Components:

 20.      Component 1: Public Investments for Improving Connectivity and Agro-industrial13 Development. The
 component’s main objective is to increase the coverage and quality of rural public infrastructure with a perspective of
 supporting economic recovery and job creation in the agri-food systems and agro-industrial sectors, within a framework
 of balanced and sustainable territorial development that ensures social and gender equity, rational use of natural
 resources and climate change resiliency. The main areas of investment will be inter-alia: (a) Connectivity (“last mile�?
 segments in access rural/tertiary roads, water, expansion in coverage of internet networks and digital services, rural
 electricity, etc.); (b) Rehabilitation and Improvement of existing primary works for better distribution and management of
 water resources for agricultural production and processing, drainage and flood control schemes and establishment of
 efficient watershed management systems; and (c) Logistics and infrastructure for marketing and reduction in losses and
 waste (including public facilities for storage, classification and transport, as well as logistical services.

 21.      Component 2: Agro-industrial Development and Strengthening of Rural Livelihoods. The component will support
 competitive, innovative, and climate-resilient agribusiness initiatives, as well as investments to enhance the livelihood and
 living conditions of rural households, including access to water and other unmet basic needs (UBN). The component will
 support the execution of the activities both at the provincial or national level and the investments subprojects (to be
 implemented by agro-industries or producers’ organizations) will be financed through a matching grant mechanism
 leveraging contributions from the beneficiaries. The basic approach is to promote collective actions (subprojects), with
 the aim of gaining economies of scale, enhancing bargaining power, facilitating knowledge sharing, reducing costs of
 production and service delivery, improving income and living conditions, as well as climate resilience, of businesses and
 rural households. In addition, all works and infrastructure investments will include measures to increase climate resilience
 and their design and construction will follow strict climate smart practices. Activities under Component 2 will be
 supported via the following three subcomponents:

 22. Component 3: Development of Innovative, Climate Smart and Inclusive Technologies for Transformation of the

     Argentina's agricultural sector (including young people in professional agro-technical education, extensionists,
     researchers and rural entrepreneurs); and (iii) other public and private national and provincial institutions that are
     part of the Argentina´s innovation ecosystem. This component aims to promote innovation and digital agriculture, the
     adoption climate-smart practices as well as rural youth inclusion and generational transition of employment through
     the two main subcomponents described below:

            a. Subcomponent 3.1: Institutional modernization to support an innovative, climate-smart agribusiness
               sector. This subcomponent aims to promote innovation and improve the agribusiness enabling environment
               by contributing to the modernization of INTA, Argentina´s leading institution in research, extension and
               innovation in agricultural technology. Investments will be aimed at ensuring that the institution continues to
               spearhead agricultural innovation nationally and internationally including investments in equipment,
               technological infrastructure, remote sensing equipment and tools, etc.
            b. Subcomponent 3.2: Capacity building and agri-tech ecosystem strengthening. This subcomponent seeks to

               rural youth and women in agriculture and food systems and (ii) leverage INTA´s scientific and technological

               towards increasing climate change resilience and reducing the impact of climate events, helping Argentina
