    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context

1.       In recent years, El Salvador has modernized its institutions and reduced crime, but significant development
challenges persist. The COVID-19 pandemic hit El Salvador hard, with GDP dropping 7.9 percent in 2020 despite major
fiscal outlays. The country continues to face a delicate macroeconomic context linked to the debt burden exacerbated by
the COVID-19 crisis which contributed to a still fragile fiscal situation. The economic recovery following the crisis was also
impacted by high inflation stimulated by rising global fuel and transport costs. The country has made significant progress
in recent years reducing poverty and crime. However, major challenges persist to creating employment opportunities and
fostering economic growth, as well as reaching fiscal sustainability and improving access to affordable public services.

2.       Real GDP per capita grew by 3.5 percent in 2023 fueled by public investment and consumption, primarily driven
by remittances, alongside a rebound in exports. It has nevertheless tended to grow at a more modest pace averaging just
over 2.4 percent in recent years1 . Over the 2000-2023 period inflation averaged 2.6 percent, and population growth 0.28
percent. In 2023, per capita GDP in absolute terms was US$5,023. The country’s GDP is expected to grow 3.2 percent in
2024, slightly below 2023, in line with the Central American average.

3.      EL Salvador has made significant progress towards reducing extreme poverty and boosting shared prosperity.
The percentage of Salvadoreans living with less than US$6.85/day (2017 PPP) dropped from 52 percent in 2000 to 28
percent in 2022. The unemployment rate is also relatively low at 2.8 percent in 2023, down from 5 percent in 2020. This
estimate however does not capture underlying challenges in the labor force. Approximately 25 percent of Salvadoreans
are estimated to live abroad2 driven primarily by the search for better employment opportunities. Remittances have
become key for Salvadoran families3. However, remittances have counterproductive effects, contributing to lower labor
force participation and hours worked.4

4.     El Salvador faces some significant macroeconomic challenges tied to debt sustainability. The fiscal deficit
widened to 4.2 percent of GDP in 2023, up from 2.7 percent in 2022 with public debt reaching 76 percent. The government

5.      El Salvador is also highly vulnerable to climate change and weather-related hazards incurring annual losses of

    Sectoral and Institutional Context

6.      Developing domestic renewable energy (RE) resources could provide a necessary boost for economic
development and support increased climate resilience. El Salvador is endowed with a wealth of RE resources, including
climate resilient energy sources such as geothermal. The Government’s National Integral Development Strategy includes
a key pillar to accelerate investment in resilient and sustainable infrastructure, including clean energy projects8. Greater
renewable energy integration is essential to securing more affordable electricity for the population and businesses, to
create green jobs and support climate change mitigation and adaptation.

7.     El Salvador’s electricity sector is vertically unbundled with an active private sector. The electricity sector is
governed by the General Electricity Law (Ley General de Electricidad) of 1996 and regulated by the Superintendencia
General de Electricidad y Telecomunicaciones (SIGET). In 2022, the government consolidated various agencies to create
the General Directorate of Energy, Hydrocarbons, and Mines [Dirección General de Energia, Hidrocarburos y Minas
(DGEHM)], responsible for overall energy policy. The state-owned conglomerate the Comisión Ejecutiva Hidroeléctrica del
Río Lempa (CEL) operates the publicly owned hydropower facilities and oversees LaGeo, the geothermal subsidiary
company of CEL.

8.       Electricity supply comes from domestic generation using renewables and imported fossil fuels as well as from
electricity imports. El Salvador had a total installed power capacity of 2,914 MW, from a combination of hydropower,
geothermal, biomass, solar, wind, and thermal sources. Between 2011 and 2016, the percentage of generation from
hydropower plants declined significantly compared to thermal generation sources whose share of overall power
generation increased. To meet electricity demand, which was growing at an annual rate of 2-3 precent, the country
imported as much as a quarter of its power needs from the regional electricity market (SIEPAC). Starting in 2016, domestic
generation was ramped up, first with the addition of hydro and solar power capacity (adding over 800 MW between 2016
and 2023) and more recently with the addition of a 390 MW natural gas plant. These additions have helped displace
imports and contributed to El Salvador becoming a net exporter of power in 2023.

9.      However, climate change is expected to negatively impact rainfall patterns, increasing the intensity and
frequency of droughts and affecting the continuity of hydropower generation. While El Salvador produces a significant
amount of electricity from renewables, many of these sources (solar, wind, biomass, and hydro) provide variable supply
given hourly and seasonal variations in resource availability. Adding to this, hydropower generation is affected by a marked
decrease in rainfall patterns exacerbated by climate change, especially in the country’s Dry Corridor. During the El Nino
event in 2023, the country’s share of power generated from fossil fuels rose to 42% in 2023 9 exposing the country (and
those that it exports to) to high and volatile international oil and ultimately electricity prices10 as well as higher emissions
from thermal combustion.

10.      High electricity prices negatively impact businesses and households in El Salvador. In 2020, El Salvador had the
highest retail tariffs in Central America. The natural gas plant and a solar farm commissioning in 2022 helped reduce tariffs,
nonetheless electricity prices remain high compared to other Latin American countries. This affects business expansion
and the entry of prospective businesses and industries, dampening economic growth and job creation. High electricity
prices also create affordability issues for households and increase the existing energy subsidy burden for the Government.
Greater renewable development would help lower electricity prices, enhancing affordability and reducing subsidy needs.

11.     Stimulating further domestic RE generation, including from geothermal is high on the government’s agenda. A
key priority of the Government of El Salvador As part of its mandate the DGEHM is looking to increase renewable energy
production to reduce costs, ensure affordable electricity prices, and increase energy security as key priorities for the
Government of El Salvador11. El Salvador’s updated NDC (2022) also aims to reduce annual emissions from fossil fuels by
640 Kt CO2eq by 2030 compared to a business-as-usual scenario12, supported by geothermal expansion. Further
development of the country’s vast geothermal resources will be essential to meeting these development and climate
goals.

12.     El Salvador is endowed with significant geothermal potential, with large reservoirs still undeveloped. El
Salvador’s geothermal potential is estimated to be between 500-1,000 MW, but only 209 MW is currently used for power
generation. If further exploited, geothermal energy could provide more reliable power generation, displacing a portion of
thermal generation and imports, and help reduce prices and hedge against future price increases. The GoSV aims to
increase the geothermal generating capacity by 100 MW by further developing existing geothermal fields and starting
production from the new geothermal fields of Chinameca and San Vicente.

13.     Geothermal can provide additional power system resilience as well as other economic benefits. The additional
power the Chinameca and San Vicente fields would generate multiple economic benefits including: (i) strengthening
power system resilience to climate change; (ii) reducing GHG emissions from thermal generation; (iii) stimulating
investments in the economy and job creation; (iv) increasing the affordability of electricity and reducing fossil fuel
subsidies; and (v) increasing energy security by reducing vulnerability to international fossil fuel price fluctuations.

 Relationship to CPF

14.      The proposed project will support achieving key objectives of El Salvador’s Country Partnership Framework
(CPF) 2023-2027 and is aligned with other global commitments and programs, by supporting improved resilience to
disasters and climate change and quality job creation. It contributes directly to meeting the CPF’s High-Level Outcome 3
‘Improved resilience to disasters and climate change’ by adding geothermal power which is more resilient to droughts.
Specifically, it contributes to High-Level Outcome 3’s Objective 5 ‘Improved disaster risk management’ and Objective 6
‘Strengthened climate-smart interventions’. The project also supports increased private sector investment in the
geothermal sector that will support the creation of green jobs, in line with CPF High-Level Outcome 1 ‘Increased Private
Sector Led Job Creation’. In addition, the project contributes to CPF High-Level Outcome 1’s Objective 1 ‘Fiscal
sustainability’ and Objective 2 ‘Formal private sector development’ as it will help reduce and stabilize electricity costs and
tariffs that facilitates business expansion, job creation, and economic growth. Furthermore, the proposed project is
consistent El Salvador’s National Determined Contribution (NDC) and the Paris Agreement by supporting greater
diversification of the electricity generation matrix with renewables and displacing thermal generation, which contributed
to climate change mitigation and adaptation.

 D. Concept Description

17.      The proposed project will support the geothermal development of the San Vicente and Chinameca Geothermal
Fields. By financing geothermal drilling for resource confirmation and promoting geothermal direct use, the project is
expected to achieve its development objective and achieve multiple economic benefits.

The components of this project are the following:

(a) Component 1: Drilling campaign to confirm geothermal resources – This component will finance the drilling of wells
    to ensure the geothermal fluid necessary for the operation of the first-generation units in San Vicente and Chinameca
    and expand confirmed resources in the San Vincente and Chinameca fields to an estimated potential of 50 MWe. This
    component will also carry out feasibility studies for the future expansion of San Vicente and Chinameca geothermal
    fields. These activities will help attract potential private investment for the installation of successive generation units
    in both fields and will facilitate full geothermal field development in the future.

(b) Component 2: Social and environmental programs and geothermal center of excellence – This component will
    comprise: (i) Activities and investments aimed at promoting the direct use of residual geothermal heat, to create new
    economic opportunities in the communities around the geothermal plants within the Dry Corridor, (ii) Financing
    mitigation works associated with the environmental and social impact identified in the environmental impact study
    and/or required by the environmental permits for these fields, and (iii) Development of studies and training activities
    that will facilitate the establishment of the Geothermal Center of Excellence. To empower women to access
    commercial markets and close the access gap to technology, the Project will ensure that at least 25 percent of the
    component beneficiaries are female.


(c) Component 3: Technical Assistance and Project Implementation Support – This component seeks to support LaGeo in
    strengthening its technical and management capacity for the implementation of the project, including by hiring expert
    personnel for technical assistance and supervision tasks, as well as for monitoring project activities. This component
    may finance the hiring of personnel with experience in WB acquisition processes to collaborate with the project
    management unit (PMU), to support the supervision of drilling activities, and the hiring of external audits.

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts



18.       The Environmental and social risks are High, in light of potentially significant direct and indirect impacts both
from the directly financed project activities (e.g., drilling of geothermal wells in Chinameca and San Vicente) as well as
from the associated activities, to be implemented by a private sector developer, to construct and operate geothermal
power plants at the two locations. The primary environmental, health and safety impacts and risks are expected to include:
(a) significant freshwater consumption requirements during the drilling phase, in settings where local communities already
experience water shortages during the dry season (particularly in Chinameca); (b) deforestation, and resulting habitat
fragmentation, from earthworks and civil works related to the associated activities of plant development which will
require installing networks of pipelines to carry hot and cold process waters, developing and improving access roads, and
constructing transmission lines; (c) potential for surface and groundwater contamination in the case of improper disposal
of drilling muds; and (d) varied construction related impacts and risks typical of infrastructure and industrial projects
stemming mostly from the associated activities of power plant development, including noise, dust, road and traffic safety,
occupational health and safety, and potential labor influx related issues (including the resulting additional stress on limited
water resources) in the neighboring towns. Relevant positive environmental benefits are also expected, as long-term

outcomes associated with the operation include, among others, climate change mitigation and cleaner air. The potential
social risks and impacts stem largely from: (1) resettlement and land acquisitions; (2) potential labor influx from other
areas of the country; (3) high expectations within local communities for social programming as well as employment
opportunities; (4) misconceptions about water availability and use for the project. The overall risk is somewhat moderated
by the capacity of the project sponsor, LaGeo, to manage these risks and impacts – notably developed through years of
on-the-ground learning-by-doing and gradual improvement of management practices over nearly 20 years of exploratory
drilling and development at the two project sites, as well as successful development and operation of geothermal plants
elsewhere in the country. However, LaGeo lacks experience implementing World Bank/financed projects and needs to
become fully acquainted with E&S requirements. The project will also include social and environmental investments
benefiting local communities under component 2. Nonetheless, the overall risk is still considered high, particularly given
the scale of the projects (when considering also their associated activities) and the challenges of managing multiple works
contractors at two different geothermal sites, which could present significant E&S managerial, coordination, and oversight
complexities for LaGeo. The WB team will work with the client to review and update the ESIAs and related management
plans prepared in accordance with the ESF in 2019 at the time of preparation of an earlier, larger version of this project,
including additional stakeholder consultations as needed. The proposed risk classification will also be revisited during due
diligence, and if necessary updated, as more information becomes available.
