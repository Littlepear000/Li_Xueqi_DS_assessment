I. BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING


        A. Introduction

      1.     This Project Paper seeks the approval of the World Bank’s Regional Vice President to provide a
      grant in the amount of US$30.0 million from the International Development Association (IDA) for an
      Additional Financing (AF). The proposed AF would support the costs of expanding activities of the
      Malawi COVID-19 Emergency Response and Health Systems Preparedness Project (P173806) under the
      COVID-19 Strategic Preparedness and Response Program (SPRP) using the Multiphase Programmatic
      Approach (MPA), approved by the Board on April 2, 2020, and the vaccines AF to the SPRP approved on
      October 13, 2020. 1 The primary objectives of the proposed AF are to enable affordable and equitable
      access to COVID-19 vaccines and help ensure effective vaccine deployment in Malawi through
      vaccination system strengthening and to further strengthen preparedness and response activities under
      the parent project. The Malawi COVID-19 Emergency Response and Health Systems Preparedness
      Project in an amount of US$7.0 million IDA was approved on April 14, 2020 prepared under the SPRP.
      The parent project is also being restructured to reflect the inclusion of vaccination activities and revision
      to the components, results framework, institutional and implementation arrangements, and closing
      date.

      2.      The purpose of the proposed AF is to provide upfront financing to help the Government of
      Malawi (GoM) purchase and deploy COVID-19 vaccines that meet the World Bank’s vaccine approval
      criteria (VAC) and strengthen relevant health systems that are necessary for a successful deployment
      and to prepare for the future. The proposed AF will help vaccinate eight percent of the country’s
      population, while the COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access Advanced Market Commitment (COVAX AMC)
      Facility will cover 20 percent of the population by the end of calendar year 2021. Specifically, the
      proposed AF will support procurement of vaccines to cover eight percent of the population and co-
      finance deployment activities of vaccines including those under the COVAX Facility. The total of 28
      percent coverage represents nearly half of the GoM target of 60 percent coverage by mid-2022. World
      Bank financing for the COVID-19 vaccines and deployment will follow the World Bank’s VAC. As of April
      16, 2021, the World Bank will accept as threshold for eligibility of IBRD/IDA resources in COVID-19
      vaccine acquisition and/or deployment under all Bank-financed projects: (i) the vaccine has received
      regular or emergency licensure or authorization from at least one of the Stringent Regulatory Authority
      (SRAs) identified by the World Health Organization (WHO) for vaccines procured and/or supplied under
      the COVAX Facility, as may be amended from time to time by WHO; or (ii) the vaccine has received WHO
      Prequalification (PQ) or WHO Emergency Use Listing (EUL). Malawi’s regulatory authority is responsible
      for its own assessment of the vaccines’ safety and efficacy and is solely responsible for the authorization

    3.     The need for additional resources to expand the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) response
    was formally conveyed by the GoM on February 16, 2021. The request was for assistance for COVID-19
    pandemic response totaling US$131.5 million of which US$80.4 million was for vaccination. The request
    for the pandemic response included key interventions across multiple sectors inter alia health,
    education, governance, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) and security. The World Bank’s response
    on March 5, 2021 proposed multi-sectoral support including: (i) US$30.0 million AF to the parent project
    which will provide essential resources to enable the expansion of a sustained and comprehensive
    pandemic response that will appropriately include vaccination in Malawi; (ii) US$25.7 million increase in
    IDA allocation to the proposed Malawi Education Sector Reform Project (P174329) and leverage of an
    additional US$8.34 million from the Global Program for Education Multiplier to support decongestion in
    classrooms; and (iii) US$5.0 million AF for the ongoing Malawi Equity with Quality and Learning at
    Secondary Project (P164223) to ensure safe schools.

    4.      Critically, the proposed AF seeks to enable the acquisition of vaccines from a range of sources
    to support Malawi’s objective to have a portfolio of options to access vaccines under the right
    conditions (of value-for-money, regulatory approvals, and delivery time among other key features). On
    December 22, 2020, the GoM received confirmation as a COVAX AMC Group Participant. Malawi expects
    to cover the cost of vaccinating the 20 percent of the population (estimated 3,779,688) 2 through the
    COVAX Facility. Malawi’s COVAX application noted interest in purchasing additional doses through the
    COVAX Facility via cost-sharing contributions. The proposed AF may support Malawi to source vaccines
    through the COVAX as a priority and to also support the country in accessing vaccines beyond the COVAX
    Facility as necessary. The proposed IDA financing will build on this to expand Malawi’s access. The
    availability and terms of vaccines remain fluid and prevent the planning of a firm sequence of vaccine
    deployment, especially as the actual delivery of required vaccines is unlikely to be completed
    immediately. Rather, the proposed financing enables a portfolio approach that will adjust during
    implementation in response to developments in the country pandemic situation and the global market
    for vaccines.

    5.     In addition to vaccines acquired through the COVAX Facility, the African Vaccine Acquisition
    Task Team (AVATT) convened by the African Union (AU), is in the process of negotiating, through the
    United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), additional access to vaccines that would contribute to taking
    the total population covered up to 60 percent, in countries that request for it. When firm contracts are
    in place between UNICEF as the appointed procurement agent, and the manufacturers, UNICEF will
    conclude contracts with participating countries for the supply of the vaccines. These contracts will be
    reviewed by the World Bank to ensure that they comply with all operational policies and provide value
    for money in terms of both price and delivery times before financing from this Grant can be disbursed
    toward the member country upon its request.

    6.     Status of COVID-19 situation: Malawi’s first confirmed case was registered on April 2, 2020 with
    the first wave between June and August 2020. Between mid-December 2020 and March 2021, Malawi
    experienced the second wave. In less than four months since the beginning of the second wave, the
    cumulative number of confirmed cases increased fivefold from 6,055 on December 11, 2020 to 33,551
    on March 31, 2021. Similarly, a sixfold increase in the cumulative number of deaths (from 186 to 1,117)
    was recorded during the same period. As of June 10, 2021, the cumulative number of the cases and
    deaths stand at 34,432 and 1,158, respectively. Genomic sequencing of 245 COVID-19 positive samples
    collected between mid-October 2020 and mid-March 2021 found that 208 samples (84.8 percent)
    yielded the Beta B.1.351 variant, which was first detected in South Africa and 14 (5.8 percent) yielded
    the Delta B.1.617.2 variant first detected in India. As of June 10, 2021, Malawi has received 512,000
    doses of AstraZeneca vaccines and has vaccinated 365,256 people. Malawi remains at risk given: (i) the
    rise of variants in the region which have greater transmissibility; (ii) slow roll out of the vaccine due to
    challenges in global supply chain and deployment capacity compounded by vaccine hesitancy; and (iii)
    the COVID-19 situation in its neighboring region remains fluid (see annex 3).

      B. Consistency with the Country Partnership Framework (CPF)

    7.     This proposed AF is aligned to World Bank Group Malawi’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF)
    FY21-25 (Report No. 154505-MW). Specifically, it is aligned to Focus Area 3: Strengthening human capital
    development. The pandemic has increased the priority of health protection and treatment in Malawi,
    and the proposed AF is aligned to the World Bank’s response from relief to resilient recovery focusing
    on (i) saving lives; (ii) protecting the poor and vulnerable; (iii) sustainable business growth and
    employment; and (iv) strengthening policies and institutions. The proposed AF, like the parent project,
    is also aligned with both global health priorities and IBRD/IDA priorities on improving pandemic
    preparedness.

      C. Parent Project Design and Scope

    8.    The Project Development Objective (PDO) of the parent project and this proposed AF is to
    prevent, detect and respond to the threat posed by COVID-19 in Malawi and strengthen national
    systems for public health preparedness. The parent project includes the following three components
    and focuses on surveillance, testing, contact tracing and case management. This is further described in
    annex 4 and in the Project Appraisal Document (PAD) of the parent project 3:

        (a) Component 1: Emergency COVID-19 Response (US$5.30 million equivalent). This component
            provides immediate support to Malawi to prevent the spread of COVID-19 through surveillance
            and containment strategies.

          (i) Subcomponent 1.1: Case detection, confirmation, contact tracing, recording, and reporting
          (ii) Subcomponent 1.2: Health system strengthening

  D. Project Performance

10. The parent project’s progress towards achievement of the PDO and overall implementation
progress are rated Satisfactory and Moderately Satisfactory respectively in the last Implementation
Status and Results Report (ISR) of May 7, 2021, and the project continues to make good progress. As of
May 7, 2021, disbursements amount to US$5.5 million or 74.0 percent of commitments. The project has
strengthened Malawi’s capacity to prevent, diagnose and treat COVID-19 through (i) procurement of
critical medical and diagnostic supplies and equipment; (ii) training of health surveillance assistants
(HSAs), health workers and lab technicians; and (iii) support to rapid response teams. By June 30, 2021,
expenditures amounting to about US$6.0 million are estimated to be disbursed. The undisbursed
amount is committed.

11. The PIU has been effectively coordinating project planning and procurement. The PIU is shared
with the Southern Africa Tuberculosis and Health Systems Strengthening Project (SATHSSP, P155658)
and therefore has experience implementing a World Bank financed project. Given the additional
workload and accelerated implementation, assistants were added to key PIU positions (e.g.
Procurement Assistant, Medical Waste Specialist) after the parent project became effective. All legal
covenants were met. In line with the World Bank’s Environmental and Social Framework (ESF), the PIU
prepared and disclosed the ESCP, a Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP), an Environment and Social
Management Framework (ESMF), and a Labor Management Plan (LMP). There are no overdue audits.
The first audit is for the period ending June 30, 2021 and due on December 31, 2021.

evaluation, waste management). The significant support to vaccine procurement under the proposed
AF compliments other development partner financing which focuses on the broader response. The

               Health care waste management for the vaccination program is

          Box 2: Liability and Indemnification Issues in Vaccine Acquisition and Deployment

Key Issues:
   • The rapid development of vaccines increases manufacturers’ potential liability for adverse effects
        following immunization.
   • Manufacturers want to protect themselves from this risk by including immunity from suit and liability
        clauses, indemnification provisions, and other limitation of liability clauses in their supply contracts.
   • Contractual provisions and domestic legal frameworks can all operate to allocate that risk among
        market participants, but no mechanism will eliminate this risk entirely.
COVAX-financed vaccines for AMC countries:
   • COVAX has negotiated model indemnification provisions with manufacturers for vaccines purchased
        and supplied under the COVAX AMC.
   • In providing vaccines through COVAX AMC, COVAX requests COVAX AMC participants to have in place
        an indemnity agreement directly with manufacturers, and the necessary indemnity and liability
        frameworks for that purpose – either in the form of the COVAX model indemnification arrangements
        or prior bilateral arrangements with manufacturers.
   • The COVAX Facility will have a no-fault compensation scheme for AMC countries as part of its risk
        mitigation strategy. This will cover vaccines supplied only through COVAX AMC.
   • Malawi will have to consider what it will take to implement these indemnification provisions and how
        they can avail of the benefits of the no-fault compensation scheme.
Vaccines purchased outside of COVAX:
   • Malawi will need to enter direct indemnification arrangements with manufacturers.
   • Malawi does not currently have legislation in place to provide statutory immunity for manufacturers.
        Malawi does not have a national no fault compensation scheme.
   • Adoption of any such indemnification provisions would have to be in accordance with Malawi’s own
        national strategy and framework.

Possible World Bank support, depending on needs, may include: Information sharing on: (i) statutory
frameworks in OECD countries and other developing countries; and (ii) overall experience in other countries;
training and workshops for government officials to familiarize them with the issues.

III. KEY RISKS

       39. The proposed AF supports the parent project development objectives and aims to reduce the risk
       that the objective is not achieved by supporting the country-level scale-up of vaccine purchase and
       deployment amongst other related health system strengthening. However, COVID-19 vaccination
       introduces new risks to the project and assumptions of potential situations that will be mitigated to the
       extent possible, but still entail new residual risks. Specific risk assessments and associated mitigation
       measures related to planned vaccination are informed by the findings of the revised VIRAT in Malawi
       and are described below.

       40. The overall risk to achieving the PDO with the expanded scope and proposed AF for vaccination
       is High. This is due to the uncertainties and unprecedented nature and scale of the crisis. The main
       continuing “substantial” or “high” risks include: (i) adverse effects on the macroeconomic and fiscal
       situation of the GoM stemming from COVID-19; (ii) health sector institutional capacity, which can be
       easily overwhelmed if there is a surge in the number of new cases with potential disruptions in essential
       health and nutrition services; and (iii) environmental and social (discussed in detail under Environmental
       and Social section below). In addition, there are procurement related problems, stemming from
       potential difficulties in procuring critical supplies and equipment, given disruptions in global supply
       chains which is now rated as substantial residual risk. There are several additional Substantial or High
       risks related specifically to COVID-19 vaccination, namely: (i) concerns over the timely availability and
       deployment of vaccines aligned to phases in the NVDP that are safe, efficacious, and effective; (ii)
       challenges to conducting and financing a national campaign at record speed to vaccinate 60 percent by
       mid-2022; (iii) a highly fragmented health sector which could affect response efficiency; and (iv) elite
       capture, in case some groups get preferential treatment in the vaccination program. The proposed AF
       includes interventions that aims to partly mitigate most of these risks. The government will remain

proactive, with support of partners, to secure vaccines as soon as available, adopting a flexible and agile
strategy.

41. The overall technical design risk is High. The large-scale acquisition and deployment of COVID-19
vaccines entails certain significant risks. First, global demand for vaccines continues to exceed supply,
and vaccines that meet the World Bank’s VAC may not be available to be acquired in a timely manner.
Second, a mass vaccination effort stretches capacity, in particular in low-capacity environments such as
Malawi, entailing risks. The proposed World Bank support for Malawi to develop vaccination acquisition
and strategies and investment in deployment system capacity specifically aim to mitigate these risks.
First, to mitigate the risks associated with fast development of vaccines, the World Bank will rely on
robust vaccine approval processes. As the COVID-19 vaccine landscape is changing continuously, to
ensure safety and effectiveness, World Bank-financed acquisition of vaccines is required to meet high
standards as described in Board-approved criteria for COVID-19. Second, very robust implementation
support to enable large scale acquisition and deployment of COVID-19 vaccines will be provided through
the life of the project, and in collaboration and coordination with key partners. Third, the project will
draw on lessons learned from countries in the region and adapt as experience is gained and new
information becomes available. Fourth, the proposed project aims to support RCCE that includes
addressing vaccine hesitancy which will partially mitigate risk of low update. The remaining risk must be
considered against the risk of resulting in the country having less timely and effective deployment of
vaccines, potentially exacerbating development gaps and eroding past development gains.

42. There may be Substantial political risks to the proposed AF related to the commitment and ability
of the authorities to ensure appropriate targeting of the AF-supported vaccines to reach the priority
populations, based on objective public health criteria, and ability to manage public sentiment should
there be a gap between vaccine targets and vaccine delivery. These risks will be mitigated through the
assurance mechanisms that this proposed AF will support such as the development of guidelines for
prioritized intra-country allocation. There are also risks related to governance of vaccine purchase and
deployment, such as potential fraud and substandard quality. In addition, there are risks associated with
fraudulent attempts to gain access to vaccines to be administered not following approved protocols of
priority populations or for personal gain. This includes the risk of elite capture and of corruption in the
implementation of the vaccination program. The MoH will leverage the use of digital technology to
register, monitor and track vaccine patients as well as monitor service delivery point inventory control.

application of anti-corruption guidelines for vaccine purchase and deployment and robust financial
management oversight of the use of funds, as elaborated in the fiduciary risks below. Considering these
mitigation measures, the proposed AF is expected to have little impact on the overall Substantial political
and governance risk to the parent project.

IV. APPRAISAL SUMMARY

     A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis

      48. The economic rationale for investment in a COVID-19 vaccine is strong, considering the massive
      and continuing health and economic losses due to the pandemic. Malawi’s economy was on a trajectory
      for its third year of faster growth in 2020 before the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic. Malawi’s
      economy grew by 4.4 percent in 2019, a marked increase from 3.5 percent in 2018. The COVID-19
      pandemic has had a negative impact on Malawi’s economy as the growth in 2020 is estimated at 1.0
      percent, down from the projected 4.8 percent. Growth is projected to rebound in 2021 to 2.8 percent,
      although the nature of the recovery will depend on the evolution of the COVID-19 pandemic and
      governments policy actions. If the virus spreads extensively, it could severely disrupt the labor force and
      increase poverty. Although the headline inflation rate (year to year) for February 2021 was at 8.3
      percent, less than the 11 percent registered in February 2020, the rate is still high. With the expected
      increase in poverty due to the pandemic, the high inflation rate means that even less people will afford
      essential goods and services. The forecasted increase in urban poverty could range from 1.6-4.9
      percentage points. Given the strong harvest and agriculture based rural economy, in the baseline
      scenario rural poverty may see limited impact. However, vulnerability to falling below the poverty line
      could increase by 2.2 percentage points in the downside scenario.

     49. There is a strong economic case for investing in COVID-19 vaccination, considering the health and
     economic benefits of immunization. The successful delivery of a vaccine has the best potential to reverse



                                                                                                            Page 40 of 74
    The World Bank
    Additional Financing for Malawi COVID-19 Emergency Response and Health Systems Preparedness
    Project (P176402)



the negative impact of the pandemic on the economy, generating benefits that will far exceed vaccine-
related costs. A well-targeted deployment of a COVID-19 vaccine can help reduce the increases in
poverty and accelerate economic recovery. Even at levels of imperfect effectiveness, a COVID-19 vaccine
that is introduced and deployed effectively to priority populations can assist in significantly reducing
mortality and the spread of the coronavirus and accelerating a safe reinvigorating of key sectors that are
impacted. The vaccine can also reverse human capital losses by ensuring increases in productivity as
Malawians return to work. The effective administration of a COVID-19 vaccine will also help avoid the
associated health care costs for additional cases of infection.

50. While the uncertainty around the costs and effectiveness of a COVID-19 vaccine makes it difficult
to calculate its cost-effectiveness, the effective delivery of a COVID-19 vaccine will have direct benefits
in terms of averted costs of treatment and disability, as well as strengthened health systems. The
estimated costs of vaccinating 20 percent of the population of Malawi are at US$58.4 million; even if the
vaccine averts non-severe cases and no other benefits are considered, the investment is expected to
break even. Further, investments in vaccine delivery systems generate health and economic benefits
beyond just delivering the COVID-19 vaccine. First, investments in last-mile delivery systems to
administer the COVID-19 vaccine to remote communities will require strengthening community health
systems, which can have spillover effects to effective delivery of other services, helping close the
significant urban-rural gap. Second, as the COVID-19 vaccine is introduced and lockdowns and
movement restrictions are eased, patients can continue to access care for other conditions. Third, the
economic benefits of slowing down the economic downturn are likely to significantly exceed the
US$58.4 million needed to vaccinate 20 percent of the population, leaving aside the immediate health
benefits. Given both the economic and health system benefits, an effectively deployed COVID-19 vaccine
presents significant benefits.

51. The proposed project includes the procurement of oxygen that has additional economic benefits.
Oxygen is an essential intervention that saves lives and is now in high demand due to the pandemic. As
the shortage of oxygen can lead to higher deaths from COVID-19, the successful deployment and
delivery of oxygen will reduce the mortality and burden of illness of COVID-19 patients. Beyond
managing COVID-19 symptoms, the expected benefits from the deployment of oxygen in healthcare
facilities will extend to support other patients needs such as during child delivery, surgeries and trauma
care. The expected benefit is therefore anticipated to outweigh the estimated cost of procuring the
oxygen.

          69. The parent project’s environmental and social risk rating is Substantial. Activities under the
          proposed AF are expected to have positive outcomes as they include the deployment of the COVID-19
          vaccine to the population of Malawi, as well as the improvement of capacity for surveillance, monitoring
          and containment of COVID-19. However, the activities also pose environmental and social risks,
          including health and safety risks due to the dangerous nature of COVID-19. Facilities testing and treating
          patients may generate biological, chemical waste, and other hazardous by-products that could be
          harmful to human health if improperly managed. These risks will be mitigated through occupational and

70. Gender inequalities and norms are critical considerations when designing policies and
interventions in emergency situations and pandemics. They play an important role in who gets access
and how fast, to critical health services. Gender norms also influence risk of exposure to disease, as well
as of spreading it. At the same time, biological sex can influence how susceptible a person is to disease
and how well they respond to treatment and/or vaccines. In a pandemic, this has multiple implications.
On the one hand, pandemic response has to be cognizant of the gender-based differences in access to
and use of services due to limited mobility and financial capacity; and on the other hand, support needs
to be provided to at-risk groups such as family caregivers (the majority of whom are women) to reduce
their risk of getting ill and/or passing it on to others. Moreover, pandemics can create or exacerbate the
conditions that especially put women and girls at greater risk of gender-based violence.

71. As part of the ESF requirements, World Bank-supported projects can also take steps to mitigate
the risks of SEA/SH. In pandemics, access to services may be reduced due to lockdowns and reduced
mobility, and the rule of law becomes fragile, increasing the risks of GBV. For instance, women and girls
may be forced into exchanging sexual favors for access to testing, treatment, vaccines or even supplies.
Another social risk is that marginalized and vulnerable social groups, including women and disabled
populations, may have more barriers to access to COVID-19 services and information. There is also a risk
that vaccine deployment plans could leave women behind, considering the larger male mortality of
COVID-19 and the tendency in many countries to overlook the importance of gender inequalities in social

and economic activity. This risk will be reduced as teams are encouraged to carefully assess this aspect
of deployment.

72. The environmental risks are considered Substantial. Key environmental risks are those related to
(i) medical waste management and disposal; (ii) the spread of the virus among health care workers and
the population at large; (iii) occupational and community health and safety issues related to testing,
handling, transporting, disposing of supplies and medical samples, and upgrading of designated health
facilities/laboratories; and (iv) risks associated with the rehabilitation of existing healthcare
infrastructure to address increase demand from COVID-19 pressure. While risks to the safety of workers
and the community are relevant and significant, they are considered temporary, predictable, and readily
managed through the project design features and mitigation measures. No major civil works are
expected under this proposed AF. The small works planned under the parent project have not yet been
implemented. Two facilities will be rehabilitated, i.e. the Emergency Operations Centre (EOC) office
building in Lilongwe and the quarantine unit at Chileka Airport in Blantyre. In addition, the proposed AF
will construct and install an oxygen plant at a Central Hospital. All small works are expected to be carried
out within existing facilities and therefore impacts on the environment through impacts on habitats are
considered unlikely. However, construction-related risks, including but not limited to, occupation and
community health and safety, dust/noise related nuisance, sourcing of construction materials,
construction waste management are likely and are addressed in the ESMF.

73. The health facilities involved in COVID-19 diagnostic testing, treatment, and vaccination will
generate medical waste and other hazardous byproducts that if not managed appropriately may cause
health risks. In line with WHO Interim Guidance (February 12, 2020) on “Laboratory Biosafety Guidance
related to the novel coronavirus (2019-nCoV)”, and other guidelines (February 12, 2020), the parent
project developed Infection Control and Health Waste Management Plans (ICHWMP) and included WHO
standards on COVID-19 response in the ESMF for the project. The plan includes training of staff to be
aware of all hazards they might encounter. Infectious medical wastes generated by the health facilities
will be managed in accordance with the facilities ICHWMP. Medical waste generated is collected in
specific containers and transported and burned in incinerators located in strategic and safe locations
once or twice a week. Regional hospitals (Kamuzu Central Hospital in the central region and Queen
Elizabeth Central Hospital in the south) have incinerators for the treatment of hospital waste, and rural
health facilities take the waste to be treated in the nearest central hospitals or other designated medical
waste management facility with functioning incinerator. The ICHWMP will be revised to address the
management, handling and disposal of medical wastes related to the deployment of vaccines and
related project activities.

74. The parent project’s environment and social instruments have been updated to include the

for storing, transporting, and disposing of contaminated medical waste and will outline guidance (in line
with international good practice and WHO standards on COVID-19 response) on limiting viral contagion
in health care facilities. To mitigate these risks, the GoM will also revise the facilities ICHWMP addressing
the management, handling and disposal of medical wastes.

78. Climate change. The proposed AF has been screened for short and long-term climate change and
disaster risks, and high risk to the project’s activities was identified due to extreme flooding and
droughts. Malawi is a small, densely populated, landlocked country located in Southern Africa with an
overall tropical climate, yet temperatures in higher elevations can be relatively cool. Mean annual
temperature has increased by 0.9°C between 1960 and 2006, and is projected to increase by 1.1 to 3.0°C
by the 2060’s. However, as year‐to‐year variability in rainfall is very high in Malawi, long-term trends are
difficult to identify. Malawi is prone to adverse climate hazards that include dry spells, seasonal
droughts, intense rainfall, riverine floods, and flash floods. Drought and flood disasters occur in the flood
plains, wetlands, and forests of the Lower Shire Valley and directly affect over half a million people.
Projections indicate that the country will become wetter and rainfall intensity is expected to increase.
These climate hazards have increased in frequency, intensity, and magnitude over the past twenty years,
with dire consequences on food and water security, water quality, energy resources, and sustainable
livelihoods of the most rural communities. The increasing incidence of floods and droughts has also led
to infant malnutrition and chronic ailments associated with malaria, cholera, and diarrhea. Malaria alone
is expected to increase and spread to previously cool zones as temperatures increase due to global
climate change.

79. Women are especially vulnerable to impacts from climate change and geophysical hazards due to
their lower socioeconomic status and their major role as caregivers in households and healthcare
facilities. To address Malawi’s high vulnerability to climate shocks, the government has in place policies
such as the National Climate Change Policy (2013) and the Malawi’s National Adaptation Programme of

                               Annex 2: Current COVID-19 Situation in Malawi

 1.      Malawi registered its first case of COVID-19 on April 2, 2020 and as of June 10, 2021 the
 cumulative number of cases and deaths stand at 34,432 and 1,158, respectively. The country confirmed a
 high number of cases between June and August 2020 (first wave) followed by a second wave starting in
 mid-December 2020. In less than four months since the beginning of the second wave, the cumulative
 number of confirmed cases increased fivefold from 6,055 on December 11, 2020 to 33,551 on March 31,
 2021. Similarly, a sixfold increase in the cumulative number of deaths (from 186 to 1,117) was recorded
 during the same period. Unlike during the first wave, there is also a marked increase in the number of
 active cases and people needing hospitalization. Furthermore, most of the COVID-19 cases in the second
 wave were locally transmitted, which indicates that the virus was rapidly spreading in communities. The
 second wave spike in COVID-19 cases and deaths in Malawi was likely due to a more transmissible new
 COVID-19 variant first detected in South Africa (B.1.351). Gene sequencing of 24 COVID-19 positive
 samples collected between mid-December 2020 and mid-January 2021 found that 18 samples (75
 percent) were the B.1.351 variant 20.

                             COVID-19 admissions and deaths as of March 30, 2021 21




 2.       GoM response: The GoM has responded to the pandemic to minimize cases and deaths. In March
 2020, the GoM developed in collaboration with technical partners a costed contingency plan focusing on
 critical priorities and immediately committed MWK 2.5 billion (US$3.4 million) towards the COVID-19
 response. This plan was updated as the situation evolved, and more funding became available. The GoM
 gradually intensified its efforts, initially focusing on measures such as screening at airports and other
 points of entry. The measures were enhanced after the President declared a “State of Disaster” (March
 20, 2020) including: (i) redeployment of health personnel to border and PoE; (ii) travel suspension; (iii)

 closing of all schools and colleges; (iv) restricting public gatherings to less than 100 people; and (v) banning
 travel of foreign nationals from countries highly affected by COVID-19. In response to the second wave,
 the President declared a second “State of Disaster” on January 12, 2021 and identified priority needs: (i)
 testing and contact tracing; (ii) recruitment of additional medical personnel; (iii) procurement of medical
 equipment (e.g. oxygen); and (iv) increasing hospital space or infrastructure as priority needs.

 3.      Vaccination progress to date: As of May 9, 2021, Malawi has received 512,000 doses of Astra
 Zeneca (360,000 doses from COVAX Facility, 102,000 doses from the AU and 50,000 doses from the
 Government of India) and vaccinated 320,300 people. Due to the initial suboptimal uptake and to
 minimize wastage of vaccines which have a limited shelf life, the GoM announced on April 6, 2021 that
 Malawi was entering into the second phase of vaccination and extended vaccination to anyone aged 18
 years or above with a special appeal to those who cannot socially distance due to their occupation. This
 underscores the need for coordinated demand creation and community engagement to address vaccine
 hesitancy. The GoM is addressing emerging issues and challenges in the deployment process. Key
 challenges include:

     •   Low self-risk perception in general among general population and therefore see no perceived
         benefits of the vaccine
     •   Misinformation on the vaccines - The GoM’s announcement of the expiry of some vaccines led
         to belief among some that all vaccines are expired, prompting them to opt to wait for the new
         consignment. Social mobilization and public education campaigns are ongoing but lack adequate
         funding and also face coordination challenges with multiple stakeholders involved and suboptimal
         information sharing.
     •   Entrenched religious and traditional beliefs -These beliefs are mostly promoted by some religious
         leaders, some of which are entrenched in some communities that refuse any vaccines and
         medicines. Social mobilization and public education campaigns are ongoing but lack adequate
         funding and face coordination challenges as described above.
     •   Limited public education campaign in rural communities - Rural communities, where most
         people live, have not been targeted with public education campaigns to encourage them to access
         the vaccine. The MoH and partners should commit more resources towards targeting rural
         populations and ensure that adequate service sites and teams are available on the ground.
     •   Inadequate service teams at district level providing vaccine services - Many districts have very
         few teams to provide the vaccines. In some districts, there are as few as one or just two teams.
         They are getting exhausted and overwhelmed to cover the entire districts. The MoH is currently
         undertaking trainings of service providers but it will take a while to ensure they have adequate
         number of teams on the ground.

 4.      The MoH is working with key technical partners to adjust the RCCE and demand creation activities
 accordingly. The MoH has indicated that once vaccination rates increase, phased roll out will be re-
 introduced. However, the MoH and key technical partners are updating the NVDP which is a living
 document, and there may be changes to the phased approach to promote optimal uptake.

                                       Annex 3: Parent Project


   1. The parent project is comprised of three components described below. Refer to the PAD for details.
        (a) Component 1: Emergency COVID-19 Response (US$5.30 million equivalent). This
            component provides immediate support to Malawi to prevent the spread of COVID-
            19 through surveillance and containment strategies. It supports enhancement of
            disease detection capacities through provision of technical expertise, laboratory
            equipment and systems to ensure prompt case finding and contact tracing, consistent
            with WHO guidelines; and strengthening of case management capabilities.
                (i) Subcomponent 1.1: case detection, confirmation, contact tracing, recording,
                     and reporting
                (ii) Subcomponent 1.2: health system strengthening
        (b) Component 2: Supporting National and Sub-national Prevention and Preparedness
            (US$0.95 million equivalent). This component supports strengthening the capacity of
            the public health system for preparedness and respond to COVID-19 pandemic and to
            future pandemics and other threats to health security. The component supports
            improving prevention of and response planning for emerging infectious diseases in
            the context of human and animal health system development.
        (c) Component 3: Implementation Management and Monitoring and Evaluation
            (US$0.75 million equivalent). This component supports costs associated with Project
            management and coordination, M&E, operational reviews to assess implementation
            progress and logistical support. The component also supports the GRM and other
            activities in the ESCP.
