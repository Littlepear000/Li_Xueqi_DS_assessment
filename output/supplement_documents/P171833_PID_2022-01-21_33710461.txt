   B. Introduction and Context

   Regional Context

      1. Economic growth in the Eastern Caribbean has been volatile due to the concentration of activities in a
         few sectors and the impact of climate and natural disaster shocks.1 Growth volatility in the region is
         among the highest in the world, and over twice the average for the Pacific Islands between 2000 and 2019
         (Figure 1). The Organization of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS) member states are heavily reliant on
         tourism, which accounts for 35 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) on average and provides more
         than half of all jobs. As a result, economic performance is heavily influenced by developments in the
         tourism sector as well as the business cycles of the tourism source markets – the United States, Canada
         and Europe. This was especially noticeable in the aftermath of the Global Financial Crisis, as well as in the
         context of the ongoing coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic. The dependence on tourism also amplifies the

      2. Economic activity in the region contracted sharply in 2020 due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic
         (Figure 2). The health and socio-economic impact of the pandemic on the OECS countries has been severe,
         pushing the region into a recession. With the advent of the pandemic, Eastern Caribbean countries
         worked to contain the spread of infections, and as a result international travel was almost completely
         halted, resulting in a 70 percent drop in earnings from tourism. Indeed, the entire accommodation sector
         in OECS member countries (approximately 16,000 rooms) was forced to shut down during the pandemic
         with an estimated 20,000 tourism workers laid-off; 60 percent of whom were women.2 In 2020, GDP
         contracted by 12.6 percent in Grenada, 20 percent in Saint Lucia and 4.2 percent in Saint Vincent and the
         Grenadines (SVG).3 Non-tourism related economic activities also contracted due to disruptions in
         international logistics, as well as the domestic implementation of social distancing measures including
         curfews and business closures. Job losses are also estimated to be high, with estimates from Saint Lucia
         suggesting that over 70 percent of households have seen their incomes fall since the beginning of the
         pandemic, and nearly 45 percent of the working population pre-pandemic had stopped working by May
         2020.4 Similarly, 70 percent of Dominican households reported loss of employment or a reduction in salary
         as of June 2020.5 The eruption of La Soufrière volcano and subsequent heavy rains in April 2021, further
         compounded the effect of the pandemic in St. Vincent and the Grenadines,6 resulting in additional loss of
         livelihoods and assets.

         vulnerabilities and impacts of climate change on livelihoods, the economy, biodiversity, and ecosystem
         services.

      4. Economic recovery in the Eastern Caribbean will depend on countries’ abilities to collaborate to secure
         the health and integrity of their shared marine assets and increase resilience to climate-induced shocks.
         A gradual recovery is expected over the medium term, with GDP growth rebounding to about 3 percent
         and 5 percent in 2021 and 2022 respectively in Granada, 8 percent and 5 percent in 2021 and 2022 in
         Saint Lucia and 4.1 and 2.7 percent in 2021 and 2024 in Saint Vincent and the Grenadines. The economies
         are expected to return to the pre-crisis level by 2023. To support this recovery, the integrity and climate
         resilience of the region’s coastal and marine natural assets are required to underpin the countries’ shared
         blue economy including in sectors such as tourism and fisheries that represent the next frontier of

   Sectoral and Institutional Context

      5. Unleashing the blue economy of the Caribbean means rebuilding economic activities in a way that sets
         the foundation for a more equitable, inclusive, climate-resilient, and sustainable economy. The blue
         economy is defined as an approach to promote sustainable and integrated use of marine resources for
         economic growth, improved livelihoods, and job generation while preserving the health of ocean
         ecosystems.14 Anticipating and adapting to the impacts of climate change is an essential component of
         the blue economy approach, ensuring that low-carbon and climate resilient measures are incorporated in
         efforts across sectors ultimately to ensure the sustainability of marine resources and their benefits. The
         blue economy covers a range of interdependent sectors, including fisheries, coastal tourism, and waste
         management.15 Fisheries, for example, provide the hospitality industry with quality and affordable
         seafood, while the tourism sector supports fisher livelihoods and generates employment opportunities.
         Waste management is crucial to the health of fisheries and other marine resources and ecosystems which
         are, in turn, critical for the tourism sector.

   D. Project Description

   7. The UBEC Program will be implemented as a Series of Projects (SOP) over a fifteen-year period (each project has
      a life cycle of five-years) involving multi-sectoral blue economy investments to support Caribbean countries to
      strengthen their climate resilience and accelerate economic recovery from the impacts of COVID. Phase 1 of UBEC,
      to be implemented between fiscal year 2022 and 2028, involves Grenada, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the
      Grenadines and the OECS Commission, and focuses on three interrelated blue economy sectors: tourism, fisheries
      and waste management. Phase I will support national and regional activities with a total financing envelope of
      US$56 million in IDA credits (national and regional) and grants (regional) along with US$4 million in PROBLUE trust
      fund grants. Phase 2 of UBEC will involve additional OECS economies and other Caribbean economies (IDA and IBRD)
      and is programmed to begin in FY23. An additional US$3 million is being leveraged from the PROBLUE Multi-Donor
      Trust Fund in the form of RETF Grants to help prepare a cohort of Caribbean countries to join UBEC in Phase 2.

   8. The UBEC SOP provides an overarching framework for integrating activities in the tourism, fisheries, and waste
      management sectors around a shared regional developmental goal. The SOP includes interventions at the national
      and regional levels to leverage economies of scale and ensure a harmonized approach. The SOP modality also
      affords the necessary flexibility for countries to join when they are ready. The expected projects in the series are:
      • Phase 1: FY22-FY28: Grenada, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines and OECS Commission.
      • Phase 2: FY23-FY29: At least one additional country-level investment project each FY (e.g., Antigua and
          Barbuda, Belize, Dominica, Dominican Republic).
      • Phase 3: FY24 onwards: other Caribbean countries.

   9. Phase I of the Program is designed to foster economic recovery and support marine and coastal resilience in
      Grenada, Saint Lucia and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines by strengthening the sustainability and
      competitiveness of two critical, interconnected sectors – tourism and fisheries – and one underlying enabling
      infrastructure service, waste management. Growth and jobs in these sectors have been heavily impacted by COVID,
      which has exacerbated challenges related to high debt levels, climate change, and natural disasters. To boost
      economic recovery, the Program will achieve its objectives by: (i) strengthening regional and national policies and
      institutional frameworks to bring back business and attract new investments; (ii) scaling up innovative financing
      mechanisms aimed at enhancing employment and productivity in the tourism, fisheries and waste management
      value chains through establishment of a regional MSME matching grant program16 and a regional climate-risk
      fisheries insurance scheme; (iii) supporting investment in coastal infrastructure to de-risk and leverage private
      investment in blue economy activities, and strengthen climate resilience; and, (iv) adopting a contingency
      emergency response mechanism to respond to future extreme weather events. While these challenges are regional
      in nature, addressing them requires interventions at both regional and national levels. Coordinating actions among
      the participating countries is critical to maximize synergies given their common dependence on tourism, shared
      marine ecosystems and fisheries resources, and shared goal of reducing marine pollution as well as climate
      vulnerability.

   10. COMPONENT 1: STRENGTHENING GOVERNANCE, POLICIES, AND CAPACITY BUILDING (US$9 m total: US$6 m IDA;
       US$3 m PROBLUE). This Component supports development of regional and national policies, strategies and
       institutions necessary to support low-carbon and climate resilient action, foster economic recovery and job
       creation, close the gender gap, and improve sustainable management of natural resources in turn contributing to
       the integrity and long-term health of coastal and marine natural resources and the ecosystem services they provide.

      E. Implementation

      Institutional and Implementation Arrangements

            14. The Program will be implemented at the national and regional level, under a joint framework. The
                participating countries are Grenada, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines with the OECS
                Commission as the regional entity. The implementation arrangements are designed to ensure clear
                ownership of project components, proper oversight, and to strengthen regional and inter-ministerial
                coordination among and within participating countries. At the national level, the Ministry of Finance
                (MoF) of Saint Lucia, Ministry of Infrastructure Development, Transport and Implementation (MOIID)
                in Grenada and the Ministry of Tourism and Sustainable Development (MTSD) in SVG will be
                responsible for implementation through a national PIU, in close collaboration with the implementing
                line ministries for each sector. At the regional level, the OECS Commission will hold primary
                responsibility for regional coordination, implementation of the regional MSME matching grants
                scheme and regional activities.

            15. The Regional Project Steering Committee (PSC) will retain oversight of the Program. The PSC will
                comprise a Permanent Secretary-level representative from the Ministry of Finance from each country,
                and a representative from the OECS Commission (Director General or delegate), who will be
                responsible for: (i) oversight of annual workplans, (ii) regional procurement decisions, (iii) discussion
                and agreement on regional policy issues related to the blue economy including guidance to ensure
                that any legislation agreed upon at regional level is expeditiously promulgated/adopted in each
                country, (iv) oversight of regional fiduciary matters; and (v) oversight of reporting requirements from
                National and Regional PIUs. The PSC will meet quarterly, and the chair of the PSC will rotate among
                the three participating countries with the OECS Commission acting as Secretariat and member of the
