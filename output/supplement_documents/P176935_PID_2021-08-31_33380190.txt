     B. Introduction and Context

  Country Context
 1.     Nigeria is central to the World Bank’s mission of eliminating global poverty. A multiethnic and diverse federation
 of 36 autonomous states and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT), with an abundance of resources, and a young and
 dynamic society, Nigeria is already Africa’s largest country (over 200 million people) and largest economy (nominal gross
 domestic product [GDP] of around US$450 billion in 2019) and has the potential to be a giant on the global stage.

 2.       The path to sustained economic growth has, however, been turbulent for Nigeria. Historically, between 1960
 and 1999, the average annual growth rate in GDP per-capita was close to zero – that is, the economic growth was barely
 able to keep pace with the growth in population. While the Nigerian economy experienced high growth rates at the turn
 of the century with an average annual growth in per-capita GDP of 5.2 percent between 2001-2010, growth markedly
 slowed down in the past decade to a low of 2.3 percent in 2011-2014 and negative 1.9 percent in the recent six years. In
 fact, for each of the years since 2015, Nigerian economy grew at a pace slower than its population, contracting in 2016
 and 2020. The initial spurt of growth was driven by growth in the non-oil sector of the economy, particularly services and
 non-oil export-oriented manufacturing, and was accompanied by a period of macroeconomic stability. However, oil
 continues to occupy a large share of the economy and shocks to the global oil market translates to volatility and
 vulnerability for Nigeria.

 3.      Consequently, a large segment of the population still lives in poverty and have low human capital development
 outcomes. With over 40 percent of its population (over 80 million people) in poverty, Nigeria is among countries with the
 largest number of people living below the poverty line.1 Poverty exhibits large spatial and geographic disparities: the
 poverty rate in the North East (70.2 percent) is more than seven times higher than in the South West (9.7 percent) and
 urban poverty (18 percent) is about a third of rural poverty rate (52 percent). In addition to the high poverty rate, an
 additional 25 percent of Nigerians are vulnerable to falling back into poverty.2 Besides monetary poverty, Nigeria ranked
 seventh lowest globally in the World Bank’s Human Capital Index in 2020.3 Fragility, conflict, and insecurity afflict many
 parts of the country, particularly in the northeast. Weak capacity plagues the public sector. To fulfill the government’s
 ambition to lift 100 million Nigerians out of poverty by 2030, Nigeria has to make tangible progress on multiple fronts, at
 both the federal and subnational levels and ensure proper protection of the poor and vulnerable.

 4.      COVID-19 further pushed the economy downwards, but the impacts have not been as severe as anticipated. In
 2020, Nigeria experienced its deepest recession in four decades due to pandemic related domestic restrictions as well as
 capital outflows, intensified risk aversion, low oil prices, and shrinking remittances. The economy contracted by 1.8
 percent and the per-capita GDP fell by 4.3 percent. Oil production contracted by 8.9 percent; non-oil manufacturing and
 construction fell by 3.9 percent; services fell by 2.2 percent; only the agricultural sector saw a modest growth of 2.2
 percent consistent with recent growth trends of the sector. But thanks to the low COVID-19 caseload in the country, the
 subsequent rapid easing of COVID-19 restrictions, a rebound in global oil prices and remittance inflows in the fourth

 5.      Yet, the pandemic is expected to have had long lasting detrimental impacts to the economy, particularly for the
 poor and vulnerable. The overall economic growth masks the detrimental impact that the pandemic has had in Nigeria.
 Within the first few months of the pandemic, the COVID-19 National Longitudinal Phone Survey (NLPS) found that
 employment had shrunk, reducing incomes for many families, particularly those operating a non-farm household
 enterprise. By June 2020, shrinking incomes and household welfare had pushed many to work in low quality commerce
 sector and many families continued to resort to extreme measures such as lowering their food consumption in response
 to shocks.

 6.       In addition to the COVID-19 related shocks, the poor and the vulnerable were also battling a related shock of
 high and increasing inflation. Throughout 2020, the rate of inflation rose steadily and reached a four-year high of 18.2
 percent (year-on-year) in March 2021 and has remained stubbornly around the same level. Surging food prices accounted
 for 63 percent of the rising inflation in 2020. Pandemic related restrictions on travel and trade, the ensuing disruption in
 food supply chain, combined with unfavorable weather, insecurity and conflict in food producing areas put upward
 pressures on food prices. Lack of credible monetary policy and resultant anticipatory consumption and investment
 behavior from firms and consumers exacerbated the upward pressure on prices. It is expected that high inflation pushed
 an additional 7 million Nigerians below the poverty line in 2020 alone. In November 2020, about 56 percent of the
 households in the COVID-19 NLPS reported that adults had skipped meals in the past month to cope with rising prices.
 Even if the inflation rate remains stable or slightly decreasing for the rest of 2021, it will continue driving more Nigerians
 into poverty.

 7.        Key reforms and proactive pandemic response helped Nigeria avoid the worst impacts of the pandemic .
 Deepening these reforms would help ensure a robust recovery. As part of its response effort, the government
 implemented several long-delayed policy reforms, often in the face of vocal opposition. Notably, the government: (i) took
 initial steps towards eliminating gasoline subsidies; (ii) started adjusting electricity tariffs towards more cost-reflective
 levels; (iii) cut nonessential spending and redirected resources towards the COVID-19 response effort at both the Federal
 and State levels; and (iv) increased public-sector transparency and enhanced debt management, in particular around the
 operations of the oil and gas sector. By creating additional fiscal space and maximizing the impact of the government’s
 limited resources, these measures were critical to protect the economy against a much deeper recession and to set the
 foundations for an earlier recovery. However, the incomplete nature of many critical reforms, as well as the potential of
 worse pandemic outbreak threatens Nigeria’s nascent recovery.

     Sectoral and Institutional Context

 8.      The levels of social protection in Nigeria, both in terms of financing and coverage, has been very low . Chronic
 poverty and recent shocks due to COVID-19 and partly related inflation shock have put around 100 million Nigerians, more
 than half the population, in poverty. However, expenditures on safety nets and coverage of safety net programs are
 extremely low. In 2016, Nigeria spent about 0.3 percent of its GDP on safety net programs, much lower than the global
 average, average for low-and-middle income countries, as well as comparator countries in the region. By 2018, only 17
 percent of households received any social assistance and fewer than 1 percent were covered by any cash transfers, much
 lower than the rate of poverty and vulnerability in the country. The coverage of social assistance among the poor and the

 9.       Apart from low coverage, the performance of the government social safety net programs is variable and can
 also be low, as measured by other dimensions of design and implementation. These areas include the adequacy of
 program benefits; links with active labor market programs for potentially improved self-employment and wage-
 employment outcomes; ex-ante or ex-post program coverage of households that experience adverse shocks and links with
 social insurance programs; and links with nutrition, health, and education programs and services for potentially improved
 human development outcomes for children.

 10.      The limited existing social protection programs in the country also remain fragmented . While institutional and
 programmatic developments are underway to address this, the social protection system remains fragmented. Programs
 suffer from overlapping mandates and objectives of the different agencies and line ministries supervising them, which
 often leads to a and duplication of efforts. However, the setting up and operations of the National Social Safety Nets
 Coordination Office (NASSCO) under the National Social Safety Nets Project (NASSP) has made significant contributions in
 the areas of targeting and coordinating social safety net related programs at the federal government level leveraging on
 the National Social Registry (NSR) and the Development Partners Group on social protection in Nigeria. Considerable
 efforts are still needed in coordinating the broader social protection programs in the country both at the national and sub-
 national levels.

 11.     Significant and persistent gender gaps exist in Nigeria, including in access to social protection services . Nigeria
 ranks 139 out of 156 countries in the 2021 Global Gap Index which measures gender gap on indicators of economic
 participation and opportunity, education, health and survival, and political empowerment.5 According to this study,
 gender gaps barely narrowed since 2006, and got worse in the past year. Large gender gaps are present in labor force
 participation (gap of 11 percentage points) and earnings for similar work (gap of 30 percent).6 Similarly, women lag in
 terms of financial inclusion with only 39 percent of adult women owning a financial account in 2020, compared to 51
 percent for men.7 Women’s participation in financial activities also skews towards informal mechanisms and channels.
 Lower formal financial penetration could result in lower access to social transfers. Additional institutional and cost barriers
 (such as distance and price) may further enlarge the gender gap in accessing services.8 Geographic variations as well as
 varying cultural and religious norms also exacerbate existing inequalities. Going by the Framework for Advancing
 Women’s Financial Inclusion in Nigeria9, financial and digital literacy, creation of financial access points closer to
 communities and homes through agents’ networks, digital financial services and fintech products that are women friendly
 and enabling environment for enrolment in identity service are very key to promote women’s financial inclusion.

 12.     There has been recent progress which shows the government’s commitment towards providing stronger social
 protection to its citizens. Recently, the government has invested in developing its social assistance system to help the
 chronic poor as well as those affected by recent shocks and crises. The government introduced a National Social Protection
 Policy (NSPP) Framework in 2017 which seeks to establish a minimum social floor for all Nigerian citizens with key
 objectives, among others, to reduce poverty among the poor and vulnerable, empower the poor and people vulnerable
 to economic shocks, and protect individuals and households from shocks. Gender-sensitivity remains one of the central

 developing and utilizing the NSR for adequate beneficiary selection of all social and labor market programs, improving
 coordination and integration between state and national registers, and establishing a strong information management
 system. Concurrently, a social protection coordinating platform was also established in the Office of the Vice President
 which was recently transferred to the newly created Federal Ministry of Humanitarian Affairs, Disaster Management, and
 Social Development (FMHADM). The platform oversees and coordinates social protection programs throughout the
 country. This was further accompanied by an increased budget allocation to social safety net interventions, particularly in
 response to COVID-19 and related shocks that the country is facing.

 13.      The World Bank has been a key partner in the government’s effort to expand and strengthen the social
 protection programs and systems, particularly through its support to different national programs: the National Social
 Safety Net Project (NASSP, P151488), Youth Employment and Social Support Operation (YESSO, P126964), Community and
 Social Development Program (CSDP, P090644) and the recently approved COVID-19 Action Recovery and Economic
 Stimulus (CARES P174114).. These programs, particularly the YESSO and NASSP, support the development of the NSR and
 launches the country’s flagship cash transfer program for the poor (regular-CT). As of June 2021, the NSR covered 8.3
 million households mostly in rural areas, out of which 2 million households are enrolled to receive the regular-CT. The NSR
 uses a combination of geographic and community-based targeting validated through Proxy Means Test (PMT) to identify
 and determine the poverty status and living standards of those captured in the registry. Only those identified as being in
 the bottom 60 percent in the NSR are eligible for the regular-CT. Through government’s own financing in addition to the
 World Bank financing through the NASSP project, the coverage of the regular-CT is expected to be 20 million individuals
 (4m households) over the course of the next year. The NASSP project, along with the recently closed YESSO, also
 strengthens the payment system, Management Information Systems (MIS), and Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) systems
 of social assistance programs. The NASSP has promoted gender and social inclusion interventions that has facilitated the
 empowerment of women and other vulnerable groups in accessing program benefit decision making on the use of cash
 transfer received at the household level. Citizen's engagement and social accountability is another important area where
 the program established a system to facilitate accountability measures in the delivery of the program intervention. Third
 Party Monitoring (TPM) implemented through independent Civil Society Organization (CSOs) and Grievance Redress
 Mechanism (GRM) were successfully rolled out.

 14.     The government is in the process of scaling up social protection in response to COVID-19. The lack of large-scale
 safety net programs and the lack of a social registry with sufficient coverage limited the ability of the government to
 provide quick relief to its citizens in response to COVID-19. By November 2020, just 6 percent of the households had
 received any cash assistance. But the government is currently scaling up NASSP to provide short-term COVID-19 cash
 transfers (COVID-CT) to potential beneficiaries identified through the Rapid Response Registry (RRR). The World Bank has
 supported the government in its initiative through a rapid restructuring of the NASSP project in April 2020 to provide
 financing to establish the RRR and to provide COVID-CT for 2 million urban and peri-urban households. The recently
 approved CARES program also supports targeted safety nets programs and strengthens social service delivery in addition
 to improving food security and facilitating recovery and capabilities of micro and small enterprises.

 15.      The establishment of the RRR and the launch of the COVID-CT required innovations on several fronts. In absence

 innovations: (i) 1,163 poorest urban wards with a population of over 20 million people were selected based on the ward
 level poverty maps produced by applying cutting-edge machine learning algorithms to granular satellite imagery data10;

 16.       In addition, more long-term improvements are necessary to improve the social protection landscape in the
 country. The following summarizes the key directions for a more effective social protection system:
       (i)     Greater and more sustainable government financing, including through efficient use of existing resources and
               better channeling of oil and non-oil revenues.
      (ii)     Stronger coordination and alignment in program design and implementation across government agencies and
               administrative levels. Specifically, the development and usage of integrated social registry and common
               delivery platforms should help with reducing fragmentation.
     (iii)     Development and usage of better data and data systems. Data from integrated registries will need to be
               actively used to understand the coverage, gaps in coverage, and fiscal planning of safety nets programs. Newer
               technologies and data can be used to expand coverage and improve targeting of safety net program. Policies
               will need to be informed by a strong monitoring and evaluation of safety net programs.
     (iv)      Improved resilience and shock responsiveness of the social protection system, and better linkages of safety
               net programs with other programs and services. Stronger linkages with labor market and other support
               services will pave a way for beneficiaries to exit out of poverty and related safety nets programs.

 17.      The project intends to expand and build upon the activities conducted through the NASSP to provide shock
 responsive safety nets and to extend regular social assistance to the poor and vulnerable. The project will provide regular
 cash transfers and livelihoods support targeted to the poor and vulnerable building upon the infrastructure of the NSR
 and the NASSP program. The project will also provide temporary cash transfers to citizens in targeted areas affected by
 recent economic shocks building upon the infrastructure and delivery platform of the RRR and the COVID-CT program.
 Together, the project will move Nigeria towards a shock responsive safety net program and strengthen the existing safety
 nets delivery systems through better data and information integration across various administrative levels and the usage
 of digital technologies to improve targeting, registration, payment, and information management systems.
  Relationship to CPF

 18.       The proposed follow-on operation to NASSP (NASSP II) is aligned with the Country Partnership Framework (CPF)
 for the Federal Republic of Nigeria FY 21 – FY 25 (World Bank Report #153873-NG). The CPF has four strategic Pillars: (i)
 strengthening the foundations of the public sector; (ii) investing in human capital; (iii) promoting jobs and economic
 transformation and diversification; and (iv) enhancing resilience. The project will contribute to most of the pillars of the
 CPF. Most directly, the expansion of the CT programs contributes towards increasing the coverage and effectiveness of
 social assistance, a core objective under Pillar 2. It also contributes to other core objectives of improving investments in
 human capital under Pillar 2. In addition, the strengthening of social assistance delivery system will strengthen the
 foundations of the public sector (Pillar 1); the productive inclusion support provided under the project promotes jobs
 (Pillar 3); and the proposed interventions to mitigate the impact of shocks will enhance resilience (Pillar 4).

  D. Concept Description

 20.     The project builds on the NASSP to provide strengthened social assistance support to Nigerian citizens .
 Component 1 provides shock responsive time-limited cash transfers to households identified through the NSR in rural
 areas and the expanded RRR platform in urban areas. Component 2 provides a package of regular cash transfers and
 relevant productive inclusion support services to the poor and vulnerable households identified through the NSR in rural
 areas. Component 3 improves the delivery system of social assistance / safety nets program through increased
 integration and use of digital technologies at various stages of the delivery chain. Component 3 also provides support to
 program management.

 Component 1. Economic Shock Responsive Cash Transfer (ESR-CT).

 Component 1 will provide temporary cash transfers to eligible beneficiaries in response to the current economic shocks
 affecting the country. The increase in inflation in the recent months, partly attributed to the disruption in supply chain of
 food and other essential consumption items due to COVID-19, has pushed millions of Nigerians into poverty and reduced
 the welfare of many more. The increase in prices affects residents in both rural and urban areas but the impacts are
 disproportionately higher for the poor and vulnerable.

 Component 2. Extended Regular Cash Transfer (ER-CT) for the poor and the vulnerable.

 21.      Component 2 will continue the social assistance provided to the poor and vulnerable under NASSP by extending
 the duration of the transfers to the same households and combine it with a package of support services to improve their
 livelihoods. The objective of this component is two-fold. First, a package of support services (including skills and
 entrepreneurship) packaged with cash transfers have been found to be more effective than individual services provided
 alone. Second, the package provides households with a productive inclusion support which can have a lasting impact on
 the households even after direct support ends. The NASSP is currently piloting various packages and service delivery
 options. In addition, a series of impact evaluations are evaluating the effectiveness of various support packages and service
 delivery options. Effective packages and service delivery options will be scaled-up under this project. The package of social
 assistance provided under the project will include: (i) basic cash transfers, (ii) conditional exit grants, and (iii) productive
 inclusion support services.

  Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
  .
 23.      The Project is assessed as low for Environmental risk and Moderate for Social risk. The Project will not give rise
 to or implement any civil works nor land acquisition. However, the risk of exclusion from cash transfer is likely to transpire
 due to (i) peoples’ inability to register for the benefit by phone, (ii) the fact that the poorer and more vulnerable ones do
 not have a bank account for transfer cash transfers and, (ii) lack or poor network connectivity likely to adversely affect
 smooth self-registration and targeting process. Nonetheless, the project will put specific support to ensure that persons
 with disabilities, marginalized groups, and persons residing in IDP camps, and ethnic minorities are not excluded from the
 intervention. Other potential risks include the (i) risk of elite capture of cash delivery to project beneficiaries, (ii)
 inadequate stakeholders’ consultation/engagement, (iii) grievances and complaints about service delivery, (iv) project
 induced GBV/SEA/SH risks. The Project would mitigate these risks by implementing; (i) Social Accountability mechanisms
 to minimize the risk of elite capture and enhance transparency and accountability, (ii) by developing a Stakeholders
 Engagement Plan (SEP) to ensure robust and ongoing stakeholders’ engagements throughout the project cycle and,
 (iii)responsive and efficient GRM and the respective GRCs at various levels.

 24.      The NASSP Project established mechanisms (such as Project-level Grievance Redress Mechanism (GRM), SEA/SH
 Risk Action Plan, Citizens Engagement, Communication approaches and TPMs) that will be adopted under NASSP Phase
 to ensure that lessons learned, and implementation experiences are appropriately incorporated to address the potential
 social risks and strengthen accountability. Other impacts are (I) the risk of getting infected with COVID 19 if the crowds
 are not properly managed during the cash collection at financial entities, Considering this risk, the project will mainstream
 attention to occupational and community health and safety by building on good industry international practice (especially
 WHO), and ensure that points of payments are located countrywide through any bank, and in addition by phasing payment
 days over weeks according to the recipient’s ID number to reduce the risk of large gatherings in any one point of payment
 at the same time. Communications about payments and signage around payment points will also be used to reinforce
 social distancing and other behavior that supports prevention The GON will follow WHO guidelines for social distancing
 and a proactive community outreach when delivering cash transfers and also expanding the use of bank accounts all over
 the country
