   B. Introduction and Context

   Country Context

   1. Central African Republic (CAR) is one of the poorest and most fragile countries in the world. In 2019, per capita GDP
      averaged US$468)—much lower than the average of US$1,130 in countries affected by fragility, conflict, and violence
      (FCV) in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). The extreme poverty rate remained high at 71.4 percent in 2020.

   2. CAR’s economy is small (US$2.2 billion GDP), driven by agriculture and extractives, and heavily dependent on
      imports. Economic growth in CAR has historically been driven by these sectors. Mining and forestry have dominated
      the export market (consisting primarily of diamonds and rough wood). Asia and the EU are CAR’s main export markets
      (~90 percent) with China representing 54.3 percent of CAR’s total exports, while exports within Africa represent only
      14 percent of total exports. Official exports in 2018 totaled US$99.4 million. Agriculture remains a major source of
      formal and informal employment - the sector accounts for 77.3 percent of total employment and 39 percent of GDP.
      In recent years, the services sector share of GDP has increased and contributes approximately as much as agriculture
      to GDP. The agriculture and services sectors were the main supply-side drivers of economic growth in 2019, growing
      at 3.1 percent and 2.3 percent, respectively.1

   3. Since gaining independence in 1960, the country has faced successive episodes of conflict and violence that have led
      to enormous development challenges. The Bangui Forum in 2015 began the process of stabilization after the 2013
      political and military crisis. In 2019 a peace agreement was signed which shifted the efforts towards recovery and
      development and, despite repetitive violation by several armed groups, contributed to a significant decrease in
      violence. However, the most recent contentious presidential elections (December 2020) have exacerbated tensions
      and led to the return of some violence in the provinces. The government has, among other measures to bring back
      peace, planned to organize the Republican dialogue.

   4. The formal private sector is small, constrained by structural challenges, including limited access to finance and poor
      infrastructure, poor skills, gaps in legal and regulatory frameworks governing economic activities, and a fragile
      security environment. CAR has among the lowest education and gender equality indicators in the world. The poor
      quality of primary education, low participation rates in secondary school education for girls, and high levels of violence
      against women and girls, with 11,000 reported incidents each year (2016), 74 percent of which involve children, remain
      pressing challenges.



   5. The COVID-19 pandemic has had a negative impact on the economy. The pandemic drew significant government
      resources that could have been spent addressing the country’s shortcomings. With the onset of containment
      measures, supply chains were interrupted which had a negative impact on all sectors, increasing costs and business
      risks. The resulting economic downturn, as in other parts of the region, provoked widespread unemployment, shrank
      domestic markets and decreased liquidity. While the country received additional resources from donors during the
      pandemic and partly recovered through adaptation, the new post-election instability ahs resulted in backsliding. The
      country’s economic growth has been revised downward to 0.8 percent for 2020 (−1.1 percent in per-capita terms), 3.6
      percent below pre-COVID-19 the Projections. The fiscal position is estimated to have deteriorated as tax revenues have
      declined and public expenditures risen due to the pandemic. The World Bank estimates that the pandemic pushed
      approximately 70,000 additional people into extreme poverty in 2020.2

   6. CAR is also highly vulnerable to climate change, ranked 180 out of 181 countries in the 2020 ND-GAIN Index3. CAR
      has a population of just over 4.7 million people (2019) with an annual population growth rate of 1.7 percent.
      Approximately 41.4 percent of the population currently lives in urban areas. This is projected to increase to 48 percent
      and 60 percent of the population by 2030 and 2050, respectively. The high levels of poverty and low-degree of
      development in CAR limit capacity of poor households and communities to manage climate risk, increasing their
      vulnerability to climate-related shocks. Impacts of climate change will affect the poorest and most vulnerable the most
      and hinder CAR’s development agenda and efforts at economic sustainability and political stability, further impacting
      poor and vulnerable groups. Adverse climatic events may destroy infrastructure, interrupt business and the functioning
      of the government. Floods are a recurring and devastating natural disaster and are responsible for the largest share of
      economic and human losses from natural disasters in CAR.




    Sectoral and Institutional Context

   Business Environment and Investment Climate

   7. CAR is one of the most challenging places in the world to do business. Businesses lament the unpredictability of the
      business environment with respect to settling disputes, predatorial behavior of tax authorities and a casual respect
      for legal agreements. Reform workstreams funded by the project will target some of these problems. The Bertelsmann
      Transformation Index4 which examines economic governance in transitioning and developing economies classified CAR
      as weak (<3 out of 10) in key dimensions of its business environment including markets and competition, and protection
      of private property. Furthermore, CAR was ranked 151st out of 160 countries in the World Bank’s 2018 Logistics
      Performance Index. CAR also ranked 133rd out of 152 economies in UNIDO’s Competitive Industrial Performance Index
      which measures how successful a country’s industries are at producing and selling their goods on domestic and foreign
      markets, and subsequently, how they contribute to structural change and development.

   The Public-Private Institutions Supporting Private Sector Development

   8. Key ministries and public organizations entrusted to improve the business environment, implement reforms, and
      offer training, information and services to businesses lack capacity. The Ministry of Small and Medium Enterpreneurs
      (SMEs) and Private Sector Development and the Ministry of Commerce and Industry have limited resources to
      implement their respective workplans, and work with businesses and other stakeholders to understand and be
      responsive to their needs. These needs include facilitating import/export and investment promotion, market research,
      and information and facilitating implementation of regulations and economic reforms that help businesses.

   9. The government’s efforts to address obstacles to private sector development have had limited impact, and the
      ecosystem consisting of public and privately funded services for SMEs is embryonic and functions poorly. The
      government established the SME and Artisan’s Business Center (Centre d’Assistance aux Petites et Moyennes

   11. Professional associations, GICA which advocates for the few, formal businesses and the National Union of Businesses
       (Union Nationale du Patronat Centrafricain- UNPC) representing the interests of 7 national professional unions
       participate in the work of the CMCAA to inform the reform agenda. With its 149 members, the Central African
       Businesswomen’s Association (Association des Femmes d’Affaires Centrafricaines – AFAC) is a prominent advocate for
       women-owned MSMEs and provides training to its members and others. Since 2013, provisions favorable to women
       have been adopted.6 These organizations and other businesses associations provide services such as networking
       opportunities, training, advocacy and information to businesses.

   Firms and Informality

   12. Successive crises, and the 2013 Crisis in particular, have had a pernicious, negative impact on the private sector.
       Infrastructure, institutions, and social capital were destroyed or negatively impacted, all of which have made
       businesses less competitive, increased costs, and decreased the availability of business support services. Many
       businesses closed, or lost assets, people, with the skills and means to do so, left the country impacting both the
       government and public institutions as well as the private sector. The majority of MSMEs have significant deficits in
       business and management skills. They do not employ adequately modern business practices (accounting, budgeting,
       planning, marketing techniques, technology). This impacts their ability to access credit, develop linkages with more
       sophisticated companies, access markets, and improve their businesses. These dimensions of firms, and, thus the
       households of those they employ, more vulnerable to shocks arising from conflict, climate change or other adverse
       conditions.

   13. CAR’s informal sector represents approximately 60-80 percent of the output produced by the private sector.
       Extensive discussions across government ministries, businesses, banks, and business associations over the past 5 years
       revealed a range of causes for informality. The primary causes cited were the avoidance of taxes and predatorial
       behavior by officials, and the low value placed on business opportunities foregone by remaining informal such as
       accessing financing, recovery of value-added taxes, accessing tenders and using more sophisticated business
       arrangements and contracts. Also prominent were observations of the inefficiency of government agencies charged

   14. A sample of 135 MSMEs was surveyed in a 2017 study that identified sectors of opportunity for MSMEs identified
       constraints to their development and made recommendations to alleviate them.5 This diagnostic found that MSMEs
       face critical deficits in skills, access to finance and platforms for collaboration horizontally and vertically. While over 90
       percent of MSMEs in Bangui surveyed had a bank account or an account with a microcredit institution, only 22 percent
       had obtained a credit of any kind over the past 3 years. For smaller cities, these figures are much worse. Estimated
       financing needs based on the repayment capacity of MSMEs in the city of Bangui were US$48.7 million while the
       outstanding loans and other financing amounted to US$9.6 million (May 19, 2017).

   15. Furthermore, there are challenges faced by women with respect to business and employment. Only 6.46 percent of
       firms have majority female ownership compared to 13.2 percent for Sub-Sahara Africa. Vulnerable employment among
       women is 96.5 percent and among men is 88.8 percent in CAR for 2019, indicating a gap of 7.7 percent and has
       worsened among women since 1991. The rate of vulnerable employment is 5.9 percent higher for women in CAR than
       the average rate for women in Sub-Saharan Africa.7

   Financial Sector

   16. CAR’s financial sector is underdeveloped. The financial system is concentrated in Bangui. The country has 4 banks,8 8
       microfinance institutions (MFIs)9 and 3 insurance companies. The sector is supervised and regulated at the regional
       level by the CEMAC regional institutions including BEAC (the regional central bank), COBAC (the regional banking
       supervisor) and COSUMAF (the regional capital markets regulator). The banking sector remains highly concentrated
       with one bank controlling close to half of the banking sector assets. Banks’ balance sheets are growing and represent
       now US$556 million, 17 percent more than last year. Deposits represent US$407 million, 22.9 percent more than last
       year. Credit is also increasing and represents US$297 million with approximately 15,000 active accounts. Orange offers
       basic services of cash-in and cash-out transactions on mobile money account, transfers between OM accounts, and a
       few payment services. As of October 2019, OM transactions were US$6 million up from US$4.4 million in September
       2019.

   17. Access to finance remains one of the largest constraints for private sector development in particular for SMEs.
       Despite a regionally integrated financial sector, banks display poor intermediation indicators. Private credit as a
       percentage of GDP represents only 12.3 percent. According to a recent BEAC report, almost 78 percent of the credit
       goes to large enterprises, 22.3 percent to SMEs and 7.35 percent to households. The low access to finance numbers is

   The Skills Deficit

   19. Given the organization’s role in vocational training, the effectiveness of the Central African Agency for Vocational
       Training and Employment (L’Agence Centrafricaine pour la Formation Professionnelle et l’Emploi- ACFPE) is relevant
       to private sector development. The ACFPE is funded out of a tax on salaries and offers training to businesses and
       individuals but has difficulty funding modernization of the agency’s facilities and expanding in line with demand.
       Discussions with the ACFPE, indicated that the businesses seeking to fill positions cannot find the training and business
       skills they need (accounting, management, insurance, finance, administration) amongst available candidates as a result
       of the:1) a lack of labor market analysis prior to developing training programs; 2) insufficient coordination between
       training centers and companies; and 3) insufficient opportunities for training trainers in CAR.

   20. Approximately 23 percent of Central Africans aged 15 to 19 years old and 30 percent of aged 20 to 24 years old were
       neither in education, employment nor in training in 2018. This proportion is much higher among girls (29 percent and
       34 percent respectively) than boys (17 percent and 26 percent respectively). Among youth who are engaged in an
       economic activity, agriculture is the predominant sector (77 precent), especially outside Bangui.

   21. A World Bank mission in November 2019 held a series of direct discussions with businesses of all sizes. Businesses
       most often trained staff in house even when hiring graduates of local technical and vocational education and training
       (TVET) providers or hired skilled tradespeople from neighboring countries to perform a range of functions:
       construction, electrical engineering, masonry, carpentry, welding, plumbing, etc. These skills are in high demand.
       Businesses also indicated that TVET providers often lacked the assets to properly instruct students in the evolving
       techniques used across sectors. The speed with which technology changes and the impact of this phenomena on all
       sectors makes it difficult for poorly resourced TVET to renew equipment, tools, and assets to remain current and
       relevant. This problem, along with the difficulty of finding and retaining experienced instructors leads to deficits in the
       skills businesses need to grow (In most African countries, the TVET system is broken, instructors are not incentivized
       to train, their salaries are low, curriculum outdated, etc.).

   22. Apprenticeship is considered one effective way to develop job-specific skills; however, the apprenticeship system is
       not structured and remains almost entirely informal in CAR. Traditional apprenticeship constitutes the predominant

        avenue of skills acquisition in CAR, just like in most of SSA countries10. In the informal sector, a young worker with no
        qualifications often enters an informal enterprise as an apprentice and will reach a higher status only if he/she is
        promoted or manages to get hired as a skilled worker in another enterprise. Field visits of small-scale enterprises in
        Bangui showed that there was no contractual arrangement, oral or written, between masters and apprentices
        concerning the duration of the apprenticeship, nor other dimensions. There is no recognized certification provided to
        apprentices upon completion of their training. Finally, apprentices only benefit from on-the-job training, but they do
        not benefit from any theoretical training nor any foundational skills training programs.

   23. As a result, Central African firms often are unable to find job-specific skills necessary for their development in the
       labor market. Mechanics and electricians for maintaining and repairing equipment (elevators & generators) and truck
       drivers, welders were cited as examples in interviews conducted for the CAR Skills Training and Youth Employability
       (STYE) study. To address this skill shortage, large companies recruit outside CAR, while MSMEs use traditional
       apprenticeships (on-the-job training in an informal setting).

  D. Project Description

  Component 1: Investment Reforms and Facilitation (US$4 million equivalent)

  24. Component 1 will fund TA needed to implement priority reforms to improve CAR’s investment climate. The objective
      is to improve policies, regulations, and support government organizations that are mandated to enable private sector
      development. Related agencies include the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, the CMCAA and the Ministry of SMEs
      and Private Sector Development.11 TA and capacity building for these organizations will be based on diagnostics
      performed under the PPA. These diagnostics will focus on the strategy, organizational structure, mandates and
      responsibilities, operational plans of public organizations contributing to enable private investment and providing
      services to businesses. Using regional and global good practices, they will identify gaps, make recommendations, and
      propose policy and regulatory improvements, as well as training and other actions to optimize the government’s overall
      schema of how they enable investors and businesses. The Project will then fund the implementation of priority reforms
      committed to by the key agencies, including legal, regulatory, institutional, and administrative improvements.
      Implementation action plans, including TA and consulting services on specific reform topics, training courses for staff,
      and equipment will be provided to these institutions, aligned with clearly defined performance and results measures.

  25. Building on previous diagnostics and ongoing policy dialog, a preliminary list of activities to be supported by
      Component 1 of the Project may include:
          • Mapping and recommendation on the organizational structure of investment-related institutions;
          • Formulation of investment policy and targeted investment promotion strategy;
          • Studies, TA, transaction advisory to support the PPP Unit and PPP delivery;
          • Implementation regulations and decrees for the Investment Code, commercial laws and laws on SMEs;
          • Reform of sectoral regulations for the agriculture12, construction, and ICT sectors to remove key obstacles to
              investment;
          • Public-private dialogue consultation and validation activities related to the reform process;
          • Maintaining, updating, and making available the Practical Guide of Business Regulations.13

  26. Exact priority reforms and supporting activities will be finalized after the PPA diagnostics, around effectiveness or soon
      after. The project will fund the following tasks led by the CMCAA in collaboration with implicated government agencies
      as required: 1) identification of best practices for business environment improvements, 2) development of reform action
      plans, 3) legal experts for drafting of laws, regulations and implementation decrees, and 4) Public-private dialogue
      consultation and validation activities.

  Component 2: Access to Credit (US$9 million equivalent)

  27. Access to credit is highly constrained in CAR and a risk sharing mechanism is necessary to incentivize the banking sector
      to serve existing and future SMEs. SMEs are perceived as high risk by the financial sector due to several shortcomings
      including asymmetry of information and weak credit infrastructure; lack of collateral; limited managerial skills of current
      and prospective entrepreneurs; and weak collateral enforcement regime. Facing these limitations, banks apply high
      interest rates and significant collateral requirements (150 percent and above of the value of the loan) on SME loans to
      better manage the inherent credit risk. As a result, SMEs have limited opportunities to access formal finance in affordable
      terms. In order incentivize banks better serve SMEs, the government of CAR is in the process of establishing a Partial
      Credit Guarantee (PCG) scheme (Box 1). A well calibrated PCG with robust capitalization, governance and operational

  Subcomponent 2.1: Set Up the Partial Credit Guarantee Mechanism (US$4 million equivalent)

  29. Subcomponent 2.1 will finance the procurement of goods and technical assistance services to ensure the
      implementation of the PCG is compliant with global good practices. The vision of CAR’s government is to establish a
      PCG with i) robust corporate governance systems; ii) efficient and transparent operational procedures; iii) sustainable
      business model (striking the balance between effective guarantee coverage ratio and price to incentivize banks to use
      the product while at the same time minimizing moral hazard); iv) independent supervision from the banking sector
      regulator; and v) effective financial and M&E reporting systems. During the first half of 2021, the government has been
      working on the bylaws and some internal regulations of the PCG with the support of the UNDP and the World Bank
