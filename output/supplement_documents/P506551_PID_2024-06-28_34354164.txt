    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context

1. While Tunisia’s economy has remained resilient, the country is navigating a delicate economic situation. Tunisia has
   one of the most diversified economy in Africa, with a historically large services sector that has grown over the past
   decade.1 In 2023, significant export gains came from hotels and restaurants (+12.8 percent) thanks to the tourism
   recovery, and from mechanical and electric sectors (+5.8 percent).2 Export growth, driven by high-complexity products
   like electrical machinery and medical apparatuses, has outpaced overall economic growth in recent years, becoming
   crucial to the economy.3 On the other hand, severe drought, tight financing conditions and the modest pace of reform
   implementation slowed economic growth in 2023, leaving the economy below its pre-Covid level and marking one of
   the slowest recoveries in the MENA region. This has led to higher unemployment and lower labor force participation,
   with unemployment reaching 16 percent in Q4 2023 and labor force participation rate dropping to 46 percent, below
   pre-pandemic levels.4 Further, the growth slowdown amplifies the challenges Tunisia faces in covering its external
   financing needs despite an improving external balance, and keeps the budget under pressure as the low growth affects
   tax revenues. 5

    2. Tunisia’s economic challenges are compounded by its acute vulnerability to climate change, making climate
       resilience and the green transition critical to its future economic development. Tunisia’s location makes it one of
       the countries most exposed to climate change in the Mediterranean region.6 If no urgent measure is taken to address
       climate change-related risks, particularly water shortages, the Tunisian economy could shrink by 3.4 percent of GDP
       by 2030 (close to TD 5.6 billion a year or US$1.8 billion in net present value). On the other hand, adequately
       addressing climate change and decarbonizing the electricity sector could increase the GDP by 9 percent, reduce
       poverty by 12 percent and cut energy emissions by 80 percent by 2030.7

    3. Economic challenges and vulnerability to climate change are affecting Tunisians unequally. Inequality, as measured
       by the Gini Index, increased from 32.8 to 33.7 between 2015 and 2021, mainly driven by disparities within urban and

       meet labor market demands, with projections indicating substantial job creation, including 70,000 additional jobs in
       energy by 2035, 65,000 in green hydrogen alone by 2035, and 400,000 more by 2050.


    Sectoral and Institutional Context

    5. Tunisia’s tertiary enrolment is on par with the MENA regional average and is expected to further increase in the
       coming decade. As of 2022, tertiary gross enrolment ratio is close to 40 percent in Tunisia – compared to 41 percent
       in MENA.10 The number of students enrolled in tertiary education more than tripled between 1995 and 2005 due to
       the massive expansion of secondary education and an increase in school-aged population, before decreasing over
       the last decade, largely due to declining demographic patterns, and then slightly increasing again in the last two years,
       reaching 305,635 students in 2022/2023.11 According to projections from the National Institute of Statistics for the
       19-24 age group in 2040, this trend is expected to persist over the next five years, but the number of students will
       see exponential growth, reaching an unprecedented peak in the second half of the next decade.12 Most students are
       enrolled in public institutions, although the share of students enrolled in private tertiary education has rapidly
       increased in recent years, from 11.5 percent in 2017/18 to 14.7 percent in 2022/23. Women make up the majority
       of both the student body (66 percent) and faculty (55 percent of researchers).

    6. Tunisia has accomplished several critical successes in higher education over the past decade. Major achievements
       include (i) high female representation, both among students and faculty; (ii) significant strides in higher education
       quality, as reflected in improved international rankings, accreditation and ISO-certifications; (iii) a strong
       commitment to equity, whether at the regional level through the opening of universities in interior regions or among
       students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds through a widespread access to scholarships and student
       services; (iv) enhanced outreach to the private sector through co-constructed programs, internships, applied learning

 7. Nonetheless, remaining challenges need to be addressed for higher education to play its role in the country’s
    transition to a green and knowledge economy. Specifically, several systematic challenges exist including: (i) low
    employability of graduates, partly due to the lack of flexibility for HEIs to quickly adapt their programs in collaboration
    with industry, thus not contributing to a high skilled labor force and creating dissatisfaction among unemployed
    graduates; (ii) relatively insufficient graduates in key priority sectors including renewable energy, water, digital skills
    and health to support the country transition towards a green and knowledge economy; (iii) a high share of repeaters,
    with almost one out of four (22 percent) students repeating at least one grade; (iv) inequity across students from
    different regions, genders, and socio-economic backgrounds and between coastal and interior universities and (v)
    insufficient autonomy and accountability for results of public universities, hindering greater efficiencies and
    performance improvements.

 8. Research holds a critical place in Tunisia, as the country ranks among Africa's top three countries in research
    output, but it has not yet had a significant impact on the socio-economic environment. Over the last two decades,
    the number of scientific publications per million inhabitants rose eight-fold, from 100 in 2001 to close to 800 in
    2022.13 Tunisia ranks 79 out of 132 countries on the World Intellectual Property Organization’s (WIPO) Global
    Innovation Index 2022, and 9th among Arab countries. Tunisia is particularly well-ranked in terms of human capital,
    research, knowledge and technology production – reaching the fifth rank in terms of the share of STEM graduates.
    On the other hand, Tunisia has opportunities to enhance its research sophistication and business environment,
    university-industry collaborations in R&D and knowledge absorption.


     Relationship to CPF

 9. The proposed project would contribute to all three high-level outcomes (HLO) of the new Country Partnership
    Framework (CPF) for Tunisia 2023-2026. The Country Partnership Framework (CPF) lays out the World Bank Group’s
    strategy in Tunisia for FY23–27, building on Tunisia’s 2023-25 development plan, the Tunisia Vision 2035, the World
    Bank’s Systematic Country Diagnostic as well as the Tunisia Country Climate and Development Report, with the
    overarching objective to support the Tunisian government’s development plan to expand the economy to benefit all,
    create quality jobs, and boost resilience to climate change. The new project will support all three HLOs, namely: (i)
    Strengthened human capital (HLO 2) by expanding access to employable programs and enhancing higher education
    quality, and by contributing to improving health services (ii) Improved climate resilience (HLO 3) by educating
    graduates with skills for driving the energy transition and climate adaptation in Tunisia's economy and (iii) More and
    better private sector jobs (HLO 1) through an economic sector-based approach, focusing on providing higher skills and
    innovation for priority sectors like digital, green, and blue economy. The proposed project would also aim to boost

To improve the employability of students and strengthen the quality and governance of tertiary education

 Key Results (From PCN)
10. PDO achievement will be measured by a subset of the preliminary PDO indicators proposed below. Four PDO
    indicators maximum will be selected and refined during preparation:
       1. Increased enrolment in degree programs in priority disciplines (enrolment disaggregated by gender, i.e.
           female enrolment in programs with higher female employment outcomes) – [Employability]
       2. Number/percentage of priority programs with graduate employment tracking system in place [Employability]
       3. Number of programs with international accreditation (by disciplines) [Quality]
       4. Number/percentage of HEIs/faculties with programs performance analysis in strategic plans [Governance]

D. Concept Description
11. The PDO will be achieved through a set of activities grouped into the following two components:

    •     Component 1 aims to boost tertiary institutions to deliver quality and relevant programs and better student
          services, via a competitive funding mechanism in close partnership with industry. More specifically, it will focus
          on 1. supporting the expansion and improvement of programs in high-employable, high-priority areas;
          2. supporting institutions to provide better teaching and learning environment to students, including through
          upgraded infrastructure and greening of campuses, and through student support and welfare services and
          3. supporting training, education and applied research for climate adaptation, mitigation, and resilience. As such,
          Component 1 aligns with all three axes of the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research strategy,
          namely Axis 1 focusing on strengthening qualified and responsible human capital for intelligent and inclusive
          development; Axis 2 on scientific research and innovation, oriented towards the future needs of the economy and
          society as well as Axis 3 on an improved university life to promote student autonomy and foster creativity.

    •     Component 2 aims to strengthen higher education and scientific research system, governance, and digital
          capacities – thus complementing component 1 and laying the foundation to its success. The project will provide
          specific support to higher education and scientific research institutions to enhance their governance and project
          management capabilities, including through the development and implementation of their 5-year strategic plans
          and the operationalization of the Public Establishment of a scientific and technological nature status (Statut
          d’Etablissement Public à caractère Scientifique et Technologique or EPST). The project will also support the digital
          transformation of the system -- both at the central and at university level. Finally, it will aim to strengthen system-
          wide governance, including through support to the Tunisian Agency for Evaluation and Accreditation (in French,
          Agence Tunisienne d’Evaluation et d’Accreditation, ATEA). Component 2 aligns with the transversal axis on
          governance of the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research strategy and lays the foundation to ensure
          the success of component 1.
