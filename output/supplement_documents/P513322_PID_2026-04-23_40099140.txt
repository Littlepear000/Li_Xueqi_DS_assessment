                              1.     The Social Protection Project for Inclusion, Resilience, Innovation, and Transformation (SPIRIT) is a standalone
                              Investment Project Financing operation with Performance-Based Conditions (PBCs) that aims to enhance the
                              integration, access, and inclusiveness of Ukraine's social protection system. The proposed SPIRIT project will be financed
                              by an IBRD loan of US$500 million supported by a bilateral guarantee from the Government of the United Kingdom (UK),
                              an IBRD loan of US$360 million backed by the credit enhancement from the Advancing Needed Credit Enhancement for
                              Ukraine (ADVANCE Ukraine) Trust Fund supported by the Government of Japan,1 and expected grant funding of US$20
                              million from the Ukraine Relief, Recovery, Reconstruction, and Reform Trust Fund (URTF).2

                              B. Introduction and Context

                              lasting social effects that are most acutely felt by vulnerable households. Over four years of hostilities have resulted in
                              significant civilian casualties, widespread destruction of critical infrastructure, and massive population displacement. As

                          Sectoral and Institutional Context

                          3.      The improvements in social protection supported by the operation will help Ukraine provide relief to vulnerable
                          groups, protect human capital, build resilience, and support a sustained recovery for millions of Ukrainians. Ukraine
                          faces simultaneous crises that exceed the structural capacity of its social protection system. Poverty has risen sharply; a
                          substantial share of the population requires assistance, and massive displacement, large-scale disability, and the
                          prospective return of veterans have placed unprecedented demands on a system designed for a different era. These
_For Official Use Only_




                          challenges are particularly acute in three interconnected domains—social assistance, social services, and disability
                          support—where insufficient integration, accessibility gaps, and limited inclusiveness constrain the system's ability to
                          respond. Because these deficiencies directly affect the living conditions and opportunities of millions of Ukrainians, the
                          reforms supported by the operation can deliver a rapid, tangible impact.

                          4.     Ukraine’s social protection system has shown remarkable resilience and reform momentum despite the
                          ongoing invasion—demonstrating genuine political ownership that is unusual in conflict-affected settings. Social
                          protection expenditures represent the second-largest item in the state budget for 2025-2026, accounting for
                          approximately 15 percent of GDP, with social assistance comprising around 2 percent. The system continued to deliver
                          uninterrupted payments to pensioners, IDPs, low-income households, and other vulnerable groups, reaching 60-80
                          percent of the population with at least one social protection measure. The GoU sustained and accelerated its reform
                          agenda, introducing new social assistance programs, modernizing social services legislation, developing a national
                          roadmap for disability assessment reform, and advancing digital systems and registries to ensure timely support. Despite
                          system-wide challenges, the government introduced pilots to test a new model for financing and delivering social
                          services. As a first step toward a more diversified model—one that complements local capacities with centrally funded
                          services—the GoU launched pilots to finance priority social services for vulnerable groups from the state budget.

                          5.     Despite these strengths, social assistance and social services remain fragmented or underprovided, with large
                          exclusion rate of vulnerable households and no effective coordination between these types of support to help people
                          out of poverty. Cash transfers substantially exceed expenditures on social services programs, and the centralized cash
                          benefit assistance system is not sufficiently coordinated with decentralized social services. The social services system
                          suffers from critically low coverage, a financing model biased toward institutional care, and a governance gap between
                          centrally led reforms and local implementation capacity. The decentralization reform shifted responsibility for planning,
                          organizing, financing, and delivering basic social services to municipalities (territorial communities), but the invasion has
                          severely strained local capacities and budgets. Local authorities require a functional marketplace and a strong regulatory
                          framework that allows them to purchase services from various providers, including non-governmental organizations and
                          the private sector. Social assistance payment levels are pegged to the outdated Subsistence Minimum (SM). It does not

                          capture citizens’ needs and is used to calculate over 180 types of payments, many of which have nothing to do with
                          poverty. As a result, benefit levels fall short of actual living costs and fail to adequately address real needs. If these
                          challenges are not addressed, they will continue to weaken the system's effectiveness in combating poverty, increase

                          effective than restoring discontinued functions.

                          6.     People with disabilities lack an integrated package of support that addresses functional, social, and economic
                          needs. The disability support system is fragmented across various government agencies, which implement over 200
                          benefits and top-ups without a unified framework linking assessment, benefits, services, rehabilitation, assistive
                          technologies, and employment support. Currently, only 18 percent of persons with disabilities are employed—a 32
                          percentage-point gap compared to the European Union average. Financing remains oriented toward categorical benefits
                          and institutional care, with weaker mechanisms for individualized and community-based services. Lack of effective
                          interinstitutional coordination and the separation of benefits and services across programs further complicate access for
                          people with disabilities, limiting the system’s ability to address individual needs effectively. To improve the disability
                          support, Ukraine is transitioning from a medicalized disability assessment model to an integrated, person-centered
                          approach aligned with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF).

                              D. Project Description

                              9.       The project supports a comprehensive package of GoU's reforms designed to enhance Ukraine's social
                              protection system, focusing on integration, access, and inclusiveness. The project addresses four key developmental
                              challenges: (i) reducing social assistance fragmentation and introducing effective integration with social services to
                              strengthen poverty reduction; (ii) enhancing the capacity of the social services system at both central and local levels;
                              (iii) facilitating a shift toward a human rights-based disability support model aligned with international standards; and
                              (iv) introducing shock-responsive features to the safety net that enable rapid enrollment of crisis-affected populations
                              and unlock donor humanitarian financing that government systems currently lack the capacity to absorb. The activities
                              funded by SPIRIT will target key areas of the social protection system, including social assistance, social services, and
                              social protection for people with disabilities. SPIRIT will also invest in developing digital systems, community-based social
                              infrastructure, and enhancing the capacity of government agencies, local governments, and social workers to implement
                              the reforms supported by the project. With financial support from the UK's Foreign, Commonwealth and Development
                              Office (FCDO) and in close collaboration with UNICEF, the World Bank provides technical assistance (TA) to the GoU to
                              develop and implement SPIRIT-supported reforms.3 The proposed project has two components.

                              Component 1

                          12.    PBC 2 supports a significant shift toward an integrated model of benefits and social services delivery through a
                          case management approach. The Ministry of Social Policy, Family, and Unity of Ukraine (MoSPFU) and the Bank team
                          are developing an integrated case management model that links BSA program beneficiaries to essential social services,
                          drawing on best practices from similar models implemented with World Bank support in other countries. The current
                          centralized social assistance system lacks effective integration with decentralized social services and offers no meaningful
                          pathways out of welfare dependency. The model under development aims to consolidate the efforts of social protection
                          and employment agencies at both central and local levels, with a focus on proactively identifying families in need —
                          particularly those in crisis — and preventing them from falling into the poverty trap. This approach is expected to
                          strengthen poverty reduction efforts by helping low-income and vulnerable households achieve greater self-sufficiency
                          and reduce their dependency on benefits.

                          13.    PBCs 3 and 4 will focus on structural improvements in Ukraine’s social services system. These reforms aim to
                          establish an effective system that delivers integrated, high-quality, and user-friendly state-funded social services to an
                          increasing number of vulnerable individuals. To address persistent coverage gaps arising from decentralized financing
                          and invasion-related fiscal constraints - which have led to uneven service availability across communities – the GoU is
                          developing a diversified financing model that combines central and local budget resources and expands the use of state
                          funds to purchase priority services from public and non-state providers. Building on INSPIRE-supported pilots
                          implemented through the Fund for Social Protection of People with Disabilities (FSPPD), PBC 3 helps establish a
                          transparent, annually updated mechanism to prioritize and centrally finance the most cost-effective and high-need social
                          services. This will enable the reallocation of resources as needs change due to displacement, disability, poverty, mental

                              14.     PBC 5 enhances the impact of the BSA reform (PBC 1) by strengthening support for families with children, who
                              account for about 60 percent of BSA beneficiaries. Families with children in Ukraine face significantly higher risks of
                              poverty, exacerbated by the invasion. While the BSA provides essential income support, children and their caregivers
                              often encounter additional vulnerabilities that aren’t related to income. These can include health issues, disabilities,
                              orphanhood, or a lack of parental care, all of which require supplementary assistance. To help address these needs, the
                              SPIRIT project co-finances 15 child benefits and supports reforms in social assistance provided to families until the child
                              starts school. Key innovations, such as (i) monthly childcare support for infants, (ii) the e-Nursery (“eYasla�?) program for
                              working or studying parents of young children, and (iii) a Schoolchild’s Package for first graders, facilitate parental labor
                              market re-entry, reduce unpaid care burdens, and mitigate long-term career penalties, particularly for women.

                              15.    PBC 6 supports the establishment of a unified and effective administrative system by transferring the
                              responsibility for awarding and distributing 39 benefits to the PFU, which has become a critical pillar of social stability
                              in the current circumstances. Building on recent reforms to modernize social protection administration - including the
                              Bank-supported transfer of functions related to HUS provision under the Social Safety Nets Modernization Project

                          Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts


                          19.     The Environmental and Social (E&S) risks associated with the SPIRIT project are Moderate. No significant adverse
                          E&S impacts are expected. In fact, the SPIRIT project is anticipated to yield positive social outcomes by enhancing the
                          integration, accessibility, and inclusiveness of the social protection system. This will be achieved by promoting reforms
                          in social assistance, social services, and disability inclusion. Consequently, the project aims to minimize risks associated
                          with coverage gaps and the adequacy of support for vulnerable and disadvantaged groups within existing programs.

                          All activities financed by SPIRIT will only take place in areas under government control. A transparent and inclusive
                          methodology will be developed to select communities for implementing microprojects, explicitly excluding cities located
