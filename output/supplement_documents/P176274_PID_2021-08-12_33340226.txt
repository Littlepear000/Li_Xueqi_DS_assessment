  B. Introduction and Context

  Country Context

 Since independence in 1960, CAR has been subject to protracted cycles of conflict. The 2013 Crisis was particularly
 violent and prompted a severe economic downturn with an estimated 36 percent contraction in GDP. Even before
 the 2013 Crisis, per capita income had been decreasing. GDP per capita has almost halved since independence, from
 US$ 599 in 1960, to US$ 566 in 2012, and to US$ 385 in 2017. In fact, CAR has not experienced a sustained growth episode
 since its independence. Approximately 3.4 million people, 71 percent of the population, were living below the
 international poverty line in 2018. Overall, CAR ranks among the world’s lowest ranked countries in the Human
 Development Index (HDI) at 188th out of 189 countries.

 CAR’s recovery and context remain highly fragile and conflict affected. Violence is decreasing as a result of the February
 2019 Peace Accords and the Demobilization, Disarmament and Reintegration (DDR) process. The United Nations’
 Multidimensional Integrated Stabilization Mission in the Central African Republic (MINUSCA) is primarily responsible for
 maintaining public order and protecting the civilian population. However, the government is still unable to project power
 over two thirds of the country and is heavily reliant on both the MINUSCA mission and the broader international
 community to provide essential services to the population, including emergency food aid. Presidential
 elections in December 2020 resulted in the reelection of President Touadéra, but this result was contested by several
 rebel groups, and incidents of violence tied to these groups has increased.

 Nevertheless, CAR’s economy is rebounding from the 2013 Crisis. Bangui has doubled in size to an estimated 1.6 million
 people, approximately a third of the country’s population. Major inflows of donor financing are funding infrastructure
 investments, helping fund government payrolls and strengthening the capacity of key public institutions like the Ministry
 of Finance. The World Bank’s country portfolio is approximately US$780 million, equivalent to 35 percent of the country’s
 GDP. Private investment is increasing, primarily in and around the greater Bangui region where the security situation is
 more stable, drawing on the increased demand for services such as hospitality, food/retail, transportation and
 construction.

 While the country is well-endowed with abundant natural resources, including fertile land, water, forestry and mineral
 assets, these assets have not been leveraged adequately for the benefit of the population. Potential profits from CAR’s
 mining, hydrocarbon and forestry sectors are characterized by complex risks, yet present the most likely, long-term hope
 for generating the domestic revenues necessary for inclusive, sustainable economic development. CAR’s fiscal space
 remains extremely limited and domestic revenue mobilization is very low, partly due to the small size of the private sector,
 limited government tax enforcement capacity and the lack of state presence, especially in key mining areas where there
 is active conflict.

 The COVID-19 pandemic has negatively impacted economic growth in CAR. The pandemic drew significant government
 resources, time and effort that could have been spent implementing economic and social reforms to address the country’s
 shortcomings. With the onset of containment measures, supply chains were interrupted which had a negative impact on
 all sectors, increasing costs and business risk. The resulting economic downturn, as in other parts of the
 region, provoked widespread unemployment, shrank domestic markets and decreased liquidity. While the country has
 received additional resources from donors during the pandemic and partially recovered through adaptation, the new
 post-election instability has resulted in backsliding.

 The State is increasingly aware of the need for the private sector to create jobs, invest and grow, but faces many
 challenges to enabling it. Given its severe institutional and financial capacity constraints, the State relies heavily on the
 World Bank and other international donors to provide budget support, inform policy decisions, assist the government in
 improving the business environment, and to strengthen public institutions that businesses require to operate.


  Sectoral and Institutional Context
 Firms and Informality

 CAR’s formal private sector consists of approximately 40 medium to large firms that can be broken down into two main
 groups. The first group consists of local subsidiaries of foreign multinationals (e.g. Orange, Air France, DHL, Bolloré,
 Ecobank) that rely heavily on external support from their parent companies to overcome operational and financial
 difficulties. The second group consists of sophisticated businesses and SMEs primarily concentrated in the hands of
 locally-based business conglomerates descended from the Lebanese diaspora (2nd or 3rd generation). These firms are the
 dominant economic actors in the areas of commerce, forestry, commercial agricultural production/processing (maize
 milling, palm oil) and manufacturing (soap, cigarettes, beverages). These “survivor�? companies are able to continue
 operating even under difficult circumstances due to access to global financial markets, their experience and skills, and
 having a keen understanding of the political economy. The Central African Interprofessional Coalition of Businesses
 (Groupement Interprofessionnel de Centrafrique - GICA) counts 34 of these larger, sophisticated companies among its
 members. HUSACA, an agribusiness conglomerate and the largest company in CAR, has 3200 employees. A study
 commissioned by the European Union estimated that GICA members contribute 67 percent of government revenues.
 Subsidiaries of businesses headquartered in neighboring countries are prevalent, particularly those from Cameroun.

 Between the thousands of informal, individual entrepreneurs and these larger sophisticated companies exist between
 300 and 1000 SMEs, businesses possessing approximately 5 to 15 employees active mainly in trade, logistics, services,
 with sales of between 30 and 100 million CFAF. In 2011, 269 firms were formally registered with the Ministry of
 Commerce. Of these, only 5 firms had more than 100 employees and 45 possessed between 20 and 99 employees. In
 November 2019, it was reported that only 286 companies of all sizes were present in the register of companies making
 tax declarations at CAR’s national directorate for the BEAC.1


 The government established the SME and Artisan’s Business Center (Centre d’Assistance aux Petites et Moyennes
 Entreprises et à l’Artisanat - CAPMEA) to be a major implementor of its efforts to support SMEs and the informal
 sector. CAPMEA is a financially autonomous public entity under supervision of the Ministry of Small and Medium-Sized
 Enterprises, Artisans and the Informal Sector. CAPMEA has its legal basis in Ordinance No. 83.076 of December 17, 1983

 CAPMEA’s objectives are as follows:

             1. Promoting SMEs and the informal sector;
             2. Providing coaching and technical assistance to SMEs, informal operators and artisans;
             3. Offering technical training, information and documentation;

             6. Monitoring and evaluation of SMEs and the Informal Sector.

 CAPMEA has developed a two-year strategic plan that envisions the following specific activities: 1) Training 3000
 entrepreneurs in agricultural and commercial activities; 2) Performing a survey of MSMEs; 3) Financing and building
 CAPMEA’s facilities, training its staff and putting in place management systems; 4) Setting up a fund to extend credit to
 MSMEs and entrepreneurs with a target of funding 25 investment projects: 10 in agribusiness and 15 in commerce; and
 5) Facilitating lending to MSMEs from financial institutions and mentoring those that receive it. While CAPMEA has
 existed for a long time in various forms, it has lacked the human and financial resources to become a cornerstone of the
 SME ecosystem as the government has envisioned. It continues to be underfunded, but its core management team
 (director-general, administrative and financial manager, SME-support director) is skilled with two members having
 extensive private sector management experience.


 Financial Sector

 CAR is a member of CEMAC and its currency, the Central African franc, is pegged to the euro. Monetary policy
 and central banking functions are the responsibility of the Bank of Central African States (Banque
 des Etats de l’Afrique Centrale - BEAC). Despite the COVID-19 pandemic, the country’s financial sector performed
 relatively well, but needs to be consolidated. The sector remains dominated by four banks, which are adequately
 capitalized, with a total balance sheet of 315 billion Central African CFA Franc (CFAF) in 2020, up from CFAF 274.6 billion
 in November 2019. Deposits expanded by 20.5 percent (year-on-year) in November 2020, mainly driven by an increase in
 households’ deposits amid COVID-19. Also, total credit amounted to CFAF 169.9 billion in November 2020, up from CFAF
 157.9 billion one year earlier. Credit allocation by local banks to the private sector accounted for 89.2 percent of the total
 credit in November, while exposure to the government represented only 1.3 percent and 4.9 percent for state-owned
 enterprises. The capital adequacy ratio, which demonstrates a bank’s ability to pay liabilities and respond to credit and
 operational risks, remained extremely high at 19.9 percent in 2020, above the average of CEMAC and FCV countries at
 13.8 percent and 12.0 percent, respectively. Notwithstanding the decline in other prudential standards, including the net
 return on assets, net return on equity, and the liquid assets to short-term liabilities ratio, local commercial banks remain
 profitable and liquid. Non-performing loans (NPLs) increased from 12.6 percent in 2019 to 13.7 percent in May 2020,
 reflecting structural weaknesses in credit appraisal and inadequate supervision.

 Limited financial distribution networks pose a real challenge for CAR: the relatively small population, low population
 density, and high cost and risk to expansion means that most banks and MFIs2 are concentrated in Bangui. Despite this,
 several banks and MFIs plan on expanding into the regions, and while few players have robust digital offerings, digitization
 is considered a priority area. The key constraints to digital expansion in CAR are:

 Digital Financial Services

 CAR’s mobile money network remains limited, and thus offers a limited business case for service providers. As of
 September 2019, the only mobile money service operating at scale was Orange Money, with 50,000 clients. Only around
 one third of these users were active on a monthly basis, with the vast majority of transactions CICO (Cash-In-Cash-Out).
 Telecel’s recent launch of a mobile wallet has not yet grown into any significant number of users. Alongside an insufficient
 merchant acceptance across the country, this limited network restricts the use case for potential new users.

 Limited formal identity creates a significant Know Your Customer bottleneck for digital financial services. At present
 new clients must present either a national ID cards, a residency permit or a password – few people possess these forms
 of identity, and they can only be acquired in Bangui. There are some workarounds - those without an ID card can open an
 Orange Money account with the receipt of an ID card request, or by asking a friend of family member with an ID card to
 do it in their name, but these are not sustainable solutions; dialogue needs to take place with the public authorities to
 encourage a pragmatic approach to regulation, and consider alternative practices, such as tiered-KYC. KYC requirements
 limit uptake in the absence of a national identification system, and vulnerable segments such as cash transfer recipients
 are less likely to have an ID.


 Business Environment and Investment Climate

 CAR is one of the most challenging places in the world to do business. CAR ranked 184th out of 190 countries in
 the 2020 Doing Business Report. Access to electricity, paying taxes, dealing with construction permits and enforcing
 contracts rank the lowest among the country’s individual Doing Business indicators. Businesses lament the
 unpredictability of the business environment with respect to settling disputes, predatorial behavior of tax authorities
 and a casual respect for legal agreements. Given the high level of informality and subsequent difficulty the government
 has collecting taxes from informal businesses, the government relies heavily on extracting taxes, fees and other payments
 from the few formal businesses capable of paying them. These businesses only account for 20-30 percent of GDP.

 In April 2021, the World Bank completed the Country Private Sector Diagnostic (CPSD) for CAR, an analysis of
 opportunities for the private sector, constraints and recommendations for enhancing economic growth. The
 CPSD proposed a set of potential pathways for maximizing the development impact of private sector investment in CAR in
 the short to medium-term. These were grounded in the existing market dynamics in the country and consist of the
 following recommendations:

          1. Prioritize a spatial development approach around Bangui and along the regional road corridor to
          Douala, given potential economies of scale and relatively stable security conditions.

          2. Leverage the aid-economy by developing local economic linkages with donor-funded projects such as those
          funded by the World Bank, EU, UN, AFD, IFAD and AfDB and the MINUSCA Mission, which will remain a major
          source of capital inflows in the foreseeable future.

          3. Strengthen linkages between local SMEs and the handful of sophisticated companies in CAR; with a focus on
          priority sectors identified by the CPSD (e.g. agribusiness, construction, transportation, ICT)

          4. Enable increased private participation in service delivery such as through developing PPP delivery capacity in
          the government. Areas of PPP potential include water, sanitation, power, construction/public works.



  Relationship to CPF
 This project’s activities and PDO align with the objectives of the FY21-FY25 Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for
 CAR, specifically CPF Objective 2.3 – Enhance Private Sector Development for Job Creation and Economic Transformation.
 The CAR CPF asserts that growing CAR’s private sector is critical to supporting livelihoods, ensuring the reintegration of
 ex-combatants, and alleviating the misery that contributes to periodic episodes of violence.

 Expected results for this objective are:
 • Enhance private sector development for job creation and economic transformation.
 • Improve the investment climate in CAR via streamlined procedures to start a business, reduced cost to open a
    business, and increased access to finance.

 The project will have the following results: 1) Enhancing the growth of MSMEs, 2) Strengthening the ecosystem
 providing business support services and information for MSMEs, 3) Improving CAR’s business environment and 4)
 Increasing credit provided to the private sector.



 The progress toward the achievement of the PDO will be measured by the following:

 1) Increase in sales by beneficiary businesses receiving project-funded technical and financial assistance;
 2) Increase in jobs created by beneficiary businesses receiving project-funded technical and financial assistance;
 3) Amount of financing obtained by beneficiary businesses receiving project-funded technical and financial assistance;
 4) Increase in credit provided to the private sector as a percentage of GDP;
 5) Establishment and operating of CAR’s National Guarantee Fund;
 6) Number of business plans developed for business support service providers, NGOs and business associations;
 7) Amount of funding received by business support service providers, NGOs and business associations.
 8) Number of business environment improvements, strategies and/or reforms implemented resulting from project
 technical and financial assistance;
 9) Growth of beneficiaries of project-supported service providers and business organizations.


  D. Concept Description
  This is a USD 20 million, 6-year enterprise competitiveness project targeting primarily informal MSMEs. The project
  is structured around three technical components, a project management component and a CERC. Technical
  components focus on building out public and private organizations that provide business support services and training
  for firms, increasing credit to the private sector through supporting a national guarantee mechanism and partner
  financial institutions, and strengthening PPD mechanisms fundamental to business environment improvements. The
  project design is informed by analytical work performed over the past several years in CAR, particularly the Country
  Private Sector Diagnostic to be validated with the client around the time of a definitional mission for the project. The
  project derived in part from activity designs described in the CIIP Private Sector Support Services Delivery Design
  (P170903) ASA delivered in December 2020.

  Building CAR’s SME Ecosystem: The project will be implemented under the tutelage of the Ministry of Small and
  Medium-Sized Enterprises, Artisans and the Informal Sector and assisted by an existing or new project coordination
  unit (PCU) that possesses the skills to plan, budget and organize the activities. If an existing PCU is selected, it will be
  strengthened with additional procurement and financial management staff, administrative support, social and
  environmental safeguards experts and a separate activity coordinator. The designated PCU, the Ministry and CAPMEA
  will work together closely to develop their own capacity to the extent needed to implement these activities; and to
  assure that the activities are supervised and implemented satisfactorily.
