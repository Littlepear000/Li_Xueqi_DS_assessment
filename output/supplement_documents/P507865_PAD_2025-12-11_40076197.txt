    I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT


    A. Project Strategic Context

1.       Assam’s real Gross State Domestic Product growth has averaged 7.6 percent over the past decade, which is
higher than the national average of 6 percent. Agriculture and allied services contributed 22 percent of the Gross State
Value Added (16 percent for India) and employed 47 percent of the labor force in 2023-2024. As per the National
Institution for Transforming India Aayog report on Multidimensional Poverty (2023), the poverty headcount ratio in Assam
has declined remarkably from 32.65 percent in 2015-2016 to 19.35 percent in 2019-2021, although the state still has one
of the highest rates of multidimensional poverty in India. Moreover, Assam’s lower per capita income of Indian Rupee
(INR) 20,336 (at current prices), compared to the national average of INR 172,276, underscores the need for continued
economic improvement and inclusive growth.1

2.      Assam population was 31.2 million as per the 2011 census, constituting around 2.2 percent of the total Indian
population. As per the 2011 census, 14.8 percent of the population in the age range 0-19 years were migrant children
(13.7 percent male; 16 percent female); the Scheduled Castes (SC) population of 2.2 million made up 7.15 percent of the
state’s population; and the Scheduled Tribes (ST) population of 3.9 million made up 12.4 percent of the state's total
population. At 85.9 percent, the share of the rural population in Assam is one of the highest in the country (65 percent for
India).

3.       The state’s climate- and disaster-related vulnerabilities pose serious threats to its human development strategy.
As per the State Action Plan for Climate Change and Human Health (2.0), extreme rainfall events in the state are predicted
to increase by 38 percent. Assam's annual mean temperature has increased by 0.59°C in the period from 1951 to 2010
and is likely to increase by 1.7–2.2°C by 2050; previously unheard-of heat strokes may become common as summer
temperatures begin to reach 40°C. Assam is among India’s most climate-exposed states: about 40 percent of its land (3.1
million hectares) is officially flood-prone, and 33 of the 35 districts are categorized as flood-vulnerable.2 Severe flooding
and extreme rainfall events lead to erosion, mudslides, and landslides in the hilly areas, and cause river erosion along its
major and minor rivers, affecting millions of people, property, and vast areas of land. These vulnerabilities make it
imperative to enhance disaster resilience and climate adaptation measures. Assam is also highly prone to earthquakes,
with the entire state falling within the highest earthquake risk Zone V.

    B. Sectoral and Institutional Context

4.      Assam has seen substantial progress on several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) but more progress is
needed on SDG 4, which emphasizes inclusive and quality education and lifelong learning for all. The state ranks 24th
out of 28 states in the SDGs Index (2023-2024), reflecting challenges in areas such as hunger, education quality, gender
equality, industry, innovation, and infrastructure. Data on multidimensional poverty shows that roughly 12 percent of the
population in Assam underperforms on the indicator for years of schooling, i.e., no member of their household aged 10
years or older has completed six years of schooling.

5.      School enrollment rates in Assam have improved in the last decade. As per the Unified District Information
System for Education Plus (UDISE+) 2023-2024, Assam has 56,630 schools, of which 45,008 (79.5 percent) are government-
managed under the Department of School Education (DoSE), 5,725 (10.1 percent) are private, and the remaining 10.4

6.       However, learning levels in Assam remain low. Findings from representative surveys consistently show that
students in Assam are not achieving minimum learning levels. As per the National Achievement Survey (NAS) 2021, 58
percent of grade 3 (age 8) and 55 percent of grade 5 (age 10) students in the state do not meet minimum grade level
proficiency in language (Assamese). By grade 8 (age 14), this statistic worsens to 74 percent. Based on data from the
Annual Status of Education Report 2022, only 24 percent of grade 5 students living in rural areas in Assam can read simple
grade 2 level sentences in English, and only 65 percent of those can read with comprehension. While students in grades 3
and 5 performed marginally better than the India average on NAS 2021, performance levels worsened in the later grades,
and the state’s average scores were lower than the India average across all subjects in grade 10. Within the state, while
there is no significant gender gap, socially disadvantaged groups like the SC, ST, and Other Backward Classes score lower,
and the average rural scores are lower than the average urban scores across all schools.

7.       Several factors explain the low learning levels, including students’ low school readiness, inadequate provision
of quality Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) services, and limited availability of trained teachers. ECCE is
delivered through Anganwadi Centers (AWCs) under the Department of Women and Child Development (DWCD), and
through pre-primary schools under DoSE.3 The curriculum taught in AWCs and pre-schools is not age or developmentally
appropriate, with limited focus on the cognitive, language, and social skills necessary for school readiness. The majority of
Anganwadi Workers (AWWs) and pre-primary teachers are not trained to deliver ECCE effectively. Lack of quality
infrastructure in AWCs also negatively impacts ECCE delivery, with some centers operating from temporary/rented
community spaces, often lacking basic amenities such as water and sanitation, particularly in rural and geographically
hard-to-reach areas. According to UDISE+ 2023-2024, the percentage of trained primary teachers in the state across all
types of school management is lower than the national average – 84 percent versus 91.7 percent. This number further
declines to 66.4 percent at the secondary level, compared to the country average of 92.9 percent. Additionally, the annual
cycles of floods and other natural disasters disrupt the functioning of both AWCs and schools with damage to
infrastructure, and schools being used as shelters. Assam Flood Memorandum 2024 by the Assam State Disaster
Management Authority reported that 964 AWCs and 1,058 schools were damaged after the 2024 flood season. During
such events, children not only lose learning days but teaching materials, learning resources, and school infrastructure are
also damaged.

8.      The school network in Assam is characterized by a low ratio of secondary and senior secondary schools to
primary schools. As per UDISE+ 2023-2024, there were 46,922 primary and upper primary schools in Assam enrolling
around 5.4 million students. The state had 7,073 schools offering secondary education (grades 9-10) and 2,635 schools
offering senior secondary education (grades 11-12), with a combined enrollment of about 1.5 million. This means that for
every 100 elementary schools, there are only 21 secondary and senior secondary schools, compared to the national
average of 26. Most secondary and senior secondary schools have inadequate infrastructure: for example, only 36 percent
have integrated science laboratories or provision for co-curricular activities. Furthermore, 77.9 percent of schools have
ramps and 69.1 percent have ramps with handrails, although this is likely to be based only on the availability of a ramp for
children in wheelchairs, with no information on actual usability of ramps or on other accessibility measures for children
with other types of disability. 20.4 percent of all schools have functional toilets for children with disabilities, significantly

9.       Assam experiences higher dropout and lower transition rates than India average. As per UDISE+ 2023-24, Assam
has one of the highest student dropout rates in primary education (grades 1-5) at 6.2 percent (India average is 1.9 percent),
and a low student retention rate in elementary education (grades 1-8) of 65 percent (India average is 78 percent). The
dropout rates at both upper primary (8.2 percent) and secondary levels (25.1 percent) are significantly higher than the
national average (5.2 percent and 15.1 percent, respectively) and have been consistently higher for the last seven years.
The fragmented school network – due to long distances between primary/elementary (grades 1 to 8) and secondary
schools – is one of the prime factors behind the high dropout rates in Assam. Indeed, primary and upper-primary dropout
rates are higher in Assam than in all neighboring states, except for Meghalaya. The transition rate from secondary to senior
secondary is only 52.5 percent (India average is 71.5 percent) – worse than every other state in the country other than
Meghalaya and Bihar – and is skewed in favor of boys (54.4 percent versus 50.8 percent for girls). While the dropout rate
at the secondary level has declined from 31.5 percent in 2018-2019 to 25.1 percent in 2023–2024, it has more than
doubled at the primary and upper-primary levels. A study by the State Council of Educational Research and Training
(SCERT) Assam found that high dropout rates in government schools are largely driven by the pressure to earn a livelihood,
early marriage, low learning levels, and a lack of structured counseling services. Boys were found to have higher dropout
rates than girls, with livelihood pressure being a significant factor. Data from the National Family Health Survey (2019–
2021) shows a significant urban-rural divide in the median number of completed years of schooling (8.3 years in urban
versus 5.3 years in rural areas). Only 29.5 percent of women in Assam complete more than 10 years of schooling against
an all-India average of 41.1 percent, and 31.8 percent of women aged 20–24 in Assam were married before the age of 18
against the national average of 23.3 percent.

10.      Assam’s adolescent population in the age group 10-19 years, comprising close to 21 percent (around 6.5 million
as per the 2011 census) of the state population, faces several challenges, such as high dropout rates, early marriage, low
employment and high inactivity rates, mental health issues, and substance abuse. Anemia is another major health concern
and is more prevalent among girls (67 percent) than boys (40 percent). Barriers to improving nutritional status among
adolescents include lack of dietary diversity, illiteracy, improper sanitation, and minimal coverage of interventions during
early childhood and pregnancy. About 1.79 percent of the state’s adolescent population live with some form of disabilities
as per the 2011 census, with about 36 percent of children with disabilities (ages 5-19) not attending educational
institutions.

11.      These challenges hamper adolescents’ academic progression or workforce readiness. The age-specific
enrollment rate in Assam drops from 81.4 percent for the age group 14-15 years to 59.4 percent for the age group 16-17
years, much steeper than the India average drop of 77 to 75.5 percent. This sharp drop in school enrollment without a
clear alternate path leads to a high proportion of not in education, employment, or training (NEET) (nearly 27 percent of
adolescents/youth – 12 percent males, 44 percent females – in the age-group 18-23 years) and a high youth
unemployment rate (14.1 percent for age-group 15-29 years versus 10.2 percent India average). Among NEET females, 31
percent become NEET at age 18 (compared to 10 percent for males), and this rises to 59 percent by age 23 (18 percent
for males). The primary reasons for becoming NEET include poverty, social norms, and lack of available schools with
secondary and senior secondary grades nearby. The dropout rate increases in the higher grades for both genders - boys
due to earning pressure, and girls as they become involved in domestic duties and/or get married at a young age. As a
result, the higher education GER in the state has been low (16.9 percent versus 28.4 percent India average) and falling
(from 18 percent in 2018-2019 to 16.9 percent in 2021-2022).

12.     The state is taking several measures to improve the quality of education and adolescent wellbeing. Assam is
one of the first states in India to introduce an ECCE training course for AWWs as per the National Education Policy (NEP)
2020. The course has been developed and rolled out jointly by DoSE and DWCD. To augment the infrastructure for

being strengthened through annual training plans, in alignment with the Government of India (GoI) National Initiative for
Proficiency in Reading with Understanding and Numeracy. The state has an impressive state-level assessment system,
Gunotsav, that collects data on student learning levels annually for grades 1-9. To improve access to secondary and senior
secondary education, the state is already upgrading 400 schools to include secondary/senior secondary grades using state
funds and plans to add an additional 1,000 new secondary schools in the next five years using different central sector and
other schemes. In addition, the state has launched Shiksha Setu, a data management system that integrates academic and
administrative data from various sources, leveraging the UDISE+ database to monitor service delivery across all
government schools. Beyond infrastructure tracking, the platform is designed to capture student attendance, migration
patterns, teacher transfers, and academic performance of students.

13.     Despite ongoing initiatives, several systemic challenges continue to constrain the effectiveness and impact of
these reforms. The ECCE interventions have not been scaled up uniformly across the state, with no reliable monitoring
mechanism to test their impact on children’s early learning outcomes or to review plans for further enhancements.
Teacher training institutes face capacity constraints in identifying and addressing contextual learning needs linked to
classroom realities. There is limited integration of student and school level data (from Gunotsav and Shiksha Setu) to
inform school management practices and decision making. The absence of a coherent, long-term strategy for expanding
access to, and improving the quality of, senior secondary education further limits future planning, with ongoing school
upgradation efforts often functioning in silos. Existing support services for adolescents to help them in their school-to-
work transition face implementation and quality gaps. DWCD, as the nodal department for women, children, and
adolescents caters to a large proportion of the state’s population. However, its institutional capacity for implementation
and supervision is weak, often relying on either centrally funded programs or externally supported temporary institutional
mechanisms.

14.      The Assam: School Education and Adolescent Wellbeing Project (ASAP) will invest in bridging these gaps
through a multi-pronged approach that prioritizes investment in human capital for academic and economic inclusion
and an integrated institutional mechanism to build a sustainable system and last mile delivery. This will be achieved by
aligning and scaling up existing initiatives aimed at improving foundational learning, designing and implementing a state-
wide strategy for efficient planning of investments in school education, and leveraging existing mechanisms for a
beneficiary-driven approach to adolescent wellbeing and their transition from school to work. For the efficient and
sustainable delivery of these interventions, the project will focus on strengthening institutional mechanisms and systems
that build the capacities of teachers and frontline workers. Emphasis will be on reforms to improve coordination between
institutions and stakeholders, and data-driven decision making to enhance user centric service delivery.

 C. Project Beneficiaries

17.      The primary project beneficiaries are children in the age group of 3 to 10 years enrolled in government funded
AWCs, pre-primary and primary grades; and adolescent boys and girls aged 10 to 19 years who are in government or
government-aided upper primary, secondary, and senior secondary schools or out-of-school. This includes about 0.7
million children in the age group of 3 to 6 years enrolled in AWCs and pre-primary schools across Assam; about 2 million
children enrolled in primary schools; about 2 million adolescents enrolled in secondary education; and 20,000 out-of-
school adolescents in the age range of 10-19 years. While private schools will not receive any financing under the project,
they are expected to benefit from project interventions aimed at improving curriculum, capacity building, and system
reforms. About 110,000 teachers teaching primary grades, 60,000 teaching upper primary grades, and 40,000 teaching
secondary grades in DoSE-managed schools will benefit from training and the availability of enhanced curriculum and
teaching and learning materials. In addition, about 40,000 frontline workers (AWWs) of DWCD and about 20,000 pre-
primary teachers of DoSE will benefit from the relevant training and capacity building activities. District and sub-district
level officials of the SSA, DoSE, DWCD, and Public Works Department (PWD), and adolescent peer leaders will also benefit
from the institutional capacity building activities supported by the project.

 D. Project Components

18.     The project will provide a continuum of support through different stages of human capital development. The
focus will be on improving ECCE and school readiness, equipping school-age children with foundational skills, improving
access to and quality of secondary education, and preparing adolescents for quality jobs and livelihood opportunities. The
project will be implemented over a five-year period following Investment Project Financing (IPF) procedures. It adopts a
multi-sectoral approach to expand and strengthen the delivery of services required to improve education and adolescent
outcomes through four components.

children, the delivery of ECCE training to AWWs and pre-primary teachers, development of training and learning materials,
and upgradation of AWC infrastructure in select locations. For the primary grades, interventions will focus on improving
children’s early reading skills and developing a reliable assessment system. Targeted information and education campaigns
for parents and communities will be conducted for their engagement and monitoring. Specifically, the component will

interventions:

upgradation. To ensure the strategic long-term sustainability of investments in school education, the project will support

communities, and improving their operational efficiency, including teacher availability, quality of teaching and learning

enhancing student-teacher interaction.5 Climate resilience and disaster risk management planning for school
infrastructure will be a crucial element of the strategy given the state's vulnerability to natural and climate hazards. The
strategy will also consider existing or planned state level initiatives/schemes, centrally sponsored schemes, and private
sector initiatives aimed at school infrastructure reforms to inform resource allocations available for school upgradation
under the project.

 F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design

23.    The project’s design is informed by lessons learned through the experiences of previous and ongoing World
Bank-funded projects, analytical work, and global evidence from successful educational interventions. These lessons
demonstrate that sustainable improvements in learning outcomes and system capacity require integrated, evidence-
based approaches across the full education lifecycle.

a) Investing early in ECCE delivers high returns. Global research consistently demonstrates that investments in high-
quality ECCE yield high returns by boosting cognitive and academic outcomes and providing an integrated platform for
developmental screening, nutrition interventions, and healthy-behavior promotion. In response, the project in Assam
prioritizes capacity building for AWWs, upgrades to AWCs, including making them resilient to climate change and disasters,
and the development and implementation of an integrated ECCE curriculum that promotes school readiness. It also
emphasizes active community and parental engagement, integration of socio-emotional learning, inclusive practices for
all children, especially from marginalized communities, and a nurturing, play-based learning environment to ensure that
all children enter school ready to learn and thrive.

b) Prioritizing foundational literacy and numeracy yields lifelong gains. Global evidence, including the insights from the
Literacy Policy Package, emphasizes structured reading instruction, robust teacher development, and regular formative
assessment as key drivers of early-grade proficiency, yielding substantial returns (estimated 8–19 times the investment).
Aligned with this, ASAP will: (i) provide enhanced pre-service and in-service training on evidence-based foundational

and (iii) deploy a digitally enabled classroom assessment tool to monitor progress in real time. Furthermore, student
learning levels will be tracked as a PDO indicator, and systemic processes for providing pedagogical support to teachers
will be established to ensure equitable foundational learning outcomes across the state.

c) Teacher professional development guided by the 4Cs, viz, Content, Colleagues, Coaching, and Careers lead to greater
impact. All teacher training interventions under the project will be based on: (i) a focus on content knowledge and
pedagogical practices; (ii) opportunities to practice what is learnt with colleagues; (iii) continued support through follow-
up visits focused on training content; and (iv) enabling efficient career management pathways.

d) Modernizing school infrastructure and expanding secondary access increases retention rates. Global experience,
including World Bank-financed education infrastructure projects in South Asia, demonstrates that strategic investments
in functional classrooms, laboratories, sanitation, and Information and Communication Technology significantly increase
attendance and reduce dropout rates. The project will apply this lesson by upgrading targeted schools with modern
classrooms, science labs, and clean sanitation facilities; strengthening digital infrastructure to support learning and
teacher collaboration; and expanding capacity at secondary and senior secondary levels to smooth student transitions and
reduce bottlenecks. These upgrades will foster green, safe, and engaging environments. The climate and disaster resilient
designs for new and upgraded schools will reduce disruption to the school calendar and reduce losses to teaching and
learning materials, furniture, laboratories, and building assets.

e) Periodic assessment systems drive learning improvements. Reports like 'Smart Buys' affirm that regularly measuring

strategies, and facilitate evidence-based decision-making at both school and system levels.

f) Investing in socio-emotional learning leads to improved learning outcomes and psycho-social health of adolescents.
A meta-analysis of 82 different interventions from kindergarten to high school, where the effects were assessed 6 months
to 18 years after the program ended, demonstrated positive, long-term benefits for students from school-based
interventions. Embedding life-skills and other non-academic skills education within curriculum and policy frameworks and
supporting schools in their implementation have been shown to reduce dropouts and increase school completion in
Argentina, Brazil, and other countries. These curricula include topics such as values, relationships, coping with stress, and
dealing with peer pressure that can help adolescents with self-efficacy, confidence, and decision-making, thereby reducing
their risk of dropping out of school, early marriage, and early pregnancy.

33.       Economic analysis. The cost-benefit analysis for ASAP yields an Economic Internal Rate of Return (EIRR) of 13
percent, with a net present value of US$402.75 million at a discount rate of 5.6 percent. The project interventions are
likely to benefit approximately 4 million students through improvements in the quality of education. The EIRR is estimated
based on the anticipated outcomes reflected in the PDO indicators. The analysis assumes that: (a) the percentage of
primary years students achieving grade level proficiency in reading during the course of the project would increase by at
least 4 percentage points; (b) the transition rate from elementary to secondary would increase by 3.4 percentage points;
and (c) there would be a one-standard-deviation overall improvement in learning levels - accruing to general
improvements in the quality of education, better teaching standards, and strengthened institutional capacity – that would
increase wage earnings for school completers upon joining the workforce by 18 percent. After factoring in labor force
participation rate and unemployment rate, it is likely to positively enhance the lifetime wage earnings of student
beneficiaries completing elementary, secondary, and senior secondary education. The cost of the project includes a direct
cost totaling US$312.5 million. The project’s EIRR ranges between 12 and 14 percent in different scenarios. The project
remains viable even with higher discount rates (8 percent), lower transition rates, and a lower completion rate at senior
secondary grades. The project is found to be feasible even if the benefits reduce from 100 percent to 75 percent (EIRR –
10 percent) and 50 percent (EIRR – 7 percent).

34.     The IPF instrument will provide financing support to address critical gaps, such as long distance between schools,
classroom shortages, inadequate facilities for girls, sub-optimal teacher quality, and lack of systematic student learning
assessments, that impede human capital accumulation. These supply-side constraints are partly due to insufficient
resources, which the project targets through improved management and service delivery. The state’s planning and
implementation capacity will be improved through technical support and strengthened digital information systems.

35.      Gender. In Assam, the dropout rate in upper primary is higher for boys than girls, and the transition rate from
elementary to secondary school is lower (79.5 percent for boys compared to 85.4 percent for girls). Evidence shows that
gender inequality rises when boys and men are not educated.11 Moreover, when boys are educated, they are more likely
to support gender equality. Boys in Assam are dropping out of school and not transitioning due to: (a) lack of interest in
studies and education; (b) lack of proper career counseling and pathways; (c) pressure to provide economic support to
their households; and (d) unaddressed behavioral issues that evidence has shown to impact boys more than girls. 12 To
bridge the gap, the project will: (i) provide parental and community outreach campaigns to sensitize parents and the
community about the importance of keeping children, especially boys, in school; (ii) develop programs for life skills
education, including sexual and reproductive health, that are culturally sensitive, gender- and age-appropriate, disability-
inclusive, and evidence-based; (iii) provide academic and career counseling, with a focus on boys’ aptitudes and interests,
and (iv) provide opportunities to undertake vocational training and skills courses that will open up economic pathways.13
Outcomes will be measured through the PDO indicator that tracks improvement in transition rate from elementary to
secondary level for boys that leads to better academic and jobs pathways.

36.       School-Related Gender-Based Violence (SRGBV) negatively impacts adolescent girls and their learning
outcomes. A major gap in addressing SRGBV is the lack of systematic referral mechanisms and protocols that can support
girls to raise their concerns. To address SRGBV concerns, the project will: (i) develop a referral mechanism and protocols
that will support adolescent girls to report SRGBV; (ii) support capacity building of teachers and school leaders to address
issues related to SRGBV; (iii) build safe and inclusive learning environments that will help address infrastructural barriers
and risks of SRGBV; (iv) create safe spaces in AWCs and community spaces; (v) conduct parental and community outreach
to support out-of-school female adolescents and those at risk of dropping out; and (vi) impart career counseling and
mentorship programs to support girls’ academic aspirations, especially in STEM fields and life skills development.

37.      Citizen engagement. The project includes beneficiary-centric and citizen engagement actions, including: (i)
stakeholder consultations during preparation and implementation, (ii) outreach and capacity building activities for
community institutions and teachers, (iii) enhanced transparency and accountability through improved data management
systems, (iv) provision for collecting beneficiary feedback from students, teachers, parents, and communities, and (v) a
grievance redress mechanism. The Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) provides a systematic approach to identify and
engage stakeholders, including citizens, ensuring their views are integrated into project implementation. In addition,
special attention is given to vulnerable groups, including SC and ST communities, persons with disabilities, and migrant
families to address potential barriers like language, remote locations, and accessibility. Effective engagement, such as
public consultations, focus group discussions, community meetings and sensitization of teachers, students, parents, and
school personnel are envisaged to ensure inclusiveness and voice. The project will provide an efficient and responsive
grievance redress mechanism to address concerns, complaints, and seek feedback from citizens. The effectiveness of the
grievance redress mechanism will be tracked and analyzed throughout implementation. The DoSE, supported by a Social
Specialist, will lead SEP's implementation, ensuring timely information disclosure and citizen participation. This
comprehensive engagement strategy aims to foster constructive relationships, promote transparency, and ensure the
project's success in improving education and wellbeing for all students in Assam. Progress on citizen engagement will be
measured through the result indicator that tracks beneficiary feedback using beneficiary surveys to assess the satisfaction
of the targeted beneficiaries and inform course corrections as needed.

38.     Paris Alignment. The project is consistent with India’s Nationally Determined Contributions (2021-2030)
commitments under the Paris Agreement and India’s Long-Term Low Emission Development Strategy. By supporting
enhancements in education quality and child and adolescent wellbeing, the project contributes to India’s goals of
sustainable development and climate adaptation by building human capital and resilience to future shocks. The project
interventions will help foster long-term capacity building, enabling communities to adopt climate-friendly practices, and
thereby furthering India’s climate resilience objectives. At the subnational level, the project is aligned with the Assam
State Action Plan on Climate Change that recognizes the role of schools as important stakeholders in building awareness
among students and youth about the importance of environment for human welfare.

carbon footprint associated with digital infrastructure. By prioritizing energy-efficient hardware, cloud-based solutions
with lower emissions, and responsible e-waste management, the project ensures that the mitigation risks linked to these
activities remain low.

41.      Climate co-benefits. The project will significantly contribute to climate co-benefits by enhancing climate and

to growing climate and environmental shocks/disasters. Any infrastructure/facilities enhancement under the project will

training on adopting resource efficiency measures (including energy and water), climate and disaster resilience,
emergency preparedness, and technology for education continuity and minimizing losses in cases of unplanned school

use of sustainable and locally appropriate building materials, improved ventilation and natural lighting, water and energy
efficiency measures, and the creation of safe spaces that can serve as community shelters during emergencies. Through

 V. KEY RISKS

55.   The overall risk to achieving the PDO is Substantial given the below risks, after accounting for mitigation
measures.

56.     The technical design risk is Substantial. This is due to: (i) a multisectoral service delivery design; (ii) the need to
build management and implementation capacities at block and community levels; and (iii) the innovative design that
involves sets of interventions to be piloted, evaluated, and scaled up. The socio-cultural and topographical diversity
provide unique challenges and opportunities that require context-specific interventions and support. These risks will be
mitigated through continuous consultations with key stakeholders and by leveraging lessons from the best practices that
will be most relevant to ground realities.

57.       The institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability risk is Substantial. The capacity level of the
implementing agencies is not uniform, which may lead to challenges in executing the project and scaling up reforms. The
risk will be mitigated by investing in enhancing the institutional capacity of the implementing departments with adequate
and trained staff, providing customized technical support in implementation and evaluation design, and carrying forward
a strong project oversight to inform both the implementation and system level decisions for sustainability of reforms.

58.     The fiduciary risk is Substantial due to lack of prior experience of the implementing agencies with the World Bank-
funded projects. Further, the high volume of civil works activities to be undertaken by PWD poses compliance, consistency,
and contract management challenges, oversight risks, and possible delays in fund flows. The risk will be mitigated through
various mitigation measures, including onboarding of fiduciary personnel by all implementing agencies and provision of
customized training, in addition to the project’s fiduciary arrangements.
