    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context

    1.     Cambodia has a young population, predominantly rural, and is marked by high female labor force participation
    but significant gender disparities in employment quality. About 60 percent of Cambodia’s population of over 16 million
    live in rural areas. Children (ages 0–14) and youth (ages 15–24) constitute 29 percent and 17 percent of the population,
    respectively. Internal migration is becoming more prevalent as people move in search of jobs. Women’s labor force
    participation is high compared with other countries. In 2022, 69.6 percent of women were in the labor force versus 82.1
    percent of men. However, women are less likely to be in formal, better paid, and secure forms of employment.

    2.   In the two decades preceding the pandemic, Cambodia experienced uninterrupted economic expansion.
    Cambodia was one of the fastest growing economies in the world between 1998 and 2019 with a sustained average real
    growth rate of 7.7 percent, driven largely by tourism, manufacturing exports, real estate, and construction. The country
    reached lower middle-income status in 2015 and is aspiring to attain upper middle-income status by 2030 and high-
    income status by 2050. Cambodia has achieved considerable improvements in socioeconomic indicators such as health
    and education, but equitable access to basic public services remains a challenge. The poverty rate in the country declined
    from 33.8 percent in 2009 to 17.8 percent in 2019.

    3.    However, Cambodia’s current economic success has been built largely on the expansion of relatively low -
    technology, low wage/skill production in such industries as textiles, apparel, and basic electronics. Cambodia’s
    industrial sector remains weak and narrow as it heavily relies on exporting low-values products, placing Cambodia in
    low position in the Global Value Chains (GCVs).1 Moreover, its comparative advantage in these industries is being eroded
    as wages increase, making unit labor costs (as a share of value added) very high among comparators2.

    4.    To maintain growth prospects in the long term and remain competitive, Cambodia must diversify its exports
    and transform its labor-intensive industrial structure into a knowledge-intensive model to move to the next stages of
    participation in GVCs. The country’s Pentagon Strategy-Phase I (PS-PI) and Industrial Development Policy (IDP) (2015-
    2025) specifically calls for creating a competitive economy through knowledge and innovation. The RGC has further
    identified priority sectors in its IDP, including automotive, electronic, and agro-industrial sectors. Its National Research
    Agenda 2025 also spells out key research areas related to these priority industries, including food production and
    processing, cloud-based services, electronic and mechanical spare parts, and energy supply.

  5.    Such diversification and transformation require Cambodia to ensure that its workforce has more and better
  education, starting with a focus on ensuring that everyone has foundational literacy and numeracy skills. As the next
  section will illustrate in more details, Cambodia will not be able to expand its workforce of high-skilled workers unless
  it ensures that more children have basic numeracy skills and can read with understanding by the end of primary grades.

  6.    Recognizing the importance of human capital development, the Royal Government of Cambodia (RGC) has
  given it high priority in its PS-PI. Specifically, the Strategy recognizes the central role of education in boosting economic
  mobility, raising incomes, and improving employability, and, thereby, driving poverty reduction and economic growth.

 Sectoral and Institutional Context

  1.    The education sector is overseen by the Ministry of Education, Youth and Sport (MoEYS), which is responsible
  for formulating policies, managing programs, and overseeing the delivery of education services. MoEYS works closely
  with sub-national administrations, which, under the country’s Decentralization and Deconcentration (D&D) reforms,
  have been delegated certain responsibilities for service delivery, including education, to provincial, district, and
  commune-level authorities. Specifically, with recent reforms, the management of early childhood and primary education
  teachers has been devolved to district and municipal administrations. Non-governmental actors, including private
  schools and NGOs, also play a significant role in education delivery, particularly in rural areas. These institutions are
  integrated into the education system, adhering to national curricula, standards, and examinations. In terms of numbers,
  there are nearly 2.3 million enrolled primary students, and over 65,000 primary teacher positions3 across approximately
  7,300 primary schools. (see annex table 1 for details).

  2.     Education outcomes are mixed: on the one hand, Cambodia has made remarkable strides in increasing access to
  education; between 2001 and 2023, primary, lower secondary, and upper secondary enrollment rates rose from 84 to
  96 percent, 17 to 65 percent, and 8 to 44 percent, respectively (see Graph 1). By contrast, recent assessments of
  students’ learning outcomes show that most students are not acquiring foundational literacy and numeracy skills (see
  Graph 2). For instance, in a national assessment conducted in 2021, 81 percent of Grade 6 students were not meeting
  minimum proficiency levels in math and Khmer. The findings from the national assessment echo findings from the
  regional Southeast Asia Primary Learning Metric (SEA-PLM 2019)4 which showed that 89 percent of 5th graders in
  Cambodia were not meeting minimum proficiency levels in reading while 81 percent were not meeting the minimum
  proficiency level in math. Moreover, PISA-D 2022 results of Cambodia’s 15 year olds illustrate what happens when
  children are failing to acquire foundational literacy and numeracy skills in the primary grades: a much smaller proportion
  of children remain in the education system by that age (only about 40 percent of 15 year olds are enrolled in lower
  secondary), and most of the children who remain in school by that age continue to struggle with basic literacy and
  numeracy skills: only 12 percent of the 15-year-olds assessed were functionally numerate while only 8 percent were
  functionally literate.5 In short, compared with just a few decades ago, more children are in school and they are

    3.    Despite significant progress in providing access to education, student learning outcomes have improved only
    marginally during the past decade. National learning assessments (NLAs), conducted by MoEYS during this period, show
    persistently low scores in literacy and numeracy tests. The NLAs of grade 6 students (i.e. the final grade of primary
    education in Cambodia), conducted in 2013 and 2016, show only a marginal improvements of student learning
    outcomes. In 2013, students responded correctly, on average, on 46 percent of questions for Khmer, and 43 percent of
    questions for math. By 2016, this had only increased to 52 percent for Khmer and 48 percent for math. In 2021, there
    was a decline in percentage of correct answers for Khmer (47 percent) and math (38 percent), resulting from the COVID-
    19 pandemic’s negative impact on student learning.6

    4.     There are significant variations in outcomes across geographic areas, income groups, and gender. Similar to
    students elsewhere in the world, Grade 5 students from urban, and more socioeconomically advantaged backgrounds
    significantly outperformed their rural peers. Students from the highest socioeconomic quintile scored on average 301
    points on the SEA-PLM test compared to 279 points for students from the lowest quintile. Moreover, grade 5 girls (on
    average 291 points) outperformed grade 5 boys (on average 284 points) in reading, writing and mathematics.

    teachers are not ready to teach; (iii) children are being allowed to fall behind; (iv) the problem of slow learners is being

Factor #1 - Children are not ready to learn.


6.     In terms of their school readiness skills, Cambodia’s children are the least prepared for primary school and have
the lowest pre-school attendance rate amongst the countries who participated in SEA-PLM (2019). Specifically, SEA-
PLM data show that only one third (33 percent) of Cambodia’s children had mastered 11 ‘school-readiness skills’ (such
as being able to recognize colors; write her/his own name; count by herself/himself up to ten, etc.), compared to 58
percent of students in Vietnam, and even higher proportions in other SEA-PLM participating countries. School readiness
skills are correlated with pre-school attendance, and students that have attended preschool show significant
improvements in their readiness skills. Enrollment in preschools has increased over the past decade, but access remains
limited and focusing only on 5-year-old children. Primary students reported the lowest rate for attending pre-school for
at least one year (58 percent and similar to Myanmar), whereas in Vietnam and Malaysia this is 97 and 98 percent,
respectively (SEA-PLM 2019 data).

7.    Strong readiness skills significantly improve a student’s ability to learn once in school, and pre-school
attendance considerably improves student learning outcomes. Statistical analysis of the SEA-PLM data shows that
children with all school readiness skills when starting primary school, were on average 0.4 academic years ahead of their
peers by grade 5. Similarly, SEA-PLM data analyses demonstrates that students who had completed 2 years of pre-school
were on average 0.4 years of schooling ahead of their peers with only 1 year of pre-school, and 0.7 years ahead
compared to students without pre-school.

Factor #2 – Teachers are not ready to teach.

8.    Primary teacher education levels in Cambodia continue to be very low compared to its regional peers. The
period of Khmer Rouge rule and the Cambodian genocide in the late 1970s had left a particularly devastating impact on
human capital and teachers in the country. Since then, Cambodia has made significant progress on improving teacher


                                                                                                                     Page 5
          The World Bank

     education levels, and it has progressively increased minimum qualification criteria for primary teachers. However,
     Cambodian primary teacher education levels continue to be low compared to regional peers, according to SEA-PLM
     2019 data. Less than a quarter of primary teachers has obtained a bachelor’s degree (or equivalent), and more than
     one-in-five primary teachers did not complete secondary education (i.e. a grade 12 certificate).

     9.     Better teaching practices are consistently associated with better student learning outcomes. Although many
     factors affect student learning—household income, parents’ education levels, and child nutrition—once children enter
     school, teachers are the most important factor (World Bank 2023). For instance, a 5th grade student who benefits from
     a teacher whose performance is ranked amongst the 10 percent best is 0.28 years ahead of a similar student whose
     teacher display an average performance. Evidence from neighboring Vietnam, where primary students and teachers
     have been tracked over time, show that differences in teaching quality mean that an average second-grade student can
     end up in either the top third or the bottom third of the class by the end of fifth grade, depending on whether they have
     benefited from high-quality or low-quality teachers during those school years (World Bank estimates based on Carneiro
     et al. 2022).

     10. In addition, teachers are poorly managed, absenteeism is high, and teachers are unequally distributed across
     schools resulting in teacher shortages. A 2015 study in Cambodian primary education schools estimated that actual
     instruction time was 27 percent shorter than intended instruction time (i.e. the curriculum) and highlighted the
     importance of teacher tardiness as well as teacher absenteeism around public holidays.7 Twenty-four percent of
     Cambodian students, in the SEA-PLM 2019 survey, reported that their teachers are often absent (the highest rate amongst
     SEA-PLM participating countries), and 21 percent reported their teacher is often late. Moreover, the distribution of
     teachers is uneven across regions and is not correlated with the number of students or school-age population. In 2015,
     it was calculated that urban schools, including pre-primary, primary and secondary schools, had a significant surplus of
     teachers while rural schools had considerable teacher shortages.8 Whilst students do not benefit from teacher surpluses
     (teachers in surplus schools typically work less hours per week), teacher shortages impede student learning, typically
     result in larger classes and can also induce schools to cut weekly lessons short (see factor 4 on more details with large
     classes).

     Factor #3: Students are being allowed to fall behind.


     11. A typical Cambodian 5th grade classroom has students’ whose reading skills range widely, making the task of
     effectively teaching such group very challenging. Nearly 24 percent of 5th grade students are only able to read a few
     simple words (a skill that should be mastered by the end of grade 1). And, at the other end, 19 percent are reading at
     global minimum proficiency standards (where they are expected to read short stories and answer questions about the
     text). Although it is common for 5th grade classes to include students at several different proficiency levels, the
     Cambodian within-class variation in reading proficiency is the highest amongst SEA-PLM participating countries. Eighty-
     nine percent of Cambodian classes included students at four different levels and 51 percent of classes included students
     at five different levels. This makes it exceedingly difficult to effectively teach to all students in class and makes slow
     learners especially vulnerable to falling behind. According to 2019 SEA-PLM data, around one-third of 5th graders had

     12. Well-designed remedial programs have proven to be successful in improving school outcomes for students.9
     Yet, despite a large proportion of Cambodian primary students falling behind, most primary schools do not provide
     targeted support to slow learners. In 2021, the Ministry issued an operational guideline to support slow learners, which
     includes clear instructions to identify those who are lagging behind and provision of online remedial teaching packages
     to teachers. These guidelines encourage teachers to seat slow learners next to their stronger peers, extend the
     instruction time 15 minutes daily, and utilize three Thursdays per month to help them catch up with other students.
     However, so far, there has been little effort to ensure the implementation of these guidelines, and data from a 2024
     survey on primary teachers and instruction time suggests that, in practice, the Thursday lessons are attended by almost
     all students and are de facto integrated into the regular lesson time. In the survey, 94 percent of teachers indicated that
     almost all students attended Thursday classes, and another 5 percent indicated that more than half of their students
     attended. As such, most students that are falling behind, do not have access to targeted support to help them catch up.

     Factor #4: The problem of slow learners is being compounded by large classes.


     13. Seventy six percent of primary students are learning in classes with more than 30 students, 29 percent are in
     classes with more than 40, and five percent are in classes with more than 50 students, according to MoEYS data (2022-
     23). The MoEYS target class size is 35 to 45 students per class in lower grades (grade 1 to 3) and 40 to 50 students for
     higher grades (grade 4 to 6). However, a significant share of classes exceeds these targets in part due to a shortage of
     classrooms, and in part due to an inefficient distribution of teachers and local teacher shortages. Classroom and teacher
     shortages prevent schools from splitting excessively large classes into two separate classes.

     14. Analyses of SEA-PLM data show that students attending large classes (with more than 30 students) are doing
     worse than students in smaller classes, and learning loss accelerates in very large classes (more than 50 students).
     When controlling for other factors, Cambodian primary students that attended a class with 40 students were 0.33 years
     of schooling behind their peers in classes smaller than 30 students, and student in classes with 50 students fell behind
     around 0.66 years of schooling. Learning loss accelerated with students in classes larger than 50 students. Importantly,
     this statistical relation is found in the specific context of Cambodian primary schools, where the challenges of teaching
     poorly prepared student, across a wide range of proficiency levels, and with poorly educated teachers, is further
     compounded by the challenge to teach students in large classes.


     Factor #5 – Poor infrastructure and unsafe environments further impede learning.


     15. There is a considerable shortage of classrooms forcing most primary schools to organize two shifts per day to
     accommodate all classes. There are 63,271 primary school classes and 47,031 classrooms across 7,338 primary schools
     (according to EMIS 2022-23). The shortage of 16,240 classrooms forces two-thirds of schools to organize two-shifts.
     However, even in schools with two shifts (where each classroom is used by two separate classes each day) there are still
     classroom shortages that force schools to hold lessons outside or alternate between classes.

     16. Furthermore, many schools lack basic amenities, and a significant number of schools need to be rehabilitated.
     Eighty-seven percent of primary schools lack gender segregated toilets and 85 percent lack canteens, according to 2019
     SEA-SPM data. Forty percent of primary schools also lack safe drinking water and almost one-third of schools does not
     have a hand washing station. Moreover, many buildings are in poor condition (structural problems, leaking roof, broken
     or cracked walls, thatched roof and/or walls, etc.) that require renovation or maintenance to ensure safety. According
     to the MoEYS, out of the more than 16,000 existing primary school buildings, nearly 15 percent of the school buildings
     in poor conditions require repairs, and 5 percent of schools is deemed irreparable and will need to be replaced.10 These
     conditions not only deteriorate access to education but also severely hamper student learning when inclement weather
     prevents students from attending schools and overcrowded classrooms which reduce effective learning experience.

     17. There is evidence that the physical features of schools, and the provision of basic services (such as safe drinking
     water) impact student attendance and student learning.11 For instance, analysis of SEA-PLM data on Cambodian
     primary student shows that safe environments and infrastructure matter for student learning outcomes (see also Barret
     et al. [2026]).12 Specifically, Cambodian primary students without access to gender-separated flush toilets are, on
     average, 0.15 years of schooling behind their peers with these facilities (controlling for other factors).

     Factor #6 – Management of the sector has not been modernized


     18. While the government has increased spending on education, spending remains very low by global standards.
     The increased fiscal space allowed the government to boost overall spending, which reached 20.4 percent of GDP in
     2021, up from 16.2 percent in 2011. In line with the government’s medium-term development vision, which prioritized
     human capital accumulation, the share of social sector spending more than doubled as a share of GDP to 7.7 percent in
     2021 from 3.7 in 2011. Education sector spending also substantially increased from 1.58 percent of GDP in 2011 to 2.16
     percent in 2022. The notable increase in education expenditure is absorbed mainly by education staff salaries, which
     constitute nearly 80 percent of total MoEYS expenditure in 2021. Capital expenditure by MoEYS has significantly
     declined, plummeting from 27 percent in 2011 to 17 percent of total expenditure in 2021. However, even with this
     increase, Cambodia’s education spending as percent of GDP is much lower than the average spent by lower middle-
     income countries (4.5 percent of GDP).

     19. Moreover, inadequate management information systems (MIS) hinder efficient human resource allocation,
     leading to unequal teacher distribution across schools. Some schools have twice as many teachers per class compared
     to others, causing significant disparities. In schools with teacher shortages, teachers are overburdened by the additional
     lessons they need to teach, and schools sometimes decide to cut the number of lessons per week. The current MIS does
     not comprehensively register all human resources at the school level (such as temporary teacher contracts and overtime
     worked), which prevents an accurate diagnosis and impedes the MoEYS from effectively addressing the issue. The
     transfer of basic education functions to district and municipal administrations, as part of D&D reforms, necessitates
     improved HR information for both management and planning purposes.

Education sector plan 2024-28

 7.      In its Education Sector Plan (2024-28), the MoEYS has outlined a plan to improve educational outcomes. The
 plan aims to ensure Cambodian children are enrolled in Early Childhood Education (ECE) programs and ready for primary
 school at age six. It also seeks to have all primary school-aged children complete primary school with good competency
 in core subjects and life skills, and for Cambodian youth to complete secondary education with the necessary knowledge,
 skills, discipline, ethics, and behavior for a better future.

 8.     To achieve these goals, the plan includes several strategic actions. First, the plan targets 76 percent of grade-one
 entrants to have preprimary school experience by 2028. It aims to increase ECE enrollment through the provision of
 more preprimary schools and well-trained ECE teachers, as well as community-based childcare centers and parental
 education. Second, it aims to scale up Early Grade Learning (EGL) in all primary schools, addressing gaps in early grade
 reading (EGR) and early grade math (EGM). Currently, there are significant gaps in EGR implementation for grade 3 and
 EGM implementation for grades 2 and 3. Third, it proposes interventions for students falling behind, such as additional
 teaching hours and learning clubs. However, as discussed above, more concrete actions are needed on this front. Fourth,
 it plans to attract and retain education specialists through Continuous Professional Development (CPD) and Teacher
 Career Pathways (TCP), with an intention to produce 300 in-house education specialists by 2028. And fifth, it emphasizes
 the need for school construction and renovation to reduce overcrowded classrooms, especially in suburban, northern,
 and western regions.

 D. Concept Description

 14. The project’s activities have been selected because of their strong evidence base for improving student learning
 outcomes, at scale and in a cost effective way.13 As will be discussed below, the ambition of the proposed project is to
 ensure that every primary school student in the country can achieve minimum numeracy and literacy. As such, the project
 has been designed to be scalable in scope and cost, allowing it to potentially accommodate additional finance or follow-
 on projects and/or additional resources from development partners, should those resources materialize.

 15. The proposed project will complement and build on activities already under implementation by other development
 partners, and an ongoing IDA project, the General Education Improvement Project (GEIP). Specifically, first, as will be
 discussed in more details in subcomponent 2.2 and in box 1, several development partners have supported the design
 and, in select provinces, supported implementing of a best-practice way of strengthening teaching, using an Early Grade
 Learning package. BEIP will build on these efforts, seeking to bring them to nationwide implementation, and
 institutionalize them. Second, the GEIP focuses on establishing robust school governance, strengthening school
 management, improving accountability, and enhancing community engagement. This governance-focused approach
 creates the foundational structure for the proposed project to effectively implement its teaching and learning
 interventions, including Early Grade Learning (EGL) and Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL), along with increased
 instructional hours and enhanced teaching materials. While GEIP improves school management capacity, student
 assessments, and infrastructure across 2,000 primary schools, BEIP will strengthen the teaching approach in classrooms
 and institutionalize the mentoring system for teachers, not only in GEIP targeted schools but in all schools (building on
 the foundation laid by other development partners).

Component 2: Leveraging Cost-Effective Solutions to Enhance Student Learning (US$88.0 million)

     18. This component supports activities that address some of the underlying causes of poor learning outcomes at the
     primary grades (see discussion above). The proposed sub-components include:

Sub-Component 2.1: More and better use of instruction time (US$7.0 million)

     19. Using additional instructional hours to reinforce content helps students learn and could reduce inequality on
     student learning outcomes. The proposed subcomponent aims to ensure that these additional hours result in more
     student learning, especially for students who have fallen behind through i) making effective use of the currently prescribed
     instructional hours (such as effective classroom planning and preparation, eliminating free time and maximizing engaging
     learning opportunities), ii) adding extra hours to the existing school schedule, and iii) providing additional classrooms in
     overcrowded schools, reducing class size to enhance learning experience. Different approaches could be considered to
     extend the current instructional hours, considering (i) the additional cost implication of the extension, including possible
     cost-sharing arrangements between MoEYS and parents; and (ii) the equity considerations to ensure that poorer
     households can also benefit from the extended hours. On the cost front, costs will vary depending on whether additional
     classrooms are needed to move to a single-shift full-day schooling.

     20. There is growing empirical evidence on what are the most cost-effective ways of accelerating student learning, with
     Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL) singled out as one of the most effective interventions to accelerate learning, at scale,
     and in a cost-effective way.14 Specifically, TaRL is an evidence-based approach that focuses on assessing children's learning
     levels and tailoring instruction to meet their specific needs. It emphasizes group-based learning activities, targeted
     instruction, and continuous assessment to help children build foundational literacy and numeracy skills effectively. This
     component will introduce this approach to the hours set aside for Cambodia’s remedial instruction (see paragraph 18 for
     a description of how these hours are currently used). The project will support the development of intervention packages
     for reading and math, training teachers to conduct diagnostic assessments and use the intervention packages to support
     the students who fall behind their grades, setting up structures to support teachers and school leaders in implementing
     TaRL, and building the capacity of national and sub-national education staff to own and sustain the program.

Sub-component 2.2: Enhancing the implementation of Early Grade Learning (US$45.0 million)

     21. Apart from TaRL, the other “smart buy�? to accelerate student learning is Early Grade Reading (EGM) and Early Grade
     Mathematics (EGM) programs with benefit-cost ratios ranging from 5 to 1 to 12 to 1 (World Bank 2023). Such programs
     consist of four core activities (i) teachers are provided with scripted lesson plans; (ii) teachers are provided with initial
     training on how to use these scripts and new material; (iii) material is developed and provided to students; (iv) teachers
     are provided with ongoing mentoring support through the deployment of core mentors15. In addition, the programs have
     a heavy emphasis on monitoring and evaluation, providing continuous feedback on progress being made by students.

     22. In Cambodia’s case, with support from other donors, EGR and EGM have already been designed, implementation
     have already begun, and the efforts have been rigorously evaluated showing impressive results. This subcomponent aims

 Sub-component 2.3: Investing in learning-enhancing infrastructure (US$35 million)

     24. As mentioned above, the issue of overcrowded classrooms is prevalent in Cambodian schools with 76 percent of
     primary students are learning in classes with more than 30 students, and analysis of SEA-PLM 2019 data showing that
     these large classes are impacting student learning (see above). Reducing the number of such very large classes would
     allow teachers to pay more attend to students’ individual needs, to enforce behavioral expectations, and to closely
     monitor students’ performance.16

     25. This subcomponent aims to improve the learning environment and will finance the climate-resilient construction of
     additional classrooms in overcrowded schools and ensure the availability of gender-separated toilets and WASH facilities.
     The school buildings must have climate-informed designs, ensure energy efficiency, and be able to be designated as
     emergency shelters. To improve learning environment, the project will also provide the necessary facilities and
     equipment, such as digital tools, and library books. Where lack of classrooms is the binding constraint to reducing large
     classes, the project will finance such construction.

Component 4: Contingent Emergency Response (CERC)

 31. The objective of the contingent emergency response component (CERC) is to allow for the reallocation of financing
 in accordance with the IDA Immediate Response Mechanism in order to provide an immediate response to an eligible
 crisis or emergency, as needed. Learning from the lessons of the current COVID-19 pandemic, the project will develop
 Emergency Response Manual (ERM) and other necessary documents to ensure that CERC can be activated at any time
 during the emergency. While the ERM will be designed to release IDA financing, it will also serve as a guided document
 for the whole sector in the case of an emergency.
