    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context

1.      Ghana entered a severe macroeconomic crisis at the end of 2022, as pre-existing structural weaknesses and fiscal
imbalances were compounded by external shocks and policy slippages. In response, the Government of Ghana launched a
comprehensive debt restructuring initiative at the end of that year. The combined effects of these economic challenges, fiscal
consolidation measures, and high inflation have significantly constrained the environment for poverty reduction efforts in
the country.

2.        Ghana’s international poverty rate (measured at US$3.00 per day in 2021 purchasing power parity) is estimated
to have risen from 39.0 percent in 2017 to 40.0 percent in 2023, before slightly declining to 39.6 percent in 2024 . Inflation
alone is estimated to have pushed over 800,000 Ghanaians into poverty. Looking ahead, poverty is projected to rise again by
2027 due to a moderate growth outlook and persistent inflation beginning in 2025. In this context, while macroeconomic
stabilization remains a prerequisite for sustained poverty reduction, the continued strengthening of the Livelihoods
Empowerment Against Poverty (LEAP) program—Ghana’s flagship cash transfer initiative—along with the social spending
floor under the IMF-supported program, will be critical to protecting the poorest and most vulnerable from the adverse
effects of fiscal consolidation.

3.      According to the 2023 UNDP Human Development Report, Ghana’s Human Development Index (HDI) was
approximately 0.628, placing the country around 143rd out of 193 nations. However, when adjusted for inequality, the HDI
drops to about 0.399—reflecting a 36.5 percent loss and highlighting persistent structural disparities in access to education,
healthcare, and income. Gender inequality remains a significant challenge: the HDI for females was estimated at 0.607,
compared to 0.651 for males. Additionally, Ghana’s Gender Inequality Index (GII) stood at 0.514, indicating substantial
gender-based gaps in reproductive health, empowerment, and economic participation.

4.       In Liberia, the most recent comprehensive data from the 2016 Liberia Household Income and Expenditure Survey
(HIES) indicated a national poverty rate of 58.9 percent.1 Rural areas remain particularly vulnerable, with poverty rising
significantly—from 70.0 percent in 2014 to 81.2 percent in 2016—highlighting their limited resilience. The country continues
to grapple with the lingering effects of past crises, including the Ebola outbreak and the ongoing impacts of the COVID-19
pandemic, both of which have disrupted livelihoods and strained institutional capacity. Additionally, natural disasters such
as floods and storms have further exacerbated household vulnerabilities, especially in urban and coastal regions. Due to the
outdated nature of official survey data, the World Bank has relied on a microsimulation approach to estimate poverty trends.
While poverty levels rose sharply in recent years, the national poverty rate is estimated to have stabilized at around 60
percent as of 2024, following a gradual recovery from economic shocks.

5.     According to the 2023 UNDP Human Development Report, Liberia’s HDI was approximately 0.467, ranking the
country around 185th out of 193 nations. When adjusted for inequality, the HDI drops to about 0.281—reflecting a 39.8

6.       Accurate and timely data are essential for addressing the development challenges faced by both Ghana and Liberia.
Reliable statistics support evidence-based decision-making and help optimize the use of limited resources amid competing
priorities. Strong national data systems are critical for designing effective policies, monitoring progress, and evaluating

standardized data collection also enhances regional collaboration and helps illuminate the marginalization of women, youth,
persons with disabilities, and rural populations, thereby informing policies and programs that aim to leave no one behind.

 Sectoral and Institutional Context

7.      Ghana’s macroeconomic situation, though still fragile, has improved significantly over the past two years. In 2023,
economic growth proved more resilient than anticipated, reaching 3.1 percent, while inflation declined sharply from a peak
of 53.7 percent in December 2022 to 23.1 percent by the end of 2023. In 2024, Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth
exceeded expectations, rising to 5.7 percent. This strong performance was driven by the industry sector—particularly mining
and construction—as well as the services sector, with notable contributions from information and communications, financial
and insurance services, and transport. On the expenditure side, growth was supported by increased public consumption and
a rebound in investment spending.

8.      In Ghana, inflation reached 23.7 percent by the end of 2024, driven primarily by rising food prices and currency
depreciation. The external sector showed improvement, with the current account recording a surplus of 3.2 percent of GDP
by year-end—supported by increased gold and crude oil exports, along with strong remittance inflows. However, fiscal
performance deteriorated in 2024, as both the primary and overall fiscal deficits exceeded targets, reaching 3.7 percent and
7.7 percent of GDP, respectively. This was largely due to the accumulation of arrears and unbudgeted expenditures. Looking
ahead, GDP growth in 2025 is projected to moderate to 4.3 percent, reflecting the impact of a larger fiscal adjustment, slowing
momentum in the extractives sector, persistent inflation, and elevated interest rates.

9.       Despite being more advanced than many of its regional counterparts, Ghana’s statistical system continues to face
institutional and financial challenges that affect its performance and long-term sustainability. The Ghana Statistical Service
(GSS) has made notable progress in modernizing data collection—such as implementing nationwide Computer-Assisted
Personal Interview (CAPI) based price monitoring and regular reporting—but key constraints persist. These include limited
fiscal space, inconsistent funding, and hiring restrictions, which hinder staffing, retention, and the ability to offer competitive
incentives. Additionally, coordination across ministries, departments, and agencies remains a challenge, particularly in
integrating administrative and survey data.

10.     While Ghana has benefited from previous strategies like the National Strategy for the Development of Statistics
(NSDS), the current framework is due for renewal to meet evolving data demands and leverage emerging technologies .
The government is currently preparing the next-generation National Strategy for the Development of Statistics (NSDS), which
aims to strengthen statistical governance, expand institutional capacity, and enhance data interoperability in line with
international standards. This renewed commitment positions Ghana to further advance evidence-based policymaking and
accelerate progress toward national and global development goals.

11.       Liberia’s economy is primarily driven by the services and agriculture sectors, which together account for a
substantial share of GDP and employment. In 2023, agriculture contributed 35.8 percent to GDP and remains the backbone
of rural livelihoods, largely dominated by smallholder farming. However, the sector faces persistent challenges, including
limited access to quality inputs, inadequate extension services, poor rural infrastructure—such as roads and storage
facilities—and heightened vulnerability to climate change impacts like erratic rainfall and flooding. These constraints
continue to limit productivity and contribute to widespread food insecurity and rural poverty. While efforts are underway to
modernize agriculture through improved technologies, value chain development, and enhanced market linkages, these
initiatives require significant investment and sustained policy support.

12.      In addition, Liberia’s economy remains heavily dependent on commodity exports—particularly iron ore and
rubber—making it vulnerable to global market fluctuations. Economic diversification is therefore essential to fostering
inclusive growth and achieving sustainable poverty reduction. The country continues to face high inflation and modest
economic growth, both of which have deepened vulnerabilities among poor households. Structural challenges —including
inadequate infrastructure, limited access to reliable energy, gaps in human capital development, and governance
constraints—further hinder progress. Addressing these barriers is critical to unlocking Liberia’s development potential and
ensuring that economic growth translates into tangible improvements in living standards for all Liberians.

13.     Liberia’s statistical system continues to face institutional weaknesses and inconsistent financing, which hinder its
effectiveness and sustainability. The national statistical system lacks the autonomy to recruit qualified personnel or
implement competitive incentive structures. Limited administrative and technical coordination further constrains
performance. In addition, unreliable and insufficient funding has led to infrequent data collection, persistent staffing
shortages, outdated methodologies, and limited public access to quality data.

14.      Liberia’s first NSDS, launched in 2008, aimed to establish a coherent and harmonized national statistical system
aligned with United Nations (UN) principles. The second strategy, NSDS II (2018–2022), built on this foundation by
emphasizing capacity development, integration of multi-sectoral data systems, and responsiveness to both national and
international data needs. As NSDS II is now outdated, the government is preparing to launch NSDS III. This new strategy aims
to transform Liberia’s statistical system by aligning with global best practices in data collection, analysis, and disseminat ion.
It reflects a renewed commitment to building a robust data ecosystem that supports evidence-based policymaking and
sustainable development.

15.     Although Liberia’s civil war ended over 15 years ago, the country remains in a post-conflict recovery phase, working
to strengthen governance structures, public sector capacity, and service delivery systems. Key government institutions
continue to face challenges such as limited financial resources, weak inter-agency coordination, and gaps in technical
expertise, all of which hinder the effective implementation of development programs and reforms. With support from
international partners, the government has prioritized institutional strengthening through initiatives aimed at enhancing
transparency, accountability, and decentralization. Reinforcing institutional capacity is essential to creating an enabling
environment for private sector development, improving public service delivery, and achieving sustainable development
outcomes.

 D. Project Description

16.      The Harmonizing and Improving Statistics in West Africa (HISWA) project was approved by the World Bank Board
of Executive Directors on March 26, 2020. The project includes nine recipients with a total financing amount of US$379
million from the National Performance-Based Allocations (PBA) International Development Association (IDA) and Regional
IDA Windows. Seven project participating countries, namely, Burkina Faso (IDA Credit 65730 - US$30 million and IDA Grant
D5810 - US$30 million), Cabo Verde (IDA Credit 65740 - US$15 million), Cote d’Ivoire (IDA Credit 65750 - US$54 million),
Ghana (IDA Credit 65760 - US$150 million), Liberia (IDA Credit 65770 - US$15 million and IDA Grant D5830 - US$15 million),
Sierra Leone (IDA Grant D5840 - US$30 million) and Togo (IDA Credit 65780 - US$15 million and IDA Grant D5850 - US$15
million), as well as two regional organizations, namely, the African Union (AU) (IDA Grant D5880 - US$2.5 million) and the
Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) (IDA Grant D5820 - US$7.5 million).

17.      The parent project aims to address critical data gaps by harmonizing, producing, disseminating, and enhancing the
use of essential economic and social statistics. It seeks to improve the statistical capacity of participating countries, ensuring
that their National Statistical Systems (NSS) respond to user needs and generate more comparable and reliable statistics,
thereby facilitating better decision-making. Furthermore, the project supports the modernization of statistical methods,
organizational structures, physical infrastructure, and Information and Communication Technology (ICT) within National
Statistics Offices (NSOs), as well as Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs) responsible for statistics. This
modernization will empower these entities to fulfill their mandates more effectively.

18.       The parent project is structured into three key components.
      •   Component 1: Regional Component: Harmonization, data collection, quality improvement, dissemination and use
          of core social and economic statistics. This component funds activities aiming at supporting harmonized
          methodologies, producing core economic and social statistics, improving administrative statistics, providing training
          on methodological advancements, building human capacity, and enhancing data quality and timeliness.
      •   Component 2: Country-Specific Component: Improving or modernizing physical and statistical infrastructure. The
          activities in this component aim to address the specific constraints and needs of each NSO. They consider the
          necessary interventions, including physical, technological, and statistical infrastructure, human capacity, and legal or
          regulatory frameworks, required for the NSO to successfully implement the program of activities outlined in

19.   The first Additional Financing in the amount of US$9.6 million was approved on June 16, 2025, for Cote d’Ivoire
US$6 million and Sierra Leone US$3.6 million that brought the total project financing amount to Us$388.6 million.

20.     The proposed second Additional Financing aligns with the HISWA parent Project components and subcomponents,
as well as the World Bank's Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Ghana (Report No. 157249, 2022-2026) and Liberia
(Report No. CPF0000043, 2025-2030). The total AF amount of US$6.2 million--comprising $US$3.0 million for Ghana and
US$3.2 million for Liberia—will be allocated to components 1 and 3, specifically to address the cost overrun affecting the
implementation of key activities

21.     All documents produced during the preparation and implementation of the HISWA parent project, including the
procurement plan and the Project Procurement Strategy for Development (PPSD), remain relevant for the AF and may only
require minor revisions. Since the parent project was prepared under the previous Safeguard Policy, the Project Information
Document (PID) and the Integrated Safeguards Data Sheet (ISDS) will be updated accordingly for this AF.

22.     During the Mid-Term Review and the most recent implementation support missions, participating countries raised
concerns that cost-overruns could jeopardize the completion of key project-financed activities. These overruns have
resulted in increased expenditures across several components and may require adjustments to ensure the timely and
effective delivery of project outcomes.

23.      The delays and cost overruns encountered under the parent project stemmed from a combination of internal and
external factors. Implementation capacity had to be built in both the implementing entity and the fiduciary agencies during
the initial stage of project implementation. This slowed the execution of planned activities and contributed to increased
operational expenses. Delays in procurement planning, lengthy approval processes, and limited competition in bidding
contributed to cost escalations. The lack of robust contract management and oversight further exacerbated these issues,
leading to procurement-related bottlenecks and the need for costly adjustments later in implementation.

24.      Externally, several major shocks significantly drove up project costs. The COVID-19 pandemic severely disrupted
global supply chains, delaying procurement and bidding processes, and triggering sharp price increases for essential goods
and services. Items such as vehicles, computers, tablets, interviewer kits, and other data collection equipment became
substantially more expensive than originally budgeted. The pandemic also introduced logistical challenges for survey
implementation. Lockdowns and movement restrictions required major adjustments to fieldwork operations, leading to
increased costs for enumerator training, extended data collection periods, vehicle rentals, and additional staff compensation.
Collectively, these adjustments substantially raised the overall cost of survey operations.

25.     At the same time, governments across participating countries increasingly demanded more frequent and timely
data to monitor not only the social and economic impacts of COVID-19 on households and businesses. In response, the
project supported the rapid design and implementation of multiple rounds of phone-based surveys to address critical data
gaps. While these surveys were more feasible during lockdowns and ensured continuity in data collection, they also
introduced new cost drivers. These included expenses related to call centers or mobile service providers, airtime and
respondent incentives, enumerator training for phone-based interviews, software customization, and remote data
processing systems. In Ghana and Liberia, many rounds of the COVID-19 Impact Monitoring Survey (CIMS) were conducted,
providing critical real-time data to inform government response efforts.

26.      As a result, cost overruns have affected multiple components of the project, particularly those linked to data
collection, production, and dissemination. Given the limited resources, the AF has been strategically designed to prioritize
only the most critical statistical activities in Ghana and Liberia. These activities are deemed essential to achieving the PDO
and for maintaining the integrity of national statistical systems.

27.     The proposed AF also entails a restructuring of the parent project to: (i) extend its closing date of the original
financing for Ghana (IDA-65760) and Liberia (IDA Credit 65770 and IDA Grant D5830) from December 31, 2025, to December
31, 2026; (ii) align the AF closing date for with the new parent project closing date of December 31, 2026; (iii) revise
component costs accordingly; (iv) update the results framework end target date to reflect the revised timeline for both
countries; (v) adjust the project implementation schedule. This AF does not introduce new project components, nor does it
expand the project’s scope or geographic coverage. The parent project was originally prepared—and remains governed—
under the World Bank Safeguards Policy.


 E. Implementation

 28.     The parent project officially commenced on July 16, 2020, with the Ghana Financing Agreement becoming
 effective on that date. Liberia’s Financing Agreement became effective later, on December 2, 2020. As of now, the overall
 project disbursement rate stands at 86.8 percent. Both progress toward achieving the Project Development Objective (PDO)
 and overall implementation are rated satisfactory. Disbursement rates for Ghana and Liberia are 93.1 percent and 87
 percent, respectively. The project has made substantial progress in strengthening NSS by addressing critical data gaps and
 supporting the production of key statistical outputs—including population, business, and agricultural censuses, as well as a
 range of core household and socio-economic surveys. It has also enhanced the harmonization, dissemination, and utilization
 of high-quality data to inform, monitor, and evaluate public policies.


 Institutional and Implementation Arrangements

29.     The implementation arrangements under the Additional Financing will remain the same as those for the parent
project. As with the parent project, GSS will be the implementing agency and the Coordination and Program Management
Directorate (CPMD) within GSS will coordinate all project activities of the AF, including financial and procurement
management. In Liberia, the PIU within LISGIS and the Project Financial Management Unit (PFMU) in the Ministry of Finance
will continue to share responsibilities in managing the project's day-to-day operations, including administration, reporting,
and auditing. No changes to the existing arrangements are anticipated.

A. Summary of Key Safeguard Issues


1. Describe any safeguard issues and impacts associated with the Restructured project. Identify and describe any
  potential large scale, significant and/or irreversible impacts.
  The safeguard issues and impacts asssociated with the proposed AF has no potential large scale, significant and/or
  irreversible impact.

  In Liberia, under Component 2, the project involves the refurbishment of the LISGIS building, which includes: (i)
  repainting the building and offices; (ii) cleaning and polishing floor tiles; (iii) performing minor repairs on windows
  and doors; and (iv) replacing select existing zinc roof sheets. These activities are currently underway. They have
  minimal environmental and social impact, but a system is in place for screening at the Project Implementation Unit
  (PIU) level, and contractors are directly supervised by the PIU as they implement mitigation measures derived from
  the screening process.

  In Ghana the AF follows the World Bank’s Operational Policies with the same Safeguard policies. There is no change
  to the project’s environmental and social risk category, it will remain the same to the parent project. The potential
  span low to moderate risks and can be mitigated through good practices and appropriate mitigation measures. GSS
  has demonstrated commitment to integrating and implementing environmental and social safeguards, achieving a
  performance rating of moderately satisfactory. Documentation, monitoring, and reporting need continued attention.
  Capacity building and better coordination with national agencies can strengthen implementation. The GRM is
