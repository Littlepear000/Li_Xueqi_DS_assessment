     B. Introduction and Context

     Country Context

 1.      Costa Rica has been one of the most politically stable, progressive, and prosperous nations in the Latin America
 and the Caribbean (LAC) region.1 Successive governments have prioritized social welfare and development of the Costa
 Rican people. Political stability, the social compact, and steady growth have resulted in one of the lowest poverty rates in
 LAC (2021 poverty headcount rate at upper middle-income poverty line was 14.3 percent), with strong income growth
 among the 40 percent of the population with the lowest incomes. The country of 5.2 million inhabitants became a member
 of the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) in 2021.

 2.      Despite these achievements, the country faces structural challenges that have been intensified by the COVID-
 pandemic. Unemployment rates nearly doubled during the pandemic, surpassing 20 percent in mid-2020, and family
 income declined despite the government’s emergency response. Employment data indicates large gaps in terms of gender,
 age, and region, with the most disadvantaged being women, youth, and rural populations.

3.      Costa Rica is vulnerable to natural disasters such as extreme weather events that have been exacerbated by
climate change. Significant changes in the patterns of precipitation and increases in temperatures have been recorded in
the recent decades, as well as changes in land use and its degradation. In the future, the climate in Costa Rica will be
marked by dry extremes, such as the drought of 2008 and rainy extremes, such as during the El Niño of 2014-2015.2 The
country is also exposed to high risk of river, urban and coastal floods, meaning that potentially damaging and life-
threatening river floods are expected to occur at least once in the next 10 years.3 The risk level for earthquakes in the
country is classified as high, indicating a greater than 20% chance of a potentially damaging earthquake occurring within
the next 50 years. Additionally, there are high risks of landslides, tsunamis, and volcanic hazards.

     Sectoral and Institutional Context

 4.      Costa Rica’s spending on education has consistently been high compared to other countries, and educational
 indicators are amongst the best in LAC. The literacy rate for adults aged 15 and older is 97 percent, and the share of adults
 aged 25 and above who had no formal education has declined from 21.2 percent in 1950 to 3.4 percent. Younger cohorts
 are also attaining more years of education as time goes by; 25–29 year-olds had only about four years of education on
 average in 1950; in 1980 they had on average a complete primary education (above six years), and in 2010 they had a
 complete basic education (nine years on average).4 According to the 2021 Human Capital Index (HCI), a child born in Costa
 Rica can expect to be 63 percent as productive as he or she could be with the current education and health services
 available. Though this number appears low, it is higher than the LAC average.

 5.      Costa Rica spends more than six percent of GDP on education compared to the OECD average of five percent,
 but learning results are substantively behind the OECD average. While spending on education has stopped growing in
 the past 4 years, since 2010 the constitution mandates spending 8 percent of GDP on education, above the OECD average
 of 5 percent. Spending is in the opposite comparative direction of the results obtained in terms of student learning.
 Learning outcomes in Costa Rica are more strongly correlated to socioeconomic conditions than other OECD countries,
 which does not bode well for the future of productivity and social harmony. The OECD average difference in adequate
 performance (children achieving at least PISA Level 2 in reading) between children from the bottom and top quartiles of
 the PISA index of economic, social, and cultural status in 2018 was 29 percent. In Costa Rica, the difference in performance
 between the top and bottom quintile was a much higher 50 percent. Differences in educational attainment tend to
 become magnified through youth and adult life in the acquisition of human capital.

 6.       Gender imbalances in learning attainment exacerbate learning gaps correlated with socioeconomic conditions .
 PISA results, further confirmed by regional assessments, show gaps in performance between boys and girls, with girls
 outperforming boys in reading, and boys outperforming girls in mathematics, sciences, and a measure of digital literacy.
 Employers decry the paucity of adequate Science, Technology, Art, and Mathematics (STEAM) skills, an area where
 diversity is critically important. Given the relationship between mathematics and digital skills and the digital economy that
 Costa Rica wants to create, the gender gap against girls is a cause for concern, as is the gap against boys for the
 foundational skill of reading.

 7.      Costa Rica received more than 200,000 migrant refugees in 2022 and though public schools are legally bound
 to enroll migrant children, the increase in migration places stress on the education. In recent decades, the country has
 seen the arrival of migrants and refugee applicants fleeing political and economic conditions in Colombia, Cuba, El
 Salvador, Nicaragua, and Venezuela. According to UNHCR, the number of asylum seekers in Costa Rica increased from
 33,000 in 2018 to nearly 240,000 in 2022. While the country offers public education to all children regardless of migratory
 status, more efforts are needed to ensure that enrollment requirements do not exclude migrant children from the
 classroom, and that learning environments are inclusive. The last migration cycle has also shown that a significant number
 of migrants and asylum seekers aspire to professional careers and/or possess high levels of education, but there are still
 challenges to ensure that these students can continue their studies to allow the country to benefit from their skills and
 experience.

 8.        The MEP intends to implement a far-reaching reform program, outlined in the Ruta de la Educación (Roadmap
 for Education: 2022-2026). The central objective of the Ruta is for the education system “to develop responsible citizens
 with lifelong competencies for solidarity for life and dignified work.�? The Ruta presents a comprehensive series of reforms,
 a number of which have already begun implementation. The major markers of the Ruta are: (i) administrative and
 management system reform; (ii) strengthening the curriculum with programs and policy measures directed towards
 building lifelong competencies and employability/entrepreneurship skills, including English proficiency and digital skills;
 (iii) revitalizing pedagogy with programs for accelerated learning, directed interventions with early warning for at risk
 students, and a focus on enhancing foundational learning; (iv) teacher professional development and teacher
 accompaniment; (v) investment in connectivity, digital equipment, and related training for hybrid instruction; (vi) other
 interventions, including early childhood education, enhancing student welfare, infrastructure strengthening, updated
 student assessment including both formative and summative assessment, equity-oriented programs, including a program
 for migrants and indigenous education, and programs for innovation in service delivery. The implementation of the Ruta
 is intended to be a responsibility beyond merely the Government, with alliances from different private sector stakeholders
 and civil society.

  Relationship to CPF

 9.       This Project is aligned with HLO 2 (Strengthened and Sustained Human Capital) of the upcoming Costa Rica
 Country Partnership Framework for FY24-FY28. The Project is specifically aligned with CPF Objective 3: Education Sector
 Overhaul. Priorities for the education system described in the CPF include elements that inform the proposed project: (i)
 quality of education; (ii) equity in educational opportunity; and (iii) relevance of education for success in the labor market.
 Finally, the CPF mentions that closing the digital skills gap is critical to reduce the illiteracy and income inequality divide.
 The inclusion of digital competencies as curricular content in the educational system will stimulate internet access and
 use. The project will also support the CPF approach aimed at fostering skills for green jobs and employability in high value-
 added sectors.

 10.     The Project is consistent with Costa Rica’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC), submitted in December
 2020 and its National Decarbonization Plan developed in 2019. Costa Rica committed to be a net zero emissions economy
 by 2050. Its plan identifies three implementation phases (initial, inflection and normalization) through ten decarbonization
 axes and eight cross-cutting strategies. In the 2020 NDC update, adaptation and mitigation targets were established
 through 13 areas of action: mobility and transportation; development and land use planning; energy; infrastructure and
 construction; industry, commerce and services; integrated waste management; agriculture; forests and terrestrial
 biodiversity; oceans, water resources and blue biodiversity; action for climate empowerment; transparency and
 continuous improvement; finance; and climate change finance, strategies and plans. The Project contributes to the NDC
 through teacher training modules on climate change and adaptation and digital modes of learning.

 11.    The proposed project is being prepared in close coordination with a proposed project supporting the
 Government’s education sector reform being financed by the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB). Both the IDB
 and IBRD financed projects will support the education sector reform.

     D. Concept Description

 13.       The Project will support the administrative and pedagogical transformation of the education sector through
 five components, including the use of Performance Based Conditions (PBCs) under three components. The components
 are: (i) Support for Administrative Transformation and Information Systems Modernization; (ii) Support for Pedagogical
 Transformation for Competency-Based Learning; (iii) Support for Inclusive Pathways to Employability; (iv) Project
 Management and Technical Assistance; and (v) Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC). PBCs would allow a
 portion of loan disbursements based on achievement of agreed results under Components 1, 2, and 3, and provide for
 reimbursement of incurred eligible expenditures. A portion of the loan disbursements would also be made under
 investment project financing, through procurement of required items. Potential PBCs to be included are:

            PBC 1: Implementation of the deconcentration of selected functions to regional offices
            PBC 2: Implementation of a learning recovery program (including curriculum consolidation, teacher guides and
            assessments) at the Secondary level
            PBC 3: Migrant and Indigenous Students receive targeted support

 14.     Component 1: Support for Administrative Transformation and Information Systems Modernization. This
 component will support the administrative transformation of the Ministry of Public Education (MEP) to transform
 management practices with the objective to increase the system’s efficiency. It will include a subcomponent focused on
 the inventory, evaluation and reform of the MEP’s administrative structure and a subcomponent on information systems
 modernization. This component will finance consultancies, development and acquisition of software, cloud
 implementation activities and related licensing and technical assistance.

 15.     Component 2: Support Pedagogical Transformation for Competency-Based Learning. Component 2 will support
 the pedagogical transformation of the education system, for competency-based learning to meet the overarching
 educational goal of “responsible citizens with lifelong competencies for solidarity for life and dignified work.�? . The first
 subcomponent will focus on learning recovery and acceleration at the Secondary level, and the second one on supporting
 hybrid learning and connectivity in schools. This component will finance technical assistance (firms and individuals),
 procurement of didactic materials, and the establishment and functioning of internet connections and equipment (smart
 boards, projectors, headphones).

 16.       Component 3: Support for Inclusive Pathways to Employability. Component 3 will support relevant and inclusive
 skills through the delivery of skills for employability for all students, and activities targeting indigenous communities, and
 migrants, refugees as well their host communities. The first subcomponent will focus on employability skills, while the

 18.     Component 5: Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC). Due to the Costa Rica’s vulnerability to

 not have any initial funding allocation, but in the event of an emergency, uncommitted funds could be reallocated from
 other components in accordance with an Emergency Action Plan prepared by the Government and the CERC’s
 implementation modalities.
