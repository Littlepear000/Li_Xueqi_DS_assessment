    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context

1.       Côte d’Ivoire has experienced strong and sustained economic growth since the end of its civil conflict in 2011,
emerging as one of West Africa’s fastest-growing economies. Between 2012 and 2019, it was one of the fastest-growing
economies in Sub-Saharan Africa, with an average annual GDP growth rate of around 8 percent, driven largely by private
sector activity in services, retail, and construction. However, while national poverty declined from 55 percent in 2011 to
39.4 percent in 2018, the benefits of growth have been unevenly distributed. Rural poverty remains high, decreasing only
marginally from 62.5 percent in 2008 to 56.8 percent in 2015, and more recent data suggests stagnation in rural poverty
reduction. The 2019 UNDP Human Development Report placed Côte d’Ivoire’s HDI at 0.516, ranking it 165 out of 189
countries. When adjusted for inequality, the HDI falls to 0.331—a 35.8 percent loss—highlighting persistent structural
disparities. Gender inequality is also pronounced: the HDI for women was 0.445 compared to 0.559 for men, and the
Gender Inequality Index (GII) was 0.657, ranking the country 157 out of 162. Recent poverty assessments using the 2021/22
Harmonized Survey of Household Living Conditions (EHCVM) confirm that poverty reduction continues to be concentrated
in urban areas, particularly Abidjan, while rural areas continue to experience high poverty rates. The national poverty rate
declined only slightly from 39.5 percent in 2018 to 37.5 percent in 2021, rural poverty remained largely stagnant. Given
these disparities, Côte d’Ivoire’s future development strategy emphasizes inclusive growth—especially in rural areas and
for women—through expanding social protection and targeted poverty reduction programs and (ii) enhancing gender
equity by improving access to education, health, and economic opportunities.

2.       In Sierra Leone, the most recent available data from the 2018 Integrated Household Survey (SLIHS) indicates that
the national poverty rate was 56.8 percent in 2018. The country continues to grapple with the long-term impacts of
conflict, the Ebola epidemic, and more recently, the Coronavirus Disease (COVID-19) pandemic—all of which have disrupted
livelihoods and weakened institutions. Natural disasters such as floods and landslides have further deepened household
vulnerabilities, particularly in urban and low-lying coastal areas. As of 2024, the national poverty rate is estimated to have
increased to 57.8 percent.1 Sierra Leone, like many resource-rich African nations, has historically relied on commodity
exports—particularly minerals—for economic growth. However, this dependence has made the economy highly
susceptible to external shocks. Diversifying the economic is critical to achieving inclusive growth and sustainable poverty
reduction. The country continues to struggle with high inflation and sluggish growth, which have intensified household
vulnerabilities. At the same time, structural constraints—including inadequate infrastructure, unreliable energy access,
limited human capital, and governance challenges—pose additional significant barriers. Addressing these bottlenecks is
essential to unlocking Sierra Leone’s development potential and ensuring that economic gains translate into improved
living standards for all.

3.      High-quality data is vital to addressing Cote d’Ivoire and Sierra Leone’s development challenges. Reliable and
timely statistics support evidence-based policymaking and more efficient resource allocation, especially amid fiscal
constraints and competing priorities. Strong national data systems are essential for designing effective policies, monitoring
progress, and evaluating impact. They also promote transparency and accountability, which are foundational to good
governance. Moreover, harmonized and inclusive data can foster regional cooperation and highlight the exclusion

 Sectoral and Institutional Context

 4.       There has been progress in the production of statistics over the last 20 years in Africa. From the early 2000s,
 strategic planning through National Strategies for the Development of Statistics (NSDS) has been adopted, and by 2015
 most African countries implementing one or more. These have helped improve their legal framework—such as new
 statistical laws—and elevated the status of National Statistical Office (NSO). Since 2015, the World Bank has committed
 to improving the statistical capacity of low-income countries given persistent challenges in terms of the statistics to
 monitor the twin goals. The initial focus targeted household surveys, supporting countries to conduct at least one
 household survey every 3-5 years. AS a result, there has been significant improvement in the availability of household
 level data: between 2016 and 2018, 44 countries in Sub-Saharan Africa conducted a household survey.

 5.      Statistics in Africa continue to face institutional weaknesses and inconsistent financing. Many NSOs lack
 autonomy to recruit qualified staff or design and implement effective incentive structures. Internal administrative and
 technical coordination within NSOs is often inadequate or absent. In addition, unreliable and insufficient funding leads
 to infrequent data collection and persistent staffing challenges, resulting to significant data gaps, outdated
 methodological standards, and limited public data access to quality data.

D. Project Description

 7.       The Harmonizing and Improving Statistics in West Africa (HISWA) project was approved by the World Bank
 Board of Executive Directors on March 26, 2020. The project includes nine recipients with a total financing amount
 of US$379 million from the National Performance-Based Allocations (PBA) International Development Association
 (IDA) and Regional IDA Windows. Seven project participating countries, namely, Burkina Faso (IDA Credit 65730 -
 US$30 million and IDA Grant D5810 - US$30 million), Cabo Verde (IDA Credit 65740 - US$15 million), Cote d’Ivoire
 (IDA Credit 65750 - US$54 million), Ghana (IDA Credit 65760 - US$150 million), Liberia (IDA Credit 65770 - US$15
 million and IDA Grant D5830 - US$15 million), Sierra Leone (IDA Grant D5840 - US$30 million) and Togo (IDA Credit
 65780 - US$15 million and IDA Grant D5850 - US$15 million), as well as two regional organizations, namely, the
 African Union (AU) (IDA Grant D5880 - US$2.5 million) and the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS)
 (IDA Grant D5820 - US$7.5 million).

 8.      The parent project aims to address critical data gaps by harmonizing, producing, disseminating, and
 enhancing the use of essential economic and social statistics. It seeks to improve the statistical capacity of
 participating countries, ensuring that their National Statistical Systems (NSS) respond to user needs and generate
 more comparable and reliable statistics, thereby facilitating better decision-making. Furthermore, the project
 supports the modernization of statistical methods, organizational structures, physical infrastructure, and Information
 and Communication Technology (ICT) within National Statistics Offices (NSOs), as well as Ministries, Departments,
 and Agencies (MDAs) responsible for statistics. This modernization will empower these entities to fulfill their
 mandates more effectively.

 9.         The parent project is structured into three key components.

      •     Component 1: Regional Component: Harmonization, data collection, quality improvement, dissemination
            and use of core social and economic statistics. This component funds activities aiming at supporting
            harmonized methodologies, producing core economic and social statistics, improving administrative
            statistics, providing training on methodological advancements, building human capacity, and enhancing data
            quality and timeliness.
      •     Component 2: Country-Specific Component: Improving or modernizing physical and statistical
            infrastructure. The activities in this component aim to address the specific constraints and needs of each
            NSO. They consider the necessary interventions, including physical, technological, and statistical
            infrastructure, human capacity, and legal or regulatory frameworks, required for the NSO to successfully
            implement the program of activities outlined in Component 1.
      •     Component 3: Project Management and Monitoring and Evaluation: This component aims to support
            project management and monitor project outcomes in each participating country and regional organization,
            ensuring the effective and efficient execution of project activities. NSOs are the implementing agencies,
            overseeing the technical management and coordination of the project. Each country's project management
            team is tasked with managing activities, reporting, auditing, environmental and social safeguards, and
            ensuring compliance with fiduciary policies. Specifically, this component encompasses project staff costs,
            project coordination expenses (such as those for the steering committee), operating costs, and other
            necessary expenditures.

12.     During the Mid-Term Review and the most recent implementation support missions, participating
countries raised concerns that cost-overruns could jeopardize the completion of key project-financed activities.
These overruns have resulted in increased expenditures across several components and may require adjustments to
ensure the timely and effective delivery of project outcomes.

13.       The delays and cost overruns encountered under the parent project stemmed from a combination of
internal and external factors. Implementation capacity had to be built in both the implementing entity and the
fiduciary agencies during the initial stage of project implementation. This slowed the execution of planned activities
and contributed to increased operational expenses. Delays in procurement planning, lengthy approval processes, and
limited competition in bidding contributed to cost escalations. The lack of robust contract management and oversight
further exacerbated these issues, leading to procurement-related bottlenecks and the need for costly adjustments
later in implementation.

14.      Externally, several major shocks significantly drove up project costs. The COVID-19 pandemic severely
disrupted global supply chains, delaying procurement and bidding processes, and triggering sharp price increases for
essential goods and services. Items such as vehicles, computers, tablets, interviewer kits, and other data collection
equipment became substantially more expensive than originally budgeted. The pandemic also introduced logistical
challenges for survey implementation. Lockdowns and movement restrictions required major adjustments to
fieldwork operations, leading to increased costs for enumerator training, extended data collection periods, vehicle
rentals, and additional staff compensation. Collectively, these adjustments substantially raised the overall cost of
survey operations.

15.      At the same time, governments across participating countries increasingly demanded more frequent and
timely data to monitor not only the social and economic impacts of COVID-19 on households and businesses. In
response, the project supported the rapid design and implementation of multiple rounds of phone-based surveys to
address critical data gaps. While these surveys were more feasible during lockdowns and ensured continuity in data
collection, they also introduced new cost drivers. These included expenses related to call centers or mobile service
providers, airtime and respondent incentives, enumerator training for phone-based interviews, software
customization, and remote data processing systems. In Côte d’Ivoire and Sierra Leone, many rounds of the COVID-19
Impact Monitoring Survey (CIMS) were conducted, providing critical real-time data to inform government response
efforts.

16.      As a result, cost overruns have affected multiple components of the project, particularly those linked to
data collection, production, and construction. Given the limited resources, the AF has been strategically designed to
prioritize only the most critical statistical activities. These activities are deemed essential to achieving the PDO and

E. Implementation

19. The parent project is currently being implemented across all seven participating countries and two regional
organizations. Although the implementation officially began on July 13, 2020, with the Ghana Financing Agreement
becoming effective on the same date, actual implementation was delayed due to the impacts of the Coronavirus
Disease (COVID-19). To date, the project has disbursed 83 percent of total project funds, with both progress toward
achieving the PDO and overall implementation progress rated as Satisfactory. Country-specific disbursement rates are
as follows: Côte d’Ivoire – 85 percent and Sierra Leone – 99.9 percent. The project has made substantial contributions
to strengthening NSS by addressing key data gaps and supporting the production of critical statistical outputs, including
population, business and agricultural censuses, as well as a range of core household and socio-economic surveys.
Furthermore, it has enhanced the harmonization, dissemination, and use of high-quality data to inform, monitor, and
evaluate public policies—within national planning and program design processes.

20. In Cote d’Ivoire, the project became effective on August 31, 2020, and in Sierra Leone on September 10, 2020.
Since then, both countries have made substantial progress, with both the PDO and overall implementation progress
currently rated as Satisfactory.

parent project. As under the parent project, the national Project Implementation Unit (PIU) will manage the project's
day-to-day activities, including handling management, reporting, and auditing responsibilities. There will be no
changes anticipated to the PIU arrangements.

I. KEY SAFEGUARD POLICY ISSUES AND THEIR MANAGEMENT

 A. Summary of Key Safeguard Issues


1. Describe any safeguard issues and impacts associated with the Restructured project. Identify and describe any
  potential large scale, significant and/or irreversible impacts.
  The project in Sierra Leone will involve civil works for the rehabilitation and expansion of the Sierra Leone Statistics
  office complex. The potential activities include the demolition of walls to pave way for expansion works, installation
  of water and sanitation facilities, replacement of windows and doors, doors and roofing, foundation works for building
  extension. Risks associated with these activities include health and safety of communities and workers, sources of
  borrow material, transportation risks, air water and soil pollution, waste disposal of demolished sections amongst
  others.
  In Cote d’Ivoire, the expected risks and impacts will be identical to those identified for the parent project. Activities
  are related to household based surveys, economic statistics including enterprises and agriculture, health and
  education surveys and use of big data and international standards. The project will also support institutional reforms
  and capacity building. Given the proposed activities, most of the anticipated risks are related to workers and
  communities health and safety associated with staff travel during census and potential of conflicts with communities
  or risks related to sexual exploitation, harassment and abuse (SEA/SH).
