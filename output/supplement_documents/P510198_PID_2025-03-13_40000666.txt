 B. Introduction and Context

 Project Strategic Context

1.     The Lao PDR Pollution and Waste Management Project (PWMP) is a US$34.98 million Investment Project Financing
operation that aims to enhance the country's capacity to manage waste, plastics, and pollution and improve municipal
solid waste (MSW) services in Vientiane Capital (VC). The six-year project also looks to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG)
emissions and generate jobs for vulnerable communities as a result of these interventions. The project supports the
Government of Lao PDR’s (GoL) commitment to a low-carbon, resilient, and cleaner development pathway, the country’s
medium-term Green Growth Strategy to 2030, the 9th National Socioeconomic Development Plan 2021-2025, and its
Nationally Determined Contributions (2021), which include a commitment to reduce GHG emissions and enhance climate
resilience. The focus on VC, the country’s capital and largest waste generator, ensures that the project's outcomes serve
as a model for other cities, thereby amplifying its impact across the country.

2. The PWMP will be the second among the national level projects in the World Bank-financed Southeast Asia Regional
Program on Combating Marine Plastics (SEA-MaP) (P175659) Series of Projects (SOP) to support marine plastic solutions
at the national level. Marine plastic pollution is a transboundary issue, with shared river systems such as the Mekong
River acting as conduits for plastic waste, and shared coastlines receiving plastic waste carried by ocean currents. Plastic
pollution from Laos is transported via the Mekong River to the marine environment. Accordingly, single country solutions
to manage marine litter carry the risk of not being sufficient; there is a need for regional collaboration to combat marine
plastic pollution in critical countries with currently undermanaged plastic waste. The development objective of the SEA-

MaP is to reduce plastic consumption, increase recycling, and minimize leakages to prevent land-and sea based marine
plastic pollution in Southeast Asia. The program follows a SOP approach under two pillars: Pillar 1 includes a US$20 million
regional IDA-financed project that will help strengthen institutional capacity, harmonize standards and guidelines, and
promote innovative financing and technological solutions. Pillar 2 focuses on plastic -related investments and policy
support at national levels. The regional IDA-financed SEA-MAP project under Pillar 1 was approved in June 2022. In
addition to the PWMP, Pillar 2 of the SOP includes the US$67 million Cambodia Solid Waste and Plastic Management
Improvement Project (P170976) which was approved in May 2023.1 Implementation of the SEA-MaP is progressing
steadily and is on track to achieve its objectives of strengthening plastic policies and promoting innovative solutions. An
objective of the Cambodia project is to improve plastic management in selected areas. It is currently procuring key
consultancy packages, including for the development of a regulatory framework to better manage waste and plastics, and
site selection for waste and plastic recovery facilities and landfills. Laos will draw from the IDA Regional Window to support
reduction of plastics, along with ASEAN and Cambodia. The common results indicator for Pillar 2 projects is ‘Number of
plastics policies, guidelines, or standards established and aligned with the Regional Action Plan’. This indicator appears in
PWMP’s results framework.

3. The PWMP is being undertaken within a challenging macroeconomic environment. Economic growth in Lao PDR
averaged about 7 percent over the two decades leading up to 2019, driven by capital-intensive, resource-based, and debt-
financed patterns.2 High public debt levels have contributed to ongoing macroeconomic instability, impacting
development prospects; the World Bank assessed under the Low-Income Countries Debt Sustainability Framework that
Lao PDR is currently experiencing debt distress with an unsustainable outlook.3 The country faces both solvency and
liquidity challenges due to substantial financing needs, limited financing options, low foreign exchange reserves, and
notable depreciation pressures. Consumer price inflation accelerated in 2023, primarily due to the weaker Lao Kip (LAK),
with average annual consumer price inflation reaching 31 percent, compared to 23 percent in 2022. Higher prices for food,
transport, restaurants, and hotels were significant contributors. Despite improved revenue administration and tighter
monetary policy in 2024, pressures on the exchange rate and prices are expected to persist.

    Sectoral and Institutional Context

4.     Air and water pollution have been longstanding concerns for the Lao PDR (Laos), while solid and plastic waste
management are gaining prominence . National average ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) air pollutant levels are
four times higher than World Health Organization guidelines. Exposure to air pollution results in about 10,000 premature
deaths per year or approximately 17 percent of total annual deaths in Laos.4 The annual cost of health-related impacts of
air and water pollution, poor sanitation and hygiene, and lead exposure was estimated to be 14.6 percent of 2017 GDP.5
Waste generation has been increasing in recent years at an alarming pace, particularly in urban centers. Between 2000
and 2015, the country’s major cities saw a 40 to 60 percent increase in waste generation, with MSW generation reaching
approximately 0.75 kg per capita per day and a projected 54 percent increase in annual waste generation across Laos
between 2020 and 2035 if actions are not taken. In VC alone, 1,065 tons of waste are generated per day – making it the
highest rate in the country. Plastic comprises about 24 percent of MSW mainly from food and drink packaging and plastic
bags.6 Toxic smoke generated from open burning of waste, including plastic, contributes to GHG emissions, air pollution

5.    The GoL has established legal instruments for environmental risk management to address air and water pollution.
These include a 2024 updated Environmental Protection Law (EPL) that emphasizes Environmental and Social Impact
Assessment (ESIA) as the main tool for pollution control, and a Prime Minister’s Decree on Environmental Impact
Assessment (EIA, revised in 2022). As a result, and with World Bank support,7 the number of ESIAs approved by the ministry
in charge, namely the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment (MONRE), increased significantly between 2012 and
2020.

6.     With hydropower, mining, agriculture, and industry being the main pollution contributors, the GoL has been
prioritizing its regulation enforcement in these sectors. Agricultural biomass burning and industries such as cement
factories have been identified as some of the main sources of PM10 and PM2.5. Water pollution has been linked to mining
effluent and agricultural runoff.8 9 Investment projects in these sectors are categorized as Category 2 projects that
require an ESIA to be approved. Category 2 project owners are required to prepare and adhere to Environmental
Management and Monitoring Plans. They must submit annual compliance reports to MONRE and undergo periodic
compliance monitoring by MONRE staff. Air quality monitoring (AQM) and water quality monitoring (WQM) are carried
out by the Natural Resources and Environment Research Institute (NRERI), which operates ten AQM monitoring stations
and 21 WQM stations across Laos – both with the support of the World Bank.10

7.    Yet, there are gaps that must be bridged to ensure effective pollution control for these sectors. Regulation
compliance monitoring and enforcement systems lack standard monitoring procedures, staff capacity, and resources, as
well as a fines system for noncompliance. Further, data communicated by the AQM systems are insufficiently robust11 and
the stations are not integrated to allow effective environmental compliance monitoring, including emission monitoring.
The WQM system lacks guidelines and institutional capacities, and data is not consistently reported.

8.    Institutional arrangements for waste management include the MONRE with responsibility for national legal and
regulatory oversight of waste management policies, and the Ministry of Public Works and Transport (MPWT) with
responsibility for constructing waste management infrastructure. Provincial Departments of Public Works and Transport
and urban development authorities have responsibility for waste management at city and provincial levels including
operating and maintaining waste management infrastructure.

9.     VC’s Solid Waste Management Strategy and Action Plan (2021-2030) targets 100 percent solid waste collection,
maximizing material recovery, and financial and operational efficiency. The Vientiane City Office for Management and
Service (VCOMS) is the urban development authority for VC, and oversees waste management services, with ten private
operators contracted to collect and transport waste from households and businesses across the city. About 31 percent of
MSW generators have contracts for waste collection.12 Waste is transported by the private companies to a 50-hectare
landfill in Xaythany district of VC called the KM32 landfill. VCOMS collects waste from some areas in Vientiane and this
waste is transported to the KM16 transfer station in Xaysettha district where it is compacted before it is sent to the KM32
landfill. KM32 landfill receives about 44 percent of MSW generated in VC and is managed by a private operator contracted
by VCOMS. The Vientiane government also has in place a concessional agreement with a private operator adjacent to the
landfill to provide Refuse Derived Fuel production services with the residual waste disposed of at the landfill for a tipping
fee. VCOM’s main revenue streams include fees from waste contracts, tipping fees, and a small annual subsidy that is
provided by the Vientiane government, albeit not consistently. Approximately 264 seasonal and permanent waste pickers
from nearby villages operate at the KM32 landfill, 47 percent of whom are female, generating income from selling
recyclable waste to a waste management community center at the KM32 landfill.13

10. However, the sector and VC specifically face legislative, institutional, and infrastructure gaps that prevent them
from meeting demand. Nation-wide, the sector is faced with: (i) absence of comprehensive legislation and clear
institutional roles at the centralized and decentralized level for agencies such as MONRE and the MPWT, resulting in their
ineffective governance, operational, financial, and technical performance; (ii) lack of a national waste collection data
system, which hampers informed policymaking and planning; and (iii) waste infrastructure development that generally
lacks integrated planning, recurrent budget allocations for operation and maintenance, and comprehensive training for
infrastructure operators. A major gap is the lack of understanding of the total costs of the waste management system. In
VC, VCOMS is underperforming because of insufficient budgets and limited revenue generation, inadequate technical and
management capacities, and lack of performance incentives. MSW collection is poorly planned and coordinated, resulting
in 14-49 percent coverage14 and public grievances. VCOMS struggles to hold the private waste collectors accountable as
waste contracts do not have performance indicators. Private operators struggle to collect fees due to households’
unwillingness to pay and lack of enforcement framework, and the fee is too low (LAK50,000 per month, about US$2.25 -
less than 1 percent of VC’s disposable income levels) to meet cost recovery needs. The waste treatment facilities are
poorly managed: in KM32 landfill, for example, waste compacting and soil covering are not routinely practiced, and there
are no leachate collection and treatment protocols in place. The landfill also lacks critical infrastructure, such as a leachate
collection and transport system and a suitable leachate treatment facility. Consequently, leachate is discharged into the
surrounding area, impacting ground and surface water quality. The landfill also lacks a methane gas capturing system and
a passive gas removal mechanism, further causing environmental hazards. Consequently, waste pickers operate in high-
risk conditions and face health risks. Further, instances of children engaging in waste-picking activities have been
observed, putting them in great peril.

11. The GoL, led by MONRE, has in place a National Plastics Action Plan (NPAP) for 2024-2030 focusing on single-use
plastic reduction, enhanced recyclables collection, and recycling sector improvement . The GoL has also implemented
waste banks in a limited number of schools and government offices in VC for plastic waste collection. Small-scale efforts
have been undertaken by the GoL to reduce the use of plastic bags along with plastic reduction campaigns. Domestic
recycling is carried out mainly by private companies that process plastics into pellets for export, mostly to Thailand and
Vietnam.

12. However, key challenges facing the government in addressing plastic waste include lack of data and information
for plastic waste monitoring, low technical capacity for implementing plastic recycling interventions, and lack of
financial resources for implementing programmatic plastic waste reduction activities. Further, domestic recycling lacks
compliance regulations and monitoring of plastics collection and recycling by the private sector, raising environmental
and health concerns. These issues will become more pressing with impending plastic import bans by Thailand and Viet
Nam.

13. The PWMP aims to resolve these challenges by improving the efficiency, effectiveness, and financial sustainability
of Laos’ pollution, waste, and plastics management systems, scaling up the infrastructure and capacity of the VC
government for MSW service delivery, and improving the working conditions and livelihoods of formal and informal waste
workers (IWW). These will generate important regional outcomes as the amount of pollution flowing into shared water
bodies will decrease significantly. By doing so, it will support the GoL in implementing the EPL and EIA Decree for air and
water pollution control, VC’s Solid Waste Management Strategy and Action Plan, and the NPAP. Importantly, the project
will also support GoL’s collaboration with other ASEAN Member States on combating marine debris and their adoption of
the Bangkok Declaration on Combating Marine Debris in the ASEAN Region (2019).

 Relationship to CPF

14. The PWMP is closely aligned with the World Bank Group Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for FY2023-2026.15
Under the CPF, the project contributes to objective 8 “Improved and climate-adaptive management of forests, biodiversity
and protected areas�? through strengthening Laos’ ESIA system and other environmental protection instruments which are
key for preventing and mitigating natural resource degradation. The project will also contribute to objective 2 on
“Improved labor incomes of vulnerable households�?, as investments in new and upgraded waste management facilities
and skills training to waste pickers will facilitate development of new jobs in the sector.

15. The project is consistent with the Laos’ Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC). In the latest NDC submitted
to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), the country commits to reducing GHG
emissions by 60 percent compared to the baseline scenario by 2030, net-zero emissions by 2050, and reducing GHG
emissions from the waste sector by 40ktCO2e/y between 2020 and 2030, namely through landfill gas (namely methane)
reduction and recovery, and reduction of emissions from open burning of waste. The project directly contributes to the
NDC by improving governance and efficiency of MSW collection services, reducing open burning of waste, increasing waste
treatment including recycling and composting. Landfill rehabilitation and gas capturing will contribute to the NDC’s
commitment on methane gas reduction. On adaptation, the NDC commits to increasing resilience of urban development
and infrastructure to climate change by 2030. The project supports this commitment by reducing the vulnerability of solid
waste facilities to climate risks through climate-resilient design and enhancing urban flood drainage capacity through
improved waste collection. The project is consistent with Laos’ National Adaptation Program of Action (2009), which
promotes pollution and waste management as part of the solution for increasing Laos’ resilience to impacts of climate
change. The project will contribute to the World Bank’s Methane Reduction Program.

 D. Concept Description

18.     The project will finance the following components:

19. Component 1. Advancing Systems for Waste and Pollution Management. The objective of this component is to
strengthen the national government’s capacity for waste and pollution management. Subcomponent 1.1 will focus on
pollution management by financing the development of an operational compliance monitoring system for air and water
pollution control enforcement in the five key sectors of energy, mining, infrastructure, agriculture, and industry; and
upgrading air and water quality monitoring systems. Subcomponent 1.2 will focus on waste management by financing the
development and early-implementation of the waste management legal framework, establishment of an inter-ministerial
waste management committee (WMC), and improving waste data collection and management.

Subcomponent 1.1: Upgrading Pollution Monitoring and Enforcement

20. Development of a compliance monitoring system for air and water pollution control enforcement housed in
MONRE. The project will support the development and deployment of a compliance monitoring system for air and water
pollution control enforcement to help the government better monitor and enforce environmental protection measures,
especially for Category 2 investment projects.16 The system will comprise procedures for monitoring and evaluation (M&E)
and reporting of compliance with environmental regulations, a compliance scoring system to track and report
performance of Category 2 projects’ environmental obligations, and a system for monitoring fines and penalties. The

monitoring, including observation wells around the KM32 landfill; and (v) developing technical guidelines for water

KM32 landfill. These activities will be led by NRERI, DNEI, and the Department of Water Resources (DWR).

Subcomponent 1.2: Strengthen Waste and Plastic Management System

22. Development and implementation of waste management legal framework. The subcomponent will finance TA,
consultancy services, and training to support the development of a waste management decree as an overarching legal
framework that establishes rules and policies for waste management in the country. The decree will focus on municipal
solid waste, including plastics, non-hazardous, and hazardous waste, and define the roles and responsibilities and
coordination of the various stakeholders involved including on waste infrastructure operation and maintenance. The
decree will cover aspects such as principles of waste management planning and financing, appropriate treatment and
disposal of waste, recycling of MSW, control of illegal waste dumping, spot inspections, and GHG reduction from the waste
management sector.17 Training and dissemination activities will be financed to promote the understanding and effective
implementation of the decree through on-the-job training and workshops for staff of relevant line ministries at the
national, provincial and city levels. Preparation of the draft Decree including stakeholders’ consultations will start in the
first year of the project and will take about 1.5 years. The draft Decree will be submitted for government’s approval which
will take about 1 year as it needs to be reviewed and commented on by several ministries and agencies before it is
approved by the Prime Minister’s Office. The training and dissemination activities will be carried out after approval of the
Decree to ensure that relevant stakeholders are equipped with the necessary knowledge and skills to effectively
implement the decree. TA will support the Department of Environment (DOE) in monitoring the implementation of the
decree particularly in Vientiane Capital where PWMP’s waste management investments are focused. The DOE will lead
these activities.

23. Establishment of an Inter-ministerial Waste Management Committee. The subcomponent will finance TA and
operating costs for the establishment of an inter-ministerial WMC as a permanent government body, with operating
budget from the government, that ensures coordination and cooperation among relevant ministries in the waste

regular meetings to report on and monitor waste management issues. The governance structure of the WMC will be
finalized during project implementation, and it is anticipated that the MONRE minister will be the chair. These activities
will be initiated in the first year of the project. The DOE will lead these activities.

24. Development of a basic waste information collection system housed in MONRE. The system will allow data analysis
and informed decision-making. The system will be cost-effective with a user-friendly interface and standardized protocols
for information providers and users, including national and subnational government and waste operators. The system will
receive waste information from cities and districts, with a pilot phase planned in collaboration with the VC government.
The subcomponent will finance TA, consultancy services, and training, including for: (i) producing waste and plastics
statistics to ensure regular collection of information on generation, collection, recycling, and disposal by local authorities;
(ii) developing the system’s specifications and needed governance and operational arrangements for its implementation;
and (iii) training Provincial Offices of Natural Resources and Environment, District-level Departments of Natural Resources
and Environment, and urban development authorities in the use of the system and providing capacity building and
equipment to VCOMS for waste and plastics data collection. This activity will commence in the second year and DOE will
lead it.

25. Subcomponent 1.3: Component 1 Coordination and Reporting. This subcomponent will finance fiduciary
management, environmental and social risk management for Component 1. The Environment Protection Fund (EPF),
which is a government fund created to mobilize funds for environmental and natural resources management, and one of
the implementing agencies will lead this subcomponent.

26. Component 2: Improving Municipal Solid Waste Services in Vientiane Capital. The objective of this component is
to improve MSW services in VC by financing activities that enhance the institutional, operational, financial, and technical
capacity of MPWT and VCOMS. Subcomponent 2.1 will finance capacity enhancement activities of VC’s solid waste services
using an Integrated Solid Waste Management (ISWM) approach,19 while Subcomponent 2.2 will finance MSW service
infrastructure investments. The regional benefits of this component are reduced plastics pollution emerging from VC due
to improved collection, recovery, and disposal of MSW. Investments in integrated waste management facilities and landfill
rehabilitation will further reduce plastics pollution as well as GHG emissions by ensuring safe disposal without burning of
plastics and leakage into the environment and waterways.

the revised waste contracting procedures and fees. The subcomponent will also finance training to VCOMS and relevant
stakeholders in the application of the new fee model and monitoring system and the adjusted contract structures. These
activities will be led by MPWT and VCOMS.

29. Improving waste pickers’ livelihoods and safety. With a focus on waste pickers working in the KM32 landfill and
children engaging in dangerous waste picking activities, the subcomponent will finance goods, small civil works and
training for: (i) providing vocational training to waste pickers; and (ii) providing personal protective equipment, OHS
trainings, and a multi-function room for eating and resting. The project will provide technical assistance to support
measures for ensuring child waste pickers between the ages of 14 and 18 do not engage in hazardous work in the landfill,
and to prevent children under the age of 14 from working in the Km32 landfill. Children over 14 years old can engage in
work that is non-hazardous, does not interfere with the child’s education, is not harmful to the child’s health, moral or
spiritual development' (see more information in Annex 2). These activities will be led by VCOMS.

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts



40. It is anticipated that the project will bring net positive benefits to the environment and people. However, the project
is not without environmental and social risks, with nine of the ten Environmental and Social Standards (ESSs) considered
relevant. Five ESF instruments have been prepared for the project and were disclosed on the Recipient’s websites on
December 17, 2024: Preliminary Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (Pre-ESIA); Environmental and Social
Management Framework (ESMF); Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP); Social Impact Assessment-Social Management Plan
(SIA-SMP); and Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP).

41. The environmental risk of the project is High due to activities such as the rehabilitation of the KM32 landfill,
upgrading of the KM16 transfer station, and construction of a new transfer station in Naxaythong District. The key risks
involve groundwater pollution from leachate, surface water contamination, air pollution from open burning and landfill
fires, and health and safety hazards from waste traffic. These risks are long-term, high in magnitude, and exacerbated by
environmental (for example, flooding, extreme heat) and institutional (for example, limited capacity) challenges.
Additional risks include cumulative impacts on water resources and agriculture from legacy pollution at KM32 and adjacent
private facilities. Construction-related impacts, such as air and noise pollution and health risks, are generally moderate,
temporary, and localized. Improper siting of the Naxaythong facility could affect biodiversity and cultural heritage, though
exclusion criteria have been developed to avoid such impacts. The KM32 landfill currently operates as an open dump
without lining and limited capping, experiencing uncontrolled leachate discharge, waste burning and occasional landfill
fires, and GHG (methane) emission. Infectious medical waste from health care facilities is also found at the dumpsite. In
addition, there are legacy waste dumps and four wastewater ponds in the landfill. A Pre-ESIA conducted during project
preparation identified historic and ongoing infiltration of polluted leachate from the legacy waste on both VCOMS site
and the adjacent private concession which, if not mitigated, could potentially continue to pose significant long-term risks
to the surface water and groundwater resources, which are used for irrigation and/or domestic consumption and pose a
risk to the health of nearby residents, workers, and waste pickers. Environmental risks and impacts from TA and activities
under Component 1 may indirectly increase waste volumes due to stricter enforcement.

42. Prior to appraisal, the government prepared, organized public consultations on, and disclosed an ESMF and Pre-
ESIA. The ESMF assesses overall environmental and social risks of the Project and guides site-specific measures and E&S
risk management for TA activities. The Pre-ESIA for KM32 identifies preferred design options to mitigate risks such as
leachate and air pollution. This will be further developed into a full ESIA, Health Impact Assessment, and corresponding
Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMP)/s for the waste management infrastructure/works to be conducted
in Vientiane Capital during implementation. The ESMP/s will be included in bidding document and contracts which will
require Contractors to prepare and implement Contractor ESMP/s, and the project will develop a POM. In addition, the
project will support establishment of an Environmental and Social Monitoring Coordination Committee (ESMCC)
mandated to oversee environmental and social aspects around the KM32 landfill and adjacent Vientiane Waste
Management Company concession area and at the KM16 and Naxaythong transfer stations. Terms of reference for the
ESMCC are included in the POM. Consultations with waste pickers and local communities have informed the project
design, and further consultations will be held for the full ESIA post-effectiveness.

43. The social risk is High but accompanied by positive outcomes, such as improved waste collection and working
conditions for waste pickers. Key social risks include potential land acquisition and resettlement for construction of the
new transfer station at Naxaythong where local communities includeethnic minorities as well as possible community
concerns about new/expanded waste facilities. At the waste management facilities receiving project investments, there
are occupational health and safety risks for formal and informal waste workers as well as sexual exploitation and abuse
(SEA), sexual harassment (SH) and child protection risks. The project design safeguards waste pickers’ livelihoods by
improving working conditions through personal protective equipment, training, and safety measures, while providing
opportunities for formal employment and skill training. Instruments such as SIA-SMP, SEP, and ESCP address these risks.

44. The Implementing Agencies have experience with the World Bank safeguard policies but limited familiarity with
the ESF, and capacity for managing social aspects related to SWM is low. The client will be challenged in: (i) coordinating
among different institutional levels (central, province, and district level) within the same ministries and across ministries;
and (ii) managing and mitigating livelihood impacts to informal waste pickers, many of whom are socially marginalized
and vulnerable. Specific institutional capacity strengthening/ building measures such as the provision of additional
resources and training needs have been identified and presented in the ESF instruments, including the ESCP to ensure
ownership and sustainability of the resources.

45. A Grievance Redress Mechanism (GRM) will be established to address complaints coming from project-affected
peoples and groups. GRM Committees will be established at village, district, and central levels as well as within the PCU
of EPF, and PMU of MPWT. For workers hired by contractors, the contractors will be required to produce their GRM
procedure as a prerequisite for tender. The GRM will ensure that there are multiple channels for reporting grievances
which are readily accessible, and a consolidated mechanism for monitoring the status of grievances and ensuring
responses within a set timeframe. SEA/SH sensitive protocol and training will be instituted for sensitive complaints. The
GRM will be disclosed publicly to ensure awareness by project-affected people and other stakeholders.
