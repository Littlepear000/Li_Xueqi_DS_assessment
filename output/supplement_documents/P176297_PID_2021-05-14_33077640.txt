     B. Introduction and Context

     Country Context

     1.       Despite a solid macroeconomic policy that has favored growth over the past two decades, Peru’s economy
     remains highly dependent on a limited number of extractive activities. Driven by structural reforms and prudent
     macroeconomic policies,1 real GDP growth rate averaged over 4.8 percent between 2000 and 2019, above the average
     for Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC).2 However, even before the COVID-19 pandemic, GDP growth rates had slowed
     to a little over 3 percent per year since 2014, in part due to lower international commodity prices and contracting external
     demand. In the wake of the economic crisis brought on by the COVID-19 pandemic, GDP is expected to contract by 12
     percent in 2020, which is one of the largest predicted drops in the world.3 Unemployment has increased from 3.6 percent
     in the second quarter of 2019, to 8.8 percent in the same period of 2020. Given the continued volatility in global
     commodity prices, Peru’s increasing dependence on extractive industries, which grew by 50 percent as share of GDP over
     the past 30 years, presents a substantial risk to sustainable economic recovery and growth and underscores the
     importance of economic diversification.
     2.      Economic diversification in Peru has been increasing over the past decade, but at a slow pace, with agriculture
     products and tourism services increasingly contributing to Peru’s expor ts basket. The mining sector continues to
     dominate the economy representing 60 percent of total country exports, 60 percent of foreign exchange, and 12 percent
     of GDP in 2019.4 However, agricultural exports have expanded from 0.4 percent of GDP in 2001 to an estimated 2.7
     percent of GDP in 2019 (equivalent to US$6 billion). Seasonal exports to the northern hemisphere of high-value fresh
     fruits and vegetables (mostly grapes, avocados, blueberries, and asparagus) totaled US$2.5 billion or 1.2 percent of GDP.
     Travel and tourism exports have also increased fivefold from $837 million in 2000 to over $4.5 billion in 2018. A much
     smaller but rapidly growing sector is pharmaceuticals, medicinal chemical substances, and botanical products where in

     investments in technology, innovation, and upgrades in skills.
     3.       Increasing productivity gains and competitiveness in tradable sectors remain critical for export diversification
     and can directly contribute to the creation of better jobs in the Peruvian economy. Mining and manufacturing are the
     most productive sectors of the economy, but represent only 1 and 9 percent of total employment, respectively.5 In
     contrast, agriculture and commerce, despite accounting for the highest shares of employment, are the sectors with the
     lowest productivity. Significant differences in agricultural productivity growth continue to exist between regions and
     between categories of producers, and overall agricultural productivity in Peru remains low compared to other countries,
     in spite of the very significant surges in the past decade in the export of high-value and innovation intensive products. In
     the larger economy, productivity also varies substantially across firm size with the productivity MSME firms estimated to
     be 6, 16, and 50 percent that of large firms in Peru (ECLAC, 2013), differentials that are greater than those observed in
     many other countries in LAC and in several OECD countries.
     4.     Limited productivity gains are explained in part by limited technology adoption and innovation development in
     tradable sectors. Peruvian firms invest on average 2.5 percent of their sales in innovation, whereas their peers in Chile
     invest 3.5 percent. Moreover, technology adoption (acquired through licenses of new technology and imported capital
     goods), which is a way to move closer to the innovation frontier, is scarce. Only 7 percent of Peruvian firms have licensed
     technology from abroad, half the LAC region average and 2.5 times less than in OECD countries. Data show that the
     average age of firms that innovate is five years older and the average number of employees is 75 percent higher than
     those of firms that do not innovate, and that young and small firms are particularly constrained by product market
     competition and innovation issues and uneven access to innovation inputs 6.
     5.      High-skilled work force constraints are also impacting competitiveness and productivity. Peru’s Human Capital
     Index increased faster than most of its neighboring countries7 during the last decade, but this increase in the Index has
     been mainly due to improvements in basic education and reduced chronic malnutrition or stunting. However, other
     aspects of human capital, such as skills development, are still low, leading to a relatively high deficit in skilled workforce
     in Peru compared to other countries.8 Women, in particular, despite being more likely to attend university than men, are
     much less likely to study STEM fields9 (science, technology, engineering, and math) or pursue a doctorate program. There
     are similar disparities in the labor market – women who study STEM fields are less likely to enter STEM careers and tend
     to exit them sooner, thus limiting women’s participation in the most trade-intensive sectors.10
     6.      Finally, limited technology adoption and innovation are also constraining the country’s ability to address its
     sizable social and environmental challenges, such as adequate access to health services, regional disparities, and
     climate change. Peru has been extremely hard-hit by the COVID-19 pandemic – with 150 deaths per 100,000 population,
     it is currently among the countries with the highest death toll from the pandemic – underscoring low levels of
     epidemiological surveillance and public health preparedness.11 Peru also suffers from a substantial imbalance in the

  Sectoral and Institutional Context

  7.      Research and innovation are powerful drivers of competitiveness, productivity, and economic diversification;
  but also, a critical contributor to solutions for key developmental challenges such as climate change, inclusive growth,
  and health. Globally, innovative companies have been shown to grow 15 percent faster in sales and 8 percent faster in
  labor productivity than firms that do not innovate.13 Firm R&D expenditures significantly contribute to sales (by 14
  percent) and labor productivity growth (by 7 percent). Innovation is also increasingly perceived as integral for tackling
  environmental challenges and enhancing green growth, through the introduction of new technologies in climate change
  mitigation, and through non-technological innovations, such as environmental management practices in manufacturing.
  Scientific and technological innovation can also help address social challenges, such as health and demographic
  challenges, social exclusion, poverty, and inequality. A country’s capability to produce, use, and commercialize scientific
  research is critical for developing innovative solutions to key environmental and socio-economic challenges and for
  acquiring and adapting innovation from abroad.
  8.      In the case of Peru, both innovation pull and innovation push factors are inefficient and limit the ability of the
  National Innovation System (NIS) to contribute to development challenges. The demand (by private sector firms and
  society at large) and the supply of knowledge and R&D are both limited and misaligned. As far as Innovation push
  dynamics are concerned14 i) Peru does produce some quality R&D outputs in specific areas of excellence,15 but it does
  not produce enough R&D to create a critical mass with enough positive externalities for the economy, and ii) it does not
  sufficiently transfer the quality R&D it does produce into innovation in the economy.
  9.     The quality of research in Peru is relatively high in specific areas of excellence, but the volume of research
  output is low. Peru ranks 6th in the quality of science among 48 Latin American countries and 58th globally (out of 238
  countries) based on the h-index that measures the productivity and citation impact of scientific publications16. However,
  Peru ranks only 74th globally in number of citable documents and 70th in number of citations, pointing at the low volume
  of quality output in R&D. Medical research makes up the biggest share (almost 40 percent) of research output and has
  the highest quality (52nd globally in h-index in medicine and 6th in Latin America), making it an area with a clear and strong
  potential for knowledge and technology transfer. Engineering and Agriculture are also important areas of quality research
  in Peru’s universities with potential for impact on the development of mining, fisheries, and food production.
  10.    Quite remarkably, even in the areas where Peru sustains investment and demonstrates high quality in academic
  R&D, outputs do not translate into an increase in innovation, revealing inefficient industry-academia linkages. Analysis
  of WIPO data from 2017-2019 indicates that that the number of industrial design application and registration design
  count, as well as the number of patent applications, has stayed relatively constant over the past decade in Peru. This gap

  but evidence points to i) low quality of higher education, especially, but not only, at the higher end of the training chain
  (doctoral programs) and outside Lima, and a corresponding insufficient number of qualified researchers; ii) inadequate
  incentives and capacities for university-industry collaboration and knowledge/technology transfer; iii) substantial deficits
  and poor allocation of public and private investments in R&D and innovation18; and iv) fragmented governance and
  ineffective science, technology and innovation policies.

  13.     The low number of researchers and the low volume and impact of R&D outputs is partly driven by the deficit in
  the number of world class quality PhD programs in Peru, and the very low ratio of university faculty and research staff
  with PhDs. Even though there are more than 400 doctoral programs in Peru and the number of graduates greatly exceed
  those of neighboring countries (803 in Colombia, 807 in Chile and 2047 in Peru in 201823), a recent study of the University
  Superintendency (SUNEDU)24 reflects that only 41 percent of the PhD programs in Peru are offered by high-performing
  research universities and only 20 percent are in STEM areas and 13 percent in health. Furthermore, of the approximately
  12,300 doctoral students registered in 2020, 9,800 were enrolled in the areas of education, law, administration, and
  social sciences. Assuring and enhancing the quality of PhD programs in the country has a programmatic dimension
  through curriculum and research content relevant for innovation-seeking private sector firms and a recruitment and
  retention dimension through the creation of a pipeline of young researchers and positions for them to fill. This is further
  exacerbated by regulatory restrictions on the incorporation of new staff in public research and academic institutions, and
  lack of economic and institutional incentives to attract and retain talent. In addition, the relatively high average age of
  researchers threatens the viability of the ecosystem and creates an urgent need to prepare or attract young qualified
  PhD holders to fill faculty and research positions.
  14.     Intellectual property and technology transfer policies and capacities are also suboptimal and very uneven
  among different institutions, hindering the identification and support to knowledge with commercial potential, and
  creating misaligned incentives for researchers. A survey of 78 universities25 revealed that only eight universities have
  more than five dedicated people in their technology transfer offices. Technology transfer policies in the 16 Public
  Research Institutes (PRI) in Peru are also highly variable with seven PRIs having no technology transfer policies at all, four
  having policies that are only partially implemented, and only five institutes with fully implemented technology transfer
  policies. Other key barriers to technology transfer include insufficient human capital specialized in technology transfer,
  restrictive legal and regulatory frameworks for commercialization and technology transfer in different sectors,
  insufficient or weak technology transfer offices, lack of financial and non-financial incentives, lack of knowledge of
  relevance of research in innovation, etc.26 Finally, a 2016 report27 found that only a limited number of researchers and
  engineers dedicate a substantial part of their work to R&D and innovation activities, partly driven by strict employment
  regulations at universities and research centers.
  15.    Low R&D outputs are also explained by low public investment in basic and applied research in Peru28. In 2019,

  constraints are already affecting public investments in STI, which had increased from $171 million in 2011 to $229 million
  in 2019 only to fall back to $195 million in 2020. To preserve past gains and unleash further innovative potential, Peru
  needs to substantially increase long-term investment in strategic areas of research and, in parallel, increase the impact

  16.     There are also systemic barriers on the part of the private sector that limit private sector investment in R&D
  and constrain industry collaboration with academia. Like other Latin American countries, over two thirds of investment
  in R&D in Peru weas made by the public sector (Figure 2). Almost all R&D investment in Peru is in basic and applied
  science, and only 7.3 percent is in technological development30, which indicates market failures constraining private
  sector investment, such as low appropriability or concentrated market power in some sectors that limits innovation-
  driven competition. Most importantly, weak public-private dialogue leads to low relevance of much of the produced
  research to market demand, as well as a lack of alignment with the country’s priority developmental challenges31. Low
  relevance of research in turn leads to low interest by industries to collaborate with academia, resulting in a self-
  perpetuating cycle. A stakeholder network mapping of the innovative entrepreneurship ecosystem in Lima32 found that
  most of the relationships between actors reflect sporadic contacts (39.9 percent) with low level of joint work (16.7
  percent). The industry innovation survey found that 83 percent of surveyed firms do not carry out activities involving a
  link with research centers.33

  18.      Finally, institutional implementation capacity is low, starting with the governing body of the Science,
  Technology, and Innovation (STI) system, CONCYTEC. Three main constraints continue to prevent it from effectively
  fulfilling its role: i) despite being hosted in the Prime Minister’s Office, CONCYTEC does not yet benefit from sufficient
  recognition of its transversal mandate; ii) insufficiently developed and uncoordinated digital management and accounting
  systems do not allow CONCYTEC to have an accurate and timely overview of public expenditures on R&D and Innovation
  across the various agencies and public institutions of the overall NIS; and iii) the financial and institutional arrangements
  currently prevent CONCYTEC from directing its own funds to partner research and innovation support institutions and
  programs in line ministries. This has resulted in a fragmented institutional context with instruments and policies that
  require updating and strengthening to effectively support STI growth37. Recent analysis38 of NIS instruments in Peru found
  that they cover 11 sectors or government entities, with several agencies and entities within each sector, and a large
  concentration of expenditures on a small number of instruments. The analysis found a lot of overlap between the sectors,
  with overlapping beneficiaries and interventions mechanisms, while private sector-focused instruments showed little
  specialization in business types and needs and little emphasis on improving productivity and diversification. A Public
  Expenditure Review39 was launched in 2018 as part of the ongoing World Bank Innovation project. The first phase of the
  analysis of the policy mix found that strategic priorities identified in NIS plans and programs were not matched with
  commensurate levels of funding, and that actual investments in support of technology and knowledge transfer were
  lower in areas that were declared priorities.

  Relationship to CPF

  20.     The proposed project directly contributes to the World Bank Group Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for
  Peru for the period FY17 to FY21.41 Specifically, the project is closely linked to multiple Objectives under Pillar I:
  “Productivity for Growth.�? All components of the project contribute to Objective 2: “Facilitate absorption of skills and
  technology.�? Component 1 includes activities that reinforce the MSTQ infrastructure in Peru, therefore increasing the
  ability of innovation seeking firms and SMEs to adopt and integrate technologies in their production processes;
  Component 2 promotes a stronger prioritization of investments in doctoral programs and skills in alignment with the
  needs of the private sector; and Component 3 includes activities that improve industry-academia linkages and alignments
  between investment in R&D and the specific needs of the private sector in terms of technology and innovation, thus
  increasing the absorption rate by firms of technological advances discovered via these R&D investments. In addition, the
  project contributes to Objective 3: “Enhance the environment for sustainable private investments�? through activities
  under Components 1 and 3 that seek to improve the governance of the innovation system and improve innovation
  resources and financing thus enhancing productivity and investment. Finally, the Project aims to reduce gender
  disparities, which is a cross-cutting objective of the CPF, by providing specific incentives to female researchers
  encouraging and increasing their participation in the NIS.

  D. Concept Description

  24.     The proposed Project will strengthen Peru’s NIS through an integrated approach across three components. The
  components are linked and interdependent and address the most critical gaps in the innovation ecosystem.42 The
  activities are not all encompassing and seek to leverage and enhance the outputs of complementary infrastructure,
  services, and financing instruments of the innovation ecosystem.
  25.     The theory of change of the proposed project stipulates that: (i) strengthening CONCYTEC’s capacity as a
  governing body for the NIS, and more broadly strengthening the coordination and management capacity in the larger
  institutional framework , will greatly enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of public expenditure on innovation and
  evidence-based and regionally informed policymaking; (ii) increasing the quantity of qualified researchers and PhD
  graduates will contribute to an increase in the quantity and quality of research outputs and collaborative R&D projects43,

          and services to enhance CONCYTEC’s ability to perform its functions as Peru’s NIS governing body. This includes:
          i) end-to-end digitalization of its services46, modernization of information and knowledge management systems,

          and monitoring and evaluation capacities; iii) incentivizing the participation of women in high-level managerial
          positions in CONCYTEC and other agencies of the NIS; iv) enhancing its capacity for evidence-based decision-
          making through analytical studies and position papers on key innovation policy topics; and v) improving its access
          to relevant data through data collection surveys.
      (b) Subcomponent 1.2: Decentralization of NIS and equal access to scientific information. This subcomponent aims
          to address stark regional disparities in Peru and ensure that all regions benefit from the insights and advances in
          NIS and contribute to them. This will be achieved by decentralizing research, supporting the creation of
          macroregional NIS observatories and regional consortia in NIS, the creation of CONCYTEC regional offices47, and
          giving free access to scientific information and journals to all researchers nationally.
      (c) Subcomponent 1.3: MSTQ48 and R&D Capacity-building Grants. Grants in this subcomponent will finance i)

          and potentially to labs and institutions proposing to develop climate adaptation or mitigation related
          certifications. Investments will be prioritized based on a project-financed National Research Infrastructure
          Roadmap, which will also help aligning research infrastructure with national R&D priorities; and ii) grants to
          support the creation of regional or thematic research networks among universities, research institutions, and the
          private sector, to promote research and innovation in areas that have a critical mass of demand and/or research
          quality outputs. The grants will finance operational costs as well as training and other technical assistance
          activities to strengthen the collaboration or joint output by consortia.

  Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
  .

 Based on the available information, the E&S risk classification is Moderate. Given the nature and scale of activities, low to
 moderate environmental, health and safety risks and potential impacts are expected, mainly associated with applied
 research, technological development, and laboratory equipment and certification activities. These risks and impacts are
 expected to be: (i) temporary and/or reversible; (ii) mainly low (to moderate) in magnitude and not expected to cause
 serious adverse effects to human health and the environment, even though project activities could be located within
 natural and critical natural habitats; (iii) site-specific; and (iv) easily mitigated in a predictable manner. The social risk is
 estimated as Moderate based on: i) the client’s track record of managing project related grievances and complaints under
 P156250; ii) client’s capacity to mitigate social risks in accordance with WB-ESF given the previous project was subject to
 WB safeguard policies; and iii) the unknown specific locations of the project interventions, which could involve indigenous
 peoples and other underserved and vulnerable populations. Potential adverse E&s risks and impacts will be further
 analyzed during project preparation, as more information is available.

 A well-structured PIU with qualified staff to manage the E&S aspects of the project in line with the ESF will be set up. The

 with substantial and high E&S risk, and activities which would require Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) with
 indigenous peoples; ii) an Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP); iii) a Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP);
