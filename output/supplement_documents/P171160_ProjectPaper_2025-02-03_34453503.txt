 I.       BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING

1.      This Project Paper seeks approval of the Board of Executive Directors for a proposed additional grant from the
International Development Association (IDA) in the amount of SDR26.7 million (US$35 million equivalent) for the Somali
Integrated Statistics and Economic Planning Capacity Building Project (P181697). The original grant of US$25 million (SDR
18.3 million) was approved on June 30, 2020, and became effective on September 29, 2020. The proposed Additional
Financing (AF) will finance a 36-month extension of the project closing date of August 31, 2025, to August 31, 2028, for
the completion of the following activities: (i) scaling-up of Component 1 to support data collection to strengthen
fundamental data needs and fill sex-disaggregated data gaps on climate change, human capital, and private sector
development; (ii) scaling-up support under Component 2 to expand the investment in Information and Communications
Technology (ICT) Development and Equipment to support statistical activities in Somaliland and Federal Member States
(FMS); and (iii) scaling-up support under Component 3 to support the national development plan and strategies, and build
the capacity of the public sector to use data to address policy challenges, including on climate, human capital, and private
sector development. The parent project will also be restructured to extend the closing date.
2.       The parent project’s Project Development Objective (PDO) is to strengthen the national statistical system in the
collection, processing, and dissemination of poverty and selected macro-economic data to inform development policy and
poverty reduction activities. The PDO will be changed to reflect the activities to be carried out with the additional financing.
The four components are currently as follows:
      •    Component 1: Strengthening coordination, statistical infrastructure, and data development of the National
           Statistical System (US$11.58 million) strengthens the capacity of the National Statistical System (NSS) in key
           official statistics-producing agencies to collect data for monitoring poverty and macroeconomic indicators,
           including GDP, sector growth, and inflation. Priority data gaps include poverty statistics, climate change and
           adaptation, updating the Consumer Price Index (CPI), development of administrative data and use for official
           statistics, and national accounts. Additionally, the project supports the Somali Integrated Business Establishment
           Census (SIBEC) and the Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS), both of which provide essential data
           for designing the national CPI basket and determining commodity weights.
      •    Component 2: ICT infrastructure development and equipment of the National Statistical System (US$1.22
           million) supports the ICT infrastructure in the Somali National Bureau of Statistics (SNBS) and statistics units in
           Federal Ministries, Departments and Public Agencies (MDAs) and FMS responsible for producing official
           socioeconomic data.
      •    Component 3: Data utilization for monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and planning (US$8.7 million). This
           component aims to build capacity for using data in planning and economic policy analysis on key challenges such
           as climate change and human capital through support to the Monitoring and Evaluation Directorate (MED) and
           the National Economic Council (NEC), and enhance capacity in fiscal and macroeconomic analysis, economic
           forecasts, research, and M&E.
      •    Component 4: Project Management (US$3.5 million) supports the Recipient to strengthen its capacity for project
           planning and budgeting, management, coordination, and M&E, through providing technical advisory services,
           training, operating costs, and the acquisition of goods.
3.       Implementation status. Progress towards achieving the PDO and implementation progress are rated Satisfactory.
At the end of the calendar year 2024, the cumulative disbursement is US$22.15 million, about 91% of the total financing
of US$25 million. The parent project is on track to complete all planned activities, including the annual budget outlook
document, surveys including the SIHBS and the SIBES, reports, updated indicators from SIHBS, the Compendium of
Statistical Concepts, Definitions, and Standards, and the National Integrated M&E Framework. The project has achieved

strong outcomes, with the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators (SPI) rising from 19.6 in 2019 to 49.7 in 2023.
The number of United Nations Statistics Division (UNSD) core gender indicators reported by Somalia has nearly doubled,

4.      Draft Statistics Regulations have been prepared and are aligned with the Statistics Law 2020. A draft Code of
Practice for Official Statistics has also been produced. Both documents are currently being discussed with key stakeholders
and producers of official statistics. The current project activities have successfully improved collaboration and data sharing
among SNBS, MDAs, and statistics units in MDAs and FMSs. The first User Satisfaction Survey was undertaken, published
and disseminated in December 2021. A similar survey will be conducted in 2025, the last year of the current project, to
compare results. There has also been improved coordination and collaboration between official data producers. This was
achieved through SNBS holding advocacy and sensitization workshops with producers and users of official statistics at the
federal and state levels. Three statistics working groups have been established comprising members of NSS. SNBS has also
signed Memorandums of Understanding with key federal and state-level MDAs to enhance data sharing and collaboration.
Rationale for Additional Financing
5.       Somalia has been rebuilding state institutions and the economy since the end of the devastating civil war,
helped by strong support from the international community. However, levels of violence remain high, with conflict-
related incidents and fatalities rising in 2024. Climatic shocks, compounded by global food supply and price pressures,
have displaced millions in recent years and heightened food insecurity, obstructing poverty reduction efforts. In addition,
state fragility has limited the capacity of nascent institutions, hindering the delivery of basic services and further fracturing
public trust in government. Large and sustained investments in human and physical capital are essential if Somalia is to
achieve resilient and inclusive growth. According to the 2024 Somalia Poverty and Equity Report, over 54% of the
population lived below the national poverty line of US$2.06 per day in 2022. Despite these challenges, Somalia reached a
historic Heavily Indebted Poor Countries Initiative (HIPC) Completion Point on December 13, 2023, and joined the East
African Community (EAC) on November 24, 2023, becoming a full member on March 4, 2024.
6.       Building a reliable statistical system is integral to Somalia’s transition from fragility to shared prosperity. A
statistical system supports evidence-based policy making, greater accountability, and improved business opportunities.
Given its Fragility, Conflict, and Violence (FCV) status and limited fiscal space, Somalia needs continuous financial and
technical support in developing its statistical system. The AF will provide resources for undertaking key surveys,
strengthening data infrastructure, improving collaboration and coordination among producers and users of official
statistics, and promoting the use of data.
7.       Despite the ongoing success of the parent project, Somalia still faces data gaps. The data and statistics produced
by the original project have been useful for HIPC progress and monitoring of the National Development Plan 9 (NDP9). For
example, the Federal Government of Somalia (FGS) completed the HIPC Completion Point Triggers on Statistical Capacity
early in the process. Data from SIHBS 2022, along with the poverty analysis, were used by the FGS to monitor the NDP9
and by the World Bank and International Monetary Fund (IMF) to prepare HIPC completion documents. However, the
country can only report on 90 of 216 applicable Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) indicators.1 On November 6, 2023,
the FGS requested the AF to address key data gaps, such as data on the agricultural sector, and to strengthen and expand
data systems.
8.       Somalia faces three new data challenges: climate, private sector, and human capital. The World Bank Group
Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Somalia FY2024-2028 (Report #: 187254-SO), discussed by the Board on
February 29, 2024, highlights three high-level objectives (HLOs) for growth and poverty reduction in Somalia: inclusive,
private sector-led job creation and economic growth; enhanced human capital; and greater resilience to climatic and other

shocks. Yet all three HLOs still lack crucial data to support decision-making and implementation by the government, private
sector, civil society, and the World Bank.
    •     HL01: Private sector. Building on Somalia’s Country Private Sector Diagnostics, 2 there is a major need to
          strengthen data collection on the private sector in Somalia to inform policymaking. While the SIBEC provided
          valuable information on establishments, household enterprises, and the labor force, important gaps remain,
          notably in firm-level statistics.
    •     HL02: Human capital. Somalia faces significant human capital challenges, with limited and unequal access to
          education and healthcare. Primary school enrollment is 39 percent, falling to 29 percent at the secondary level—
          well below the averages for low-income African countries.3 Low immunization rates and poor healthcare further
          impede child survival and development. While the Somali Health and Demographic Survey (SHDS) and SIHBS have
          provided valuable insights, data gaps remain, including key indicators on child mortality and the quality of
          education needed for the Human Capital Index.4
    •     HL03: Resilience to climate and other shocks. The Poverty Assessment shows that climate change severely affects
          livestock-owning and farming households, especially among the poor. 5 However, Somalia has not carried out a
          national agricultural census, and there is no reliable data on livestock or yields, making it difficult to assess climate
          impacts or design adaptation strategies.
10.     FGS urgently needs evidence-based national strategies for Somalia in the post-HIPC era. Somalia still needs to
deepen awareness about the pivotal role of statistics across its leadership to encourage the collection and use of statistics.
MDAs working on the three HLOs still lack the capacity to analyze relevant data to formulate policies. The current National
Development Plan (NDP-9) ended in December 2024. The Ministry of Planning, Investment, and Economic Development
(MoPIED) is drafting Somalia's five-year National Transformation Plan (NTP) for 2025-2029. Once the NTP is approved,
progress must be monitored to ensure the country is on the right track to meet the targets set out under the NTP. The
NEC is also drafting Somalia’s long-term plan, Centennial Vision 2060, which will also draw and depend upon increased
data availability.
11.     Somalia’s new human capital development strategy (HCDS) will feed into the NTP. Human capital development
in Somalia relies heavily on donor support, highlighting the need for a sustainable internal strategy. Climate change
exacerbates the challenge by stoking food insecurity, damaging infrastructure, and disrupting education. The Prime
Minister's recent focus on human capital as a national priority signals a critical shift. The HCDS will aim to institutionalize
service delivery, boost public spending, and prioritize human capital. It will move away from fragmented, donor-
dependent efforts towards integrated, nationally governed frameworks, emphasizing the development of local capacities
in education, healthcare, skills, and social protection.
12.    The AF is aligned with Somalia’s participation in international and regional agreements on statistics production.
These commitments include the SDGs, the African Union’s Agenda 2063, the Intergovernmental Authority on
Development (IGAD) Vision 2050, the EAC’s 3rd Regional Strategy for the Development of Statistics through its second
National Strategy for the Development of Statistics (NSDS II), and the Strategy for Harmonization of Statistics in Africa
2017-2026 (SHaSA2).6

15.     The PDO will be revised to reflect the scope and purpose of activities funded by the AF. It will be changed to

      D. Results Indicators and Beneficiaries
24.      While direct beneficiaries remain the producers and users of official statistics, the extended scope of the AF will
directly benefit FMS government institutions and a broader set of MDAs. FMS statistics producers will see direct support
under component 1 and 2 in order to improve the quality of CPI statistics as well as their ICT infrastructure. Meanwhile,
new MDAs beyond the MoF, CBS, and the MoPIED will benefit from capacity building on the use of statistical data to
inform policymaking.
25.      Indirect beneficiaries include the entire Somali population, with a particular focus on the poor and those facing
specific barriers to building human capital. Improvements in the availability, coverage, reliability, and timeliness of
statistics are a public good. High-quality official statistics can improve the accountability, transparency, and efficiency of
government while also constituting useful and actionable information for the private sector. In addition, the AF’s focus on
climate and private sector development offers particular benefits to those most affected by climate shocks and limited
economic opportunities, namely, the poor.8 Finally, the AF’s continued impact on closing the gender gap in statistics
production and its focus on human capital development will likely benefit women and young people.

    (b) Gender
30.      Somali women experience severe shortfalls in access to economic resources and endowments compared to
men.9 Efforts to address these disparities are hindered by a lack of key gender statistics, including those listed in the UNSD

    (c) Climate Change and Disaster Risk
32.      Somalia is highly exposed to climatic shocks. Somalia is one of the most vulnerable countries in the world to
climate change, particularly in key sectors such as agriculture and livestock.12 Frequent droughts and other climate shocks
highlight the country's low resilience to climate risks. Building on the parent project, which strengthened Somalia's
statistical system, the AF focuses on gathering critical data on climate impacts, enhancing mitigation, adaptation, and
resilience, and guiding effective policy formation and decision-making in key sectors. The main climate-related risk
associated with the project is how climatic events might affect planned data collection activities. To mitigate these risks,
the project will utilize Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI) and satellite data to minimize the time needed in
the field. The proposed project will also promote climate change mitigation by reducing the need for in-person data
collection (and thus the associated vehicle emissions).
33.      The AF will also enhance Somalia’s capacity to report on international climate data aligning with the country’s
Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) under the Paris Agreement, aiming for a 30 percent emissions reduction by
2030, conditional on international support. This will be done through Subcomponent 1.2 by collecting crucial data through
climate modules in household and business surveys, helping to assess exposure to climate risks and vulnerabilities;
through Subcomponent 1.3 by collecting data on the impacts of climate change on businesses; and principally through
Subcomponent 1.6, which will collect statistics on agriculture through the implementation of an agricultural census. Data
collection through the project may support the estimation of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from key sectors like
agriculture, land use, and livestock, providing a basis for Somalia to establish climate mitigation policies and track progress

       H. Lessons Learned from the Parent Project

37.     Surveys and the census to be carried out under the AF will incorporate lessons learned. Nationally representative
data collection is relatively new in post-civil war Somalia. As a result, the parent project yielded many insights that will
inform future efforts. Learning from the 2022 survey, the SIHBS new round will be conducted year-round to capture
seasonality better and assess climate impacts. SNBS will continue using media outreach to educate citizens on the
importance of data collection, facilitating access to communities, and encouraging household participation. Field
operations will also account for seasonal population movements, such as summer relocations in Puntland to cooler areas
to escape extreme heat.15 Collaboration between FGS and FMS remains crucial, with consultations ensuring buy-in from
FMS while building capacity within the Ministry of Planning and Statistical offices at the FMS level. Extensive consultations
are held before all major operations involving FMS, particularly data collection in the field. Establishing a survey
management team is crucial for enhancing coordination, transparency, and information-sharing between FMS and the
SNBS. Additionally, more inclusive consultations during the design of surveys and implementation processes are essential.
Strong coordination, led by the SNBS, will remain essential to ensure transparency and effective information-sharing
between the FMS and the PIU. The SNBS will play a central role in fostering more inclusive consultations during the design
of surveys and procurement processes.

      III. KEY RISKS
38.     The overall risk rating for the AF is Substantial. Somalia has a uniquely complex operational environment with no
shortage of risks that have the potential to impede achievement of the project’s objectives. These risks are related to,
among other elements, security-related threats, inadequate institutional capacity, inadequate procurement and financial
management capacity, and the political economy. The AF will mitigate these risks by continuing to provide capacity-
building paired with technical assistance at various stages of the project.
39.      Political and governance risk is High. Despite progress made since the establishment of a federal government
based in Mogadishu and the adoption of a provisional constitution in 2012, Somalia continues to face significant challenges
to foster a more stable political settlement, expand government authority, and provide basic services to the population.
Continuous disputes among the political elite and insurgent attacks can distract or hamper efforts that are needed to
achieve the project’s objectives. Although the SNBS operates semi-autonomously, changes in political policy can impact
the institution, as demonstrated by the dismissal of the Director General during 2023. Somalia’s tradition of local
autonomy can also present challenges. Member states might object to a proposed survey, which could prevent data
collection from occurring. To mitigate this risk, consultations are conducted early on with all ministries and FMS during
the survey preparation phase. However, there have been instances where FMS raised objections even after an initial
agreement was reached.
41.      Macroeconomic risk is High. The country faces high macro risks and heavy reliance on foreign aid, making
government budget fragile. Though improving, the government’s limited capacity to manage external shocks could result
in in sharp budget declines. While the project provides vital data for planning, budget shortfalls may limit its long-term
impact. The primary strategy to mitigate macro-fiscal risks is to enhance the provision of accurate macroeconomic data
for policymakers through the statistics capacity-building project, World Bank-supported development policy financing
discussions, and investment projects aimed at improving revenue mobilization and public finance management. Ongoing
collaboration with the IMF is also critical for bolstering revenue mobilization and maintaining macroeconomic stability.
42.      Sector strategies and policies risk is High. Key MDAs (e.g. the Ministry of Agriculture, revenue authorities, FMS
statistical authorities) lack coherent sector strategies and policies for data production. Even after the completion of the
Second National Strategy for the Development of Statistics (NSDS 2), coordination within the national statistical system

43.      Technical design of project risk is Substantial. The project faces two main risks in this area: (i) the broad scope of

Instead of conducting standalone labor force and agricultural surveys, the AF will incorporate labor and agricultural

44.      Institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability risk is Substantial. Institutional capacity to
implement the AF is weak, reflecting a fragile state context. However, the existing PIU has demonstrated capability to
implement the project, resulting in on-schedule disbursement. Most survey outputs rely on field data collection, which is
feasible in most areas based on the current security assessment. However, a decline in security or political relations
between the various levels of government could disrupt fieldwork. These risks are mitigated by a carefully designed and
flexible sampling frame that allows adjustment of surveyed areas and by using spatial data from satellite images to study
areas not accessible due to security concerns. The intricate relationships between various government levels may also
cause delays in field activities. Additional supervision by the World Bank will help mitigate this risk, as will close monitoring
of workplans by the project steering committee and management team.
45.      Fiduciary risk is Substantial. The financial management and procurement environment in Somalia features many
challenges, including mismanagement, fraud, lack of transparency, and corruption. Despite existing anti-corruption
regulations, these issues persist and undermine trust in government institutions. To mitigate some of these risks, the
project design includes strict fiduciary controls, adherence to World Bank fiduciary rules, and a focus on social
accountability. Continuation of on-the-job fiduciary training and periodic reviews from the World Bank will help mitigate
these risks.
46.      Other risks: Data Privacy risk is rated as Substantial. A key concern relates to ensuring that data collected by the
implementing entities is protected and secure. In March 2023, Somalia enacted a Data Privacy Act. The Data Protection
Authority established under Section 6 of the Act provides services that facilitate protection of personal data, such as
awareness campaigns, registration, trainings, and the creation of regulations related to storing and processing data. The
Act requires all organizations that process personal data to register with the Authority; the Authority is also enabled to
investigate and inspect to ensure compliance with the Act, to handle complaints, and to take enforcement action for
breach of data privacy laws. In addition, the project will: i) require that all publicly displayed data/information is
anonymized; ii) limit the number of people with access to non-anonymized data/information; iii) ensure collected
data/information is encrypted; iv) undertake a Cybersecurity Audit (including revising the certification of IT staff) to
determine whether implementing agencies have proper data protection and security mechanisms and comply with
relevant regulations; and v) undertake staff capacity-building on data protection and security, which should include the
certification of personnel with access to public data and information.

    A. Economic, Technical and Financial Analysis
53.      Public investment in statistics is vital to planning, policymaking, and the monitoring of local and national
policies, as well as regional economic integration. It provides an array of development, social, and economic statistics
that the private sector may not be incentivized to compile. Moreover, such statistics are clearly a non-rival public good.

Once collated, different regional organizations, governments, citizens, and businesses can utilize them to facilitate
evidence-based decisions. Consequently, public investment in statistics carries positive externalities.
    B. Financial Management
54.      The Financial Management (FM) residual risk is assessed as Substantial. The AF will adopt the FM arrangements
of the parent project. This includes arrangements for planning and budgeting, accounting systems and capacity, internal
control, and oversight, in addition to procedures for the flow of funds, financial reporting, and external audit. In line with
the arrangements for Use of Country Systems applicable across the portfolio, the AF will continue to support these
initiatives.
55.     The project’s FM arrangements will continue to be managed by the External Assistance Fiduciary Section (EAFS)
in the Office of Accountant General. The financial management of the parent project has been handled by a qualified PIU
financial management specialist and EAFS accountants from the Office of the Accountant General at the Ministry of
Finance. The team will support payment processing, recording and financial reporting through the Somali Financial
Management Information System (SFMIS) at the FGS and the Somaliland Financial Management Information System
(SLFMIS). Interim unaudited financial reports (IFR) will be generated from the SFMIS and SLFMIS respectively and
submitted to the World Bank within 45 days after the end of each quarter. Oversight will be supported by the internal
audit function in the respective ministries of finance, while external audit will be conducted by the Offices of the Auditor
General, with both audit reports submitted to the Bank within 6 months after the end of the fiscal year. Project funds will
be disbursed into a designated account opened at the Central Bank of Somalia for FGS and the East Africa Bank in Djibouti
for Somaliland in line with existing arrangements under the parent project. The World Bank will continue to conduct
regular FM implementation support missions and capacity-building of project teams to mitigate potential fiduciary risks.

project, insteas an electronic waste management plan was prepared under the parent project will be used to mitigate

                                Annex 1. Detailed Description of Climate Co-benefits

Component 1: Strengthening Coordination, Statistical Infrastructure, and Data Development of the NSS

1.   This component is pivotal in addressing critical climate data gaps, enhancing Somalia’s resilience to climate
impacts, and guiding evidence-based climate policies.

Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS).
2.     The new round of the SIHBS under AF adds two new features to collect data to address vulnerability from
climate change. First, it will include climate modules to collect data on exposure to extreme weather, loss of
livelihoods (especially for agricultural and pastoral communities), and adaptive behaviors like migration or new
farming practices. Second, the survey will take place over 12 months to capture seasonal variations in vulnerabilities
to climate change in Somalia.
Impacts:
     • Provides data for crafting climate adaptation strategies for Somalia’s most vulnerable populations.
     • Provide climate SDG indicators such as the proportion of the population with primary reliance on clean
         cooking fuels and technology and persons affected by disasters.

Integrated Business Establishment Survey (IBES)
3.    Incorporating climate modules into the Integrated Business Establishment Survey (IBES) will provide
unprecedented data on business vulnerability to climate change. These modules will assess the economic impact of
climate shocks on firms, supply chains, and market access.
Impacts:
    • Facilitates the development of targeted climate finance mechanisms to support climate resilience in the
        private sector, especially among small and medium enterprises (SMEs).

Agricultural census and survey
4.     Agriculture is highly vulnerable to climate variability, particularly drought. In Somalia, the impacts of climate
change are especially pronounced in rural areas, where pastoral nomads are significantly affected. For decades,
Somalia has lacked comprehensive agricultural data, hindering efforts to assess the climate risks faced by this sector.
The funding from AF will allow Somalia to conduct its first Agricultural Census, which will gather data on crop yields,
livestock populations, and resource usage, such as water and fertilizers. Additionally, an expanded agricultural
module will be incorporated into the SIHBS to track the adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices, including
drought-resistant crops and efficient irrigation systems.
Impacts:
     • Informs government policy and development programs to increase agricultural resilience to climate change.
     • Supports adaptation efforts through data-driven programs to promote sustainable land and water use,
         thereby reducing the sector's vulnerability to drought and land degradation.

Food and Nutrition Monitoring
5.    Food security in Somalia is closely intertwined with the impacts of climate change, particularly droughts. The
AF will fund the bi-annual Food and Nutrition Security (FNS) Joint Monitoring Report (JMR), as required by the 2023
National Food and Nutrition Security Crisis Preparedness Plan. The JMR will frequently monitor climate factors that
affect food security, such as floods, droughts, and precipitation. The reporting protocol will also include a process for
triggering the preparedness plan to address emerging food security crises.
Impacts:
     • Mobilizes earlier responses to food and nutrition security crises caused by climate change.

Component 2: ICT Development Infrastructure and Equipment of the NSS

6.    The ICT component is instrumental in ensuring that climate data is collected, processed, and shared efficiently
across multiple government agencies and regions.

Digital infrastructure for climate data processing
7.     By upgrading Somalia’s ICT infrastructure, including data centers, networks, and servers, this component
ensures that climate-related data is processed in real-time and disseminated quickly to relevant stakeholders. This
infrastructure supports the collection of GHG data, meteorological data, and environmental statistics, feeding into
the country’s climate monitoring systems.
Impacts:
    • Accelerates data-driven decision-making, crucial for disaster preparedness and a rapid and effective
         response to climate shocks like droughts or floods.
    • Supports regional collaboration on climate monitoring, particularly in the context of Somalia’s new
         membership of the EAC and the IGAD, where joint climate data systems are critical for cross-border climate
         adaptation efforts.

Connectivity between regions
8.    The extension of ICT infrastructure to FMS, and Somaliland, will ensure that climate-related data is accessible
across all administrative regions. This will improve coordination between the central government and local
authorities in responding to localized climate impacts, particularly in remote and conflict-affected areas.
Impacts:
    • Ensures that local governments are empowered with data to implement localized climate adaptation
        strategies.
    • Facilitates knowledge-sharing and collaboration on climate resilience between FMS, strengthening national
        capacity to respond to climate-related challenges.

Component 3: Data Utilization for Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E) and Planning
9.   This component enables the Somali government to use climate data effectively for planning and decision-
making, addressing long-term climate risks and integrating adaptation strategies into national development policies.

National Transformation Plan (NTP)
10. The National Transformation Plan (NTP) for 2025-2029 presents an opportunity to incorporate climate
adaptation and mitigation into national planning. One of its four pillars is "Environment and Climate Resilience,"
which focuses on renewable energy, disaster risk reduction, climate adaptation, and resilience. The AF will provide
policy analysis and M&E to support the NTP, with a quarter of the plan dedicated to addressing climate change.
Impacts:
    • Tracks and evaluates climate actions and outcomes of national development goals, ensuring that economic
        growth strategies are sustainable and adaptive to climate change.
National Economic Council (NEC)
11. The NEC will receive support in producing environmental and climate policy studies across vital sectors. It will
also provide advisory services and create periodic economic reports, including the flagship Annual State of the
Economy Report. Additionally, the project will assist in the final phase of developing the Centennial Vision 2060,
which aims to address the adverse impacts of climate change and promote the country's blue economy among its
four key focus areas. This support will involve organizing policy dialogue forums and coordinating mechanisms across
various ministries.

   • Embeds addressing climate impacts in the country’s long-term development strategy as well as policy studies
       for the government decision.
