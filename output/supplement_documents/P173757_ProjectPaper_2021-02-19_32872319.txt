I. BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING

        A. Introduction

    1.       This Project Paper seeks the approval of the World Bank’s Board of Executive Directors to
    provide a credit in the amount of SDR 347.1 million (US$500 million equivalent) IDA for an Additional
    Financing (AF). The AF would support the costs of expanding activities of the Bangladesh COVID-19
    Emergency Response and Pandemic Preparedness Project (P173757, IDA Cr. 6605-BD) under the COVID-
    19 Strategic Preparedness and Response Program (SPRP) using the Multiphase Programmatic Approach
    (MPA), approved by the Board on April 2, 2020, and the vaccines AF to the SPRP approved on October 13,
    20201. The primary objectives of the AF are to enable affordable and equitable access to COVID-19
    vaccines and help ensure effective vaccine deployment in Bangladesh through vaccination system
    strengthening, and to further strengthen preparedness and response activities under the parent project.
    The Bangladesh COVID-19 Emergency Response and Pandemic Preparedness Project—in an amount of
    US$100 million equivalent IDA credit—was approved on April 3, 2020 under the SPRP. The parent project
    is also being restructured to update component descriptions and costs to reflect the scale-up of activities
    for vaccination, revise the results framework, and incorporate co-financing from the Asian Infrastructure
    Investment Bank (AIIB) for the original components.

    2.       In addition, this Project Paper seeks the World Bank’s Board of Executive Directors’ approval of
    a modification of the threshold for eligibility of IBRD/IDA resources in vaccine purchase (Vaccine
    Approval Criteria) that was approved by the Board as part of the COVID-19 Strategic Preparedness and
    Response Program (SPRP) Additional Financing Project Paper. There is currently only one vaccine that
    meets the threshold of (i) approval by three Stringent Regulatory Authorities (SRAs) in three regions, or
    (ii) World Health Organization (WHO) prequalification and approval by one SRA. Given the urgent need to
    accelerate vaccination campaigns and the significant progress made by other vaccines toward meeting
    these standards, this Project Paper seeks World Bank’s Board of Executive Directors’ approval to modify,
    for the purpose of this Project, the Vaccine Approval Criteria to either: (i) the vaccine has been approved
    by three Stringent Regulatory Authorities (including by Emergency Use Authorization) in two Regions; or
    (ii) the vaccine has received WHO Emergency Use Listing, and has been produced under a licensing or
    similar arrangement from a manufacturer of a parent/bioequivalent vaccine that has received a Stringent
    Regulatory Authority approval (including Emergency Use Authorization).

    3.      The purpose of the proposed AF is to help the government purchase and deploy safe and
    effective COVID-19 vaccines that meet World Bank’s Vaccine Approval Criteria and strengthen relevant
    health systems that are necessary for a successful deployment and to prepare for the future. The
    proposed AF will help vaccinate about 31 percent of the country’s population, comprising deployment of
    vaccines acquired by Bangladesh under COVAX Advance Market Commitment (AMC) to cover about 20
    percent of the population, and acquisition and deployment of vaccines to cover an estimated additional

 5.      Critically, the AF seeks to enable the acquisition of vaccines from a range of sources to support
 Bangladesh’s objective to have a portfolio of options to access vaccines under the right conditions (of
 value-for-money, regulatory standards and delivery time among other key features). The COVAX facility
 has put in place a framework that will anchor Bangladesh’s strategy and access to vaccines: on December
 6, 2020, Bangladesh submitted its application to COVAX that was subsequently accepted for purchase of
 vaccines. The proposed IDA financing will expand Bangladesh’s access. The availability and terms of
 vaccines remain fluid and prevent the planning of a firm sequence of vaccine deployment: rather the
 proposed financing enables a portfolio approach that will adjust during implementation in response to
 developments in the country pandemic situation and the global market for vaccines.

 6.       In addition to the proposed AF, the parent project is being restructured to: (a) incorporate
 financing of US$100 million from AIIB under a co-financing arrangement with the World Bank; (b) change

 activities under the parent project (US$100 million from AIIB will not be used for COVID-19 vaccination
 related activities); (c) add a new disbursement category for the proposed AF; (d) modify components and
 costs; (e) add legal covenants in support of the proposed AF; and (f) revise the results framework. No
 other changes are proposed to the project.

     B. Parent Project Design and Scope

 8.      The project development objective (PDO) is “to prevent, detect and respond to the threat posed
 by COVID-19 and strengthen national systems for public health preparedness”. The project comprises four
 components supporting implementation of the Bangladesh Preparedness and Response Plan for COVID-
 19 (BPRP) and includes a contingent emergency response component (CERC). The BPRP outlines a multi-
 sector coordination structure and ten technical pillars, estimating US$811 million to be required for the
 national response for COVID-19. Thus far several development partners have mobilized resources for a

 total of US$324 million3, including the ongoing World Bank support comprising the parent project and
 grant funds from the Pandemic Emergency Facility (PEF).

 9.      The MoHFW is leading implementation of the project and a project implementation unit (PIU)
 has been set-up under the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS). In addition, the MoHFW has
 constituted a Project Steering Committee (PSC) led by the Secretary of the Health Services Division of the
 MoHFW, and a Project Implementation Committee (PIC) led by the Director General of DGHS, for overall
 supervision of the project. These committees are convening regularly.

     C. Project Performance

 10.      The project’s progress towards achievement of the PDO is rated as satisfactory and overall
 implementation progress as moderately satisfactory, since project approval in April 2020. This PDO
 rating reflects the government’s efforts in expanding COVID-19 testing capacity and reducing the
 incidence of COVID-19 cases. As of January 31, 2021, US$37.4 million have been disbursed, which includes
 US$19.7 million for direct payments to suppliers and advance to United Nations (UN) agencies for
 procurement of goods and services. These include critical items such as ventilators, personal protective
 equipment (PPE), oxygen concentrators, COVID-19 testing kits, etc. In addition, awareness campaign
 regarding COVID-19 has been implemented through mobile based messaging and television. By March 15,
 2021, an additional US$6 million is expected to be disbursed for installation of liquid medical oxygen
 systems at public health facilities. With declining cases of COVID-19, the government has phased-out of
 the emergency response mode and is focusing on COVID-19 vaccination related activities, which is why
 there is relatively low disbursement under the parent project. The balance of US$56.6 million of the
 original IDA credit together with US$100 million from AIIB will be used to implement activities planned
 under the parent project, which will be scaled-up, including COVID-19 prevention, detection and
 treatment; strengthening laboratory capacity and hospital services; and non-pharmaceutical
 interventions (NPI), as further explained below.

 11.     The PIU, set-up under the DGHS, is coordinating project planning, management and
 procurement.The PIU incudes the required staffing – Project Director, Deputy Project Director, and
 consultants for procurement, project management/monitoring and evaluation (M&E), financial
 management (FM), environment and social. In addition, four medical officers from DGHS have been
 deputed to the PIU. As mentioned above, the MoHFW has constituted a PSC and a PIC, which are
 convening regularly. With these actions, the government has complied with all three legal covenants
 agreed during preparation of the parent project. The PIU is using country FM systems through the Office
 of the Chief Accounts and is supported by the DGHS accounts officer. For procurement, the PIU directly
 contracted local suppliers to meet emergency needs, as mentioned above, during the lockdown period in
 April/May 2020 when the global market was disrupted with no international flights. In parallel, the PIU
 entered into agreements with the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) for procurement of PPEs,
 masks, gloves, oxygen concentrators, etc., which are being supplied now and with WHO for procurement

     D. Rationale for Additional Financing and Project Restructuring

 13.      Despite the declining trend in newly reported COVID-19 cases, test positivity levels, and deaths,
 Bangladesh remains vulnerable to the risks of COVID-19. Community transmission of COVID-19 persists,
 with a large proportion of cases asymptomatic. A seroprevalence survey conducted between April and
 July 2020 indicated that at least 45 percent of Dhaka, one of the most densely populated cities in the
 world, had been infected with the COVID-19 virus. Despite this high level of infection, immunization is still
 a critical activity. Many people have not been infected, and those who have been previously infected may
 benefit from a stronger and longer-lasting immune response from the vaccine. The risk of being clinically
 severely ill from COVID-19 is particularly high among the population aged 60 years and above who account
 for 8 percent of the total population but 60 percent of total reported deaths from the disease in
 Bangladesh. At the same time, a high burden of non-communicable diseases among the adult population
 as a whole increases the risks associated with COVID-19. For example, prevalence of hypertension among
 people aged 35 years and older was 45 percent among females and 34 percent among males, while the
 prevalence of diabetes was 14 percent among both females and males in 2017 (Bangladesh Demographic
 and Health Survey 2017-18). Moreover, among those with non-communicable diseases, co-morbidity is
 common. Hence, it is important to vaccinate people to ensure their immunity against COVID-19.

   Box 2: Liability and Indemnification Issues in Vaccine Acquisition
    Background. While vaccines save millions of lives, there is also a risk that they can cause serious adverse events
    (SAEs). Such events can lead to litigation against vaccine manufacturers. Rapid development of vaccines increases
    the risk of vaccine manufacturers’ potential liability for AEFI, which can in turn affect research and development and
    future supply. Manufacturers seek to protect themselves from this risk by including clauses to limit their liability in
    their supply contracts, such as indemnification provisions and immunity from suit and liability clauses. While
    contractual provisions and domestic legal frameworks can all operate to allocate that risk among market



                                                                                                                        Page 17 of 75
The World Bank
Additional Financing for and Restructuring of the COVID-19 Emergency Response and Pandemic Preparedness
Project (P175837)



  participants, no mechanism will eliminate this risk entirely. Some countries also have no-fault compensation
  schemes to provide compensations to claimants for vaccine-related injuries.

  For vaccines acquired under the COVAX AMC, recognizing that manufacturers will require COVAX AMC Participants
  to provide a form of indemnity against product liability claims, COVAX has developed model language for indemnity
  agreements for use by COVAX AMC Participants for vaccines purchased and supplied under the COVAX AMC. In
  providing vaccines through COVAX AMC, COVAX requests COVAX AMC Participants to have in place an indemnity
  agreement directly with manufacturers, and the necessary indemnity and liability frameworks for that purpose –
  either in the form of the COVAX model indemnification arrangements or prior bilateral arrangements with
  manufacturers. Additionally, as part of its risk mitigation strategy, the COVAX Facility is developing a no-fault
  compensation mechanism to provide compensation to those individuals in COVAX AMC group participants who
  suffer an SAE which is found to be associated with the vaccines supplied through COVAX AMC.

  Bangladesh will have to consider what it will take to implement these indemnification provisions (including statutory
  implementation) and how they can avail of the benefits of the no-fault compensation scheme, if necessary. To date,
  COVID-19 AEFI surveillance and effective monitoring and evaluation systems are detailed in the NDVP. A National
  AEFI Expert Review Committee has been established by the MoHFW for review of AEFI cases, and criteria for
  compensation and indemnity clauses for vaccine manufacturers will be worked out.

  For vaccines purchased outside of COVAX, Bangladesh will need to enter into direct arrangements with
  manufacturers. To date, the GoB has entered into a tri-partite agreement with Serum Institute of India and Beximco
  Pharmaceuticals in Bangladesh, under which 30 million doses of COVID-19 vaccines were procured.

  Bangladesh does not currently have legislation in place to provide statutory immunity for manufacturers.
  Bangladesh also does not have a national no fault compensation scheme. Adoption of any such indemnification
  provisions or compensation scheme would have to be in accordance with Bangladesh’s own national strategy and
  framework.

  World Bank Assistance. The World Bank may assist Bangladesh through: (a) sharing information on: (i) statutory

  (b) providing training and workshops for government officials to familiarize them with the issues; and (c) for the
  World Bank-financed contracts, the World Bank can provide Hands-on Expanded Implementation Support (HEIS).

 Project Components

 26.     Component 1: Emergency COVID-19 Response provides immediate support to Bangladesh to
 prevent and treat COVID-19 through containment strategies including community support, deploying
 COVID-19 vaccination, as well as strengthening health systems through enhancing capacity of public
 health facilities, laboratories and immunization delivery. Specifically, this component is supporting:
        •   Sub-component 1.1 Case Detection, Confirmation, Contact Tracing, Recording, Reporting is
            provide assistance to: (a) strengthen public health laboratories and epidemiological capacity for
            detection and confirmation of cases; (b) combine detection of new cases with active contact
            tracing; (c) support epidemiological investigation; (d) strengthen risk assessment; and (e) provide
            real time data and information for guiding decision-making for response and mitigation activities.
        •   Sub-component 1.2 Social Distancing Measures is supporting implementation of social distancing
            measures and other prevention behaviors, such as mask wearing, through support teams and
            engagement of local communities.
        •   Sub-component 1.3 Health System Strengthening is providing support for strengthening capacity
            of selected public health facilities with additional capacity for oxygen therapy, intensive care units,
            isolation units, and IPC capacity including hospital infection surveillance and audit, triage, medical
            waste management systems.
        •   Sub-component 1.4 Community Preparedness is supporting high risk communities to develop and
            implement community preparedness and response plan, inter alia, community awareness
            strategy development, testing, and implementation; and for COVID-19 containment such as home
            quarantine, encouragement of mask wearing through community engagement, encouraging
            handwashing and hygiene practices, etc.

 27.     Component 2: Supporting National and Sub-national, Prevention and Preparedness is
 supporting activities which aim to improve prevention and response planning for emerging infectious
 diseases. Specifically, the component will support:
        •   Emergency Operations Center (EOC): Support for expanding the scope and improving the
            functionality of the Public Health EOC, including, inter alia, upgrading the system for
            epidemiological intelligence, connectivity, information gathering, analysis and utilization, and
            rapid response; improving risk communication and community engagement; and capacity

 Proposed New Activities

 30.     Vaccine purchasing will be done through Component 1 of the Global COVID-19 MPA (SPRP). The
 support for vaccines when available, which was anticipated in the initial Global COVID-19 MPA, will be
 scaled up as part of the containment and mitigation measures to prevent the spread of COVID-19 and
 deaths under Component 1: Emergency COVID-19 Response. Bangladesh will use the following options
 for vaccine purchase and financing mechanisms: (a) direct purchases from vaccine manufacturers; (b)
 purchase of stocks from other countries that reserve excess doses; and/or (c) advance purchase
 mechanisms by participating in COVAX. Given the unprecedented pace of vaccine development, and the
 need for both speed and prudence, COVID-19 vaccines financed under the proposed AF shall satisfy the
 World Bank’s Vaccine Approval Criteria.

 31.      The proposed AF will support investments to bring immunization systems and service delivery
 capacity to the level required to successfully deliver COVID-19 vaccines at scale, through Component 1.
 To this end, the proposed AF is geared to assist the GoB, working with the World Bank, WHO, UNICEF and
 other technical partners, to overcome bottlenecks as identified in COVID-19 vaccine readiness assessment
 in the country. COVID-19 vaccination related activities are described under newly added sub-component
 1.5 and will be financed using the proposed AF of US$500 million. These resources will be used for: (a)
 strengthening capacity for regulatory authority of DGDA, as well as cold chain system, storage
 transportation and operational cost; and (b) vaccination for about 31 percent of the population, i.e.,
 approximately 54.1 million people.

 33.      The COVID-19 pandemic has exacerbated existing gender gaps in Bangladesh as women’s
 livelihoods, mobility and ability to access healthcare has been limited. The pandemic as well as the roll-
 out of vaccinations has gender implications. Vaccinations will be provided in a context where women face
 numerous barriers in accessing healthcare services including educational attainment, social norms around
 women’s mobility, mass media exposure, financial resources, and identity documents particularly for the
 more vulnerable living in slum areas. In this context, women’s access to vaccines is compounded by lack
 of adequate information on vaccines and its distribution, and presence of female vaccinators. At the same
 time, Bangladesh also has many female front-line healthcare workers in the form of community health
 volunteers and nurses who can be trained and deployed as vaccinators. The NDVP recognizes gender gaps
 in healthcare access, vaccine hesitancy and obstacles due to lack of female vaccinators. In order to ensure
 proper outreach and vaccination of women, the project will support targeted communication, as outlined
 in the NDVP, to disseminate information regarding the COVID-19 vaccination, particularly to dispel
 misconceptions about vaccines. To encourage women to get vaccinated, the vaccination centers will
 include female vaccinators who will also support outreach activities at the community level. The NDVP
 specifies that the vaccination team will include at least one female and out of four volunteers, there will
 be at least one female volunteer. In addition, most EPI vaccinators engaged with child immunization and
 health workers at the community and union levels will be women. They will also be mobilized for COVID-
 19 vaccines as well as prioritized to receive vaccines. Vaccination sessions will be planned considering the
 privacy of clients.

    Consistency with the Country Partnership Framework (CPF)

    39.      The proposed AF is aligned with the CPF’s focus on social inclusion. The primary focus of the
    World Bank’s CPF for Bangladesh covering the period fiscal year (FY) 2016 to FY20 (Report No. 103723-
    BD, dated March 8, 2016 discussed at the Board on April 5, 2016) is to address constraints to growth and
    competitiveness. The CPF has been extended to FY2021 after the Program Learning Review (PLR). The
    PLR reaffirmed the three CPF focus areas: (a) growth and competitiveness; (b) social inclusion; and (c)
    climate and environment management. The indicative IDA19 allocation for Bangladesh is US$4 billion, of
    which US$1.8 billion is frontloaded to FY21. With the adjusted lending program proposed in the PLR fully
    delivered, the lending program for FY21–FY22 is geared to supporting the jobs and economic
    transformation agenda, expanding social safety nets, and building institutional and investment
    resilience—all priorities the COVID-19 crisis has made even more urgent. Annex 2 describes the
    adjustments made to the Bangladesh country program in response to COVID-19. The CPF aims to
    consolidate HNP gains while continuing to improve equity and address emerging challenges. This
    proposed AF respond to the unprecedented COVID-19 outbreak, which has also increased the importance
    and prioritization of health protection and treatment in the country. By building the strength of the health
    system and its resilience to shocks, it is aligned with the focus of the CPF on social inclusion, since human
    development provides a foundation for economic growth while protection of the poor is necessary for
    inclusive growth. The proposed AF, like the parent project, is also aligned with both global health priorities
    and World Bank priorities on improving pandemic preparedness.

III. KEY RISKS

      42.      The overall risk to achieving the PDO with the expanded scope of the project for vaccination is
      High. The ratings for all risk categories remain the same as those of the parent project, except for technical
      risks, as described below.

      43.     Macroeconomic risk remains Substantial. Bangladesh’s macroeconomic position was strong
      when the COVID-19 crisis started in March 2020. Since then, growth declined sharply to an estimated two
      percent in FY20 as the COVID-19 epidemic caused major disruptions to economic activities. According to
      the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the pandemic’s impact on growth in
      Bangladesh has been less than in other South Asian economies. Although exports and remittances in
      recent months have shown signs of early recovery, full economic recovery will be gradual as global
      economic disruptions continue. There is a risk that sufficient additional fiscal space may not be available
      to the GoB for COVID-19 vaccination at scale. This would be partially mitigated by the proposed AF.
      Residual macroeconomic risk will remain as the country aims to expand coverage of vaccination to 80
      percent of the population.

      of NPIs; and (b) risks associated with vaccination. Reducing the COVID-19 reproductive factor below one
      requires a combination of both NPI as well as vaccination since there is no effective cure. As it is still

      timely manner; and (b) a mass vaccination effort stretches capacity, entailing risks since Bangladesh’s
      successful large-scale vaccination campaigns have been for child immunization and not for adults. There
      are uncertainties related to the COVID-19 vaccine market, including testing, approval, availability and
      pricing, which require flexibility, close monitoring and enhanced supervision from the World Bank during

 assessments in coordination with the WHO, Gavi, UNICEF, and other partners. Besides, the vaccination

     using electronic media as well as local government offices in rural areas to disseminate information.
     Mobile based applications and text messages are also being used for communication. Local elected
     representatives and religious leaders will be mobilized.

A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis

     49.     The economic effects of COVID-19 derive from increased morbidity and mortality, direct costs
     for healthcare, the immediate impact on economic activities, and the long-term impact on human capital
     accumulation (due to school closures, etc.). In addition to the health impacts directly attributed to COVID-
     19, the pandemic poses a significant risk of indirect morbidity and mortality from other preventable and
     treatable diseases if the provision of essential health services is disrupted. Without a vaccine, the country

The World Bank

Project (P175837)



 will continue to face both the health and economic losses as the epidemic continues.

 50.      The real gross domestic product (GDP) growth rate fell to an estimated two percent in FY20 after
 the outbreak of COVID-19 and the associated global disruptions in economic activity in 2020. Economic
 growth is projected to decelerate further to 1.6 percent in FY21 assuming that the impact of COVID-19
 deepens. A micro-simulation conducted by the World Bank indicates that, on average, per capita
 household consumption would decline by 13 percent, implying that as many as 21 million people could
 fall below the poverty line. GDP growth is projected to recover to 3.4 percent in FY22, supported by a
 recovery in export demand, remittance inflows, and public investment.

 51.      The economic case for investing in COVID-19 vaccine for Bangladesh is very strong, considering
 the losses already incurred due to the pandemic. The activities to be financed by the proposed AF and the
 parent project will yield substantial returns in reduced morbidity and mortality, thus averting further
 economic damage. The economic losses due to COVID-19 in FY20 and FY21 are estimated to be US$35
 billion, based on a comparison of actual growth and pre-COVID forecasts. The proposed AF of US$500
 million compares favorably. While the uncertainty around the costs and effectiveness of a COVID-19
 vaccine makes it difficult to calculate its cost-effectiveness, the effective launch of a COVID-19 vaccine will
 have direct benefits in terms of averted costs of treatment and disability, as well as strengthened health
 systems. Regarding equity considerations, project investments will contribute to supporting the GoB in
 implementing COVID-19 vaccination in an equitable manner based on a well-targeted approach as
 described in the NDVP. It will also strengthen systems for immunization delivery which will generate
 health and economic benefits beyond just delivering the COVID-19 vaccine. First, investments in last-mile
 delivery systems to administer the COVID-19 vaccine to remote communities will require strengthening
 community health systems, which can have spillover effects to effective delivery of other services, helping
 close the significant urban-rural gap. Second, as the COVID-19 vaccine is introduced and movement
 restrictions are eased, people can continue to access care for other conditions. Third, the economic
 benefits of slowing down the economic downturn are likely to significantly exceed the US$500 million
 investments that will support vaccination of approximately 31 percent of the population, in addition to
 the immediate health benefits. Given both the economic and health system benefits, an effectively
 deployed COVID-19 vaccination campaign presents significant benefits.

 52.      Due to the “public good” nature of immunization, the proposed AF will benefit the entire
 population. Other benefits not quantified here include poverty alleviation and psychological benefits of a
 healthy population. Healthier people can work more productively and thus earn a higher return in the
 labor market. The proposed AF will contribute to human capital development and increase productivity
 directly by reducing incapacity. The project and the proposed AF will, therefore, contribute to inclusive
 and sustainable economic growth in Bangladesh.

 53.      The choice of interventions supported by the proposed AF and their technical design features
 are consistent with the strategies recommended globally to slow down the transmission of COVID-19
 and prevent associated illness and death. Although the global community is still learning about this virus,
 significant evidence has been already accumulated about how COVID-19 spreads, the severity of disease
 it causes, how to treat it, and how to stop its transmission. This evidence and associated lessons11 are

E. Environmental and Social

     69.      Activities under the proposed AF will have positive impacts as it will improve capacity for
     surveillance, monitoring and containment of COVID-19. However, it could also cause environment,
     health and safety risks due to the dangerous nature of the pathogen (COVID-19) and reagents and
     equipment used in the project-supported activities. Facilities treating patients may also generate
     biological, chemical waste, and other hazardous by-products (including those from agents, devices and
     cooling facilities) that could be injurious to human health. These risks will be mitigated with occupational
     health and safety standards and specific infectious-control strategies, guidelines and requirements as
     recommended by WHO and the United States Center for Disease Control. Effective administrative and
     infectious-controlling and engineering controls would be put in place to minimize these risks. Climate

F. Gender

    72.     Gender is a critical consideration when designing policies and intervention in emergency
    situations and pandemics. It plays an important role in who gets access and how fast, to critical health
    services. Gender also determines the social roles ascribed to people that can influence their risk of
    exposure to disease, as well as of spreading it. At the same time, the biological sex can influence how
    susceptible a person is to disease and how well they respond to treatment and/or vaccines. In a pandemic
    this has multiple implications. On the one hand, pandemic response has to be cognizant of the gender-
    based differences in access to and use of services due to limited mobility and financial capacity; and on
    the other, support needs to be provided to at-risk groups such as caregivers (majority of whom are women
    taking care of children and the elderly) to reduce their risk of getting ill and/or passing it on to others.
    Moreover, pandemics can create or exacerbate vulnerabilities that especially put women and girls at risk
    of sexual exploitation. Lockdown and additional economic stresses have increased the risks of gender-
    based violence and medium-term risk of child-marriage. Bangladesh Rural Advancement Committee
    (BRAC, an NGO) documented a nearly 70 percent increase in reported incidents of violence against women
    and girls in March and April 2020 compared to the same time last year. This is a major concern as
    Bangladesh already has high prevalence of gender-based violence with over 70 percent of married women
    reporting intimate partner violence in their lifetime. Sexual Exploitation and Abuse/Sexual Harassment
    (SEA/SH) risks associated with project activities will be assessed. To mitigate potential SEA/SH risks, the
    campaign will integrate SEA/SH components in trainings for healthcare workers, disseminate information
    on available services and helplines, and integrate SEA/SH mitigation in the SEP.

G. Climate vulnerability and resilience

     73.      Bangladesh is extremely vulnerable to the effects of climate change. The Global Climate Risk
     Index ranks Bangladesh as the world’s seventh most affected country over the period 1999-2018.14 Rising
     temperatures leading to more intense and unpredictable rainfalls during the monsoon season and a
     higher probability of catastrophic cyclones are expected to result in increased tidal inundation. This has
     been evident in the recent super cyclone Amphan, which hit Bangladesh during lockdown, and the heavy
     monsoon rains which led to flooding in various parts of the country whereby over one-third of the country
     was flooded, over 4.9 million people affected, and 42 people lost their lives: evacuation efforts were made
     all the more difficult in the context of maintaining COVID precautionary measures.

     74.     According to the most recent assessment report of the Intergovernmental panel on Climate
     Change (IPCC, 2013), continued emissions of greenhouse gases will cause further warming in
     Bangladesh. Observed data indicate that the temperature is generally increasing in the monsoon season
     (June-August). Average monsoon season maximum and minimum temperatures show an increasing trend
     annually at the rate of 0.05°C and 0.03°C, respectively. An increasing trend of about 1°C in May and 0.5°C
     in November during the 14-year period from 1985-1998 was observed15. Mean temperatures across
     Bangladesh are projected to increase between 1.4°C and 2.4°C by 2050 and 2100, respectively. This
     warming is expected to be more pronounced in the winter months (December-February). Average
     temperatures are expected to increase between 1°C and 2°C by 2100, with similar rates of warming
     projected to occur across the country16. Climate change is likely to increase the incidence of vaccine
     preventable diseases like dengue fever and cholera. A study on Bangladesh revealed strong correlation
     between weather variables (temperature, rainfall and humidity) and incidence of these climate-sensitive
     diseases17. Climate suitability for climate-sensitive infectious diseases has increased globally; for example,
     the number of cases of dengue fever recorded annually has doubled every decade since 1990, and one of
     the potential factors that contributed to this increase is climate change18.

     75.      The proposed AF will consider the vulnerability to climate change and disaster risks in design
     and implementation of activities under Component 1. For COVID-19 vaccination, the cold chain facilities
     to be set-up under the proposed AF will use renewable energy (solar-based cold chain) or other energy
     efficient cold storage technology, as feasible. This will strengthen measures in vaccine supply and
     distribution chains to improve energy efficiency or resource efficiency upstream or downstream, leading
     to an overall reduction in greenhouse gas emissions. Under Component 1, activities for strengthening
     oxygen supply in public health facilities will fill a critical gap in hospital care in Bangladesh beyond the
     immediate necessity of COVID-19 treatment by providing access to expanded oxygen treatment to those
     particularly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change, e.g., patients with cardio-vascular diseases,

 Annex 2 – Adjusting the Bangladesh Country Program in Response to COVID-19

 1.       The World Bank Group engagement in Bangladesh has been guided by the FY2016–20 Country
 Partnership Framework (CPF), which has been extended to FY2021 after the Program Learning Review
 (PLR). The PLR reaffirmed the three CPF focus areas: (1) growth and competitiveness; (2) social inclusion;
 and (3) climate and environment management. It also recognized the recent program adjustment in
 response to the Rohingya refugee crisis, and recommended greater attention to human capital, climate
 resilience, and digital transformation.

 The Country’s Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic

 2.       COVID-19 pandemic has severely disrupted economic activity and created an unprecedented
 crisis that is likely to worsen poverty in the short term. As the pandemic intensified globally, Bangladesh
 exports to Europe and the United States declined precipitously. Growth in GDP is projected to range in
 FY20 between 1.6 and 1.0 percent and in FY21 between 1.0 and –3.0 percent. The country’s achievement
 in reducing poverty between 2000 and 2016—the national poverty rate fell from 48.9 to 24.5 percent and
 extreme poverty from 34.3 to 13.0 percent—will likely be reversed. Slower GDP growth, the income losses
 of informal workers throughout the economy, and lower international remittances may add 8 to 12
 percentage points to what the 2020 poverty rate would have been without COVID-19.

 3.     Along with its health care response to the pandemic, the government has also adopted an
 emergency economic program. Its goals are to: (1) increase public spending to generate employment; (2)
 provide a stimulus package offering firms credit at low interest rates to retain workers in the
 manufacturing sector, maintain competitiveness of the enterprises especially in the export-oriented
 manufacturing sector and to revitalize economic activities; (3) expand social safety nets to meet the basic
 needs of the poor, day laborers, and other informal sector workers; and (4) increase the money supply to
 maintain liquidity in the economy while containing inflation.

 4.      The government has reached out to partners to support the national response plan (BPRP). The
 IMF Board on May 29, 2020, approved a request for disbursement of 50 percent of Bangladesh’s quota
 (US$732 million) from the Rapid Credit Facility and the Rapid Financing Instrument. ADB approved a
 US$100 million loan for health sector emergency support and a US$500 million loan in budget support
 from its Countercyclical Support Facility. The AIIB provided US$250 million in co-financing to the ADB
 budget support and JICA is preparing another US$300 million in co-financing.

 5.      Bangladesh has opted not to participate in the G20 Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI).
 However, the authorities will continue to monitor the situation and if it becomes necessary will revisit
 their position.

 Annex 3 – COVID-19 Vaccine Financing

 Summary of the results of the assessment to date:

 9.      With support of the World Bank, WHO, UNICEF and other technical partners, the GoB has
 conducted an assessment of its readiness to roll out a COVID-19 vaccination program, using the
 VRAF/VIRAT. Based on the data of the needs assessment, the proposed AF will support procurement of
 vaccines and associated consumables as well as for specific elements of health system readiness for
 delivery of the vaccine, with a focus on supply management, and adverse events following immunization
 (AEFI) and information systems. However, given the uncertainty related to the availability, types, pricing,
 and supply chain requirements, and the fact that national policies and guidelines are still being developed,
 the proposed AF includes flexibility to adapt to changes during implementation.

 10.     Target populations: the MoHFW aims to prioritize 40 percent of the total population of the
 country for vaccination, of which approximately 31 percent will be covered by the proposed AF. As
 mentioned above, these include priority groups such as frontline health and social workers, elderly
 people, and people who have co-morbidities in line with the NDVP. The final list of priority groups will be
 reviewed again against those criteria recommended by WHO for their eligibility. In total, 80 percent of the
 population will be vaccinated.

 11.    Vaccine procurement: Under the project there are three mechanisms for Bangladesh to get access
 to COVID-19 vaccines: (a) procurement through advance purchase mechanism such as COVAX, which will
 cover up to 20 percent of the total population; (b) additional procurement of COVID-19 vaccines from
 COVAX beyond the initial allocation of 20 percent and is subject to availability; and (c) direct procurement
 from manufacturers.

 12.     Planning and Management: The MoHFW has prepared the NDVP which identifies the objectives,
 strategies and targets for COVID-19 vaccination as well as implementation arrangements, accountability,
 governance structure, monitoring and evaluation mechanism, and cost estimation. The following

 16.    The NDVP includes a results framework and describes the monitoring and evaluation
 arrangements to be used for COVID-19 vaccination. The existing Grievance Redressing Mechanism of the
 DGHS for vaccine preventable diseases in children will be used for COVID-19 vaccination and deployment.
 The needs for human resource have been estimated and there may be need for surge capacity. All human
 resources involved with COVID-19 vaccination are being trained.

 17.     Supply and Distribution: The existing arrangements for EPI will be used for point-to-point supply
 and distribution of COVID-19 vaccines. The EPI systems are sufficient to cover routine immunization for
 children across the country, which is different from the COVID-19 vaccination that will target adults. The
 existing PIU will develop procurement plan for both vaccine and non-vaccine items. For some of non-
 vaccine items, UN agencies such as WHO and UNICEF will be engaged. A Vaccine and Logistic Stock
 Management will be developed under the DHIS2. EPI Superintendent, Cold Chain Technician, Statistician
 and MT-EPI will need to be trained on how to use this new module.

 18.     Program Delivery: a communications strategy has been designed for community engagement and
 advocacy. The strategy specifies objective, key message and communication approaches for a given target
 audience. Different approaches for reaching out to the targeted groups for COVID-19 vaccines, in addition
 to vaccination at the walk-in sites at health facilities will be further elaborated as the NDVP is finalized.
 For the vaccination team, one female and one male vaccinator will be selected for each team and out of
 four volunteers, there will be at least one female volunteer to encourage women in accessing COVID-19
 vaccines.

 19.      A working group on regulatory and vaccine safety has been set up. Surveillance for AEFI for COVID-
 19 vaccination will comprise four components: (a) passive surveillance; (b) stimulated passive
 surveillance; (c) active surveillance and (d) active vaccine surveillance and monitoring (AVSM) by using
 pharmacoepidemiologic methods to monitor a cohort of vaccine recipients for AESIs. Such a surveillance
 portfolio has been used for the national EPI. A National AEFI Expert Review Committee has been
 established by the MoHFW for review of AEFI cases. Similar committees will be set up at the district and
 city corporation levels for field-level monitoring.

 20.      COVID-19 vaccines will be deployed in a nationwide campaign using teams made up of six people
 (two vaccinators and four volunteers) at a variety of vaccination sites (table below summarizes these).
 The line listing of the target population will be done before vaccination under the supervision of
 District/City Corporation/Municipality/Upazila COVID-19 coordination committees. The target population
 will be registered by electronic voluntary registration using national identity card/birth registration
 certificate/passport. Vaccination sites will be in an easily accessible place to ensure access by all target
 population including older people and co-morbidity target groups. The vaccination sessions will be
 organized in well-ventilated rooms with adequate seating arrangements. One volunteer will be assigned
 to take care of older people and those with co-morbidities. For storage of COVID-19 vaccines, the existing
 cold chain facilities of EPI will be used with required upgrading to ensure these are ready for COVID-19
 vaccines. The NDVP details out the various options for storage, distribution and transport of vaccines
 across the country, assuming the temperature ranges that would be required by the different vaccines.
