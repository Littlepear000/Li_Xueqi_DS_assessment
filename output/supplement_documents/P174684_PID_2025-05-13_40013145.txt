B. Introduction and Context


  1. Madagascar has been caught in a cycle of weak growth and high poverty for several decades . Low growth and
  exposure to shocks has translated into persistent high poverty, as weak growth has not produced the number and quality
  of jobs required to lift a rapidly growing population out of poverty. Stop-and-go growth episodes and high volatility,
  rooted in political instability, vulnerability to natural disasters and global shocks have also been detrimental to
  Madagascar’s overall development.

  2. Madagascar’s economy is recovering but remains uneven. Growth is estimated at 4.2 percent in 2024. Several sectors
  in the economy have yet to return to their pre-2020 output levels. The services sector has driven growth on the supply
  side, especially tourism-related sectors and telecommunications. Following reforms to improve the business climate,
  private investment has become the key engine of growth on the demand side. Stubbornly high core inflation diminished
  private consumption’s contribution to growth in 2024, and the contribution of net exports to growth turned negative.
  Meanwhile, employment-intensive sectors such as agriculture, agribusiness, and textiles have struggled to recover,
  especially in terms of value addition per capita.
  3. Private sector–led growth has demonstrated the potential of several value chains. The potential is reflected by robust
  private sector growth in key value chains including in tourism and agribusiness; by the consistently higher level of private
  investment over public investment during the last decade; and by more diversified foreign direct investment (FDI) over
  the recent past (such as in tourism). Analytical work by the World Bank (Country Economic Memorandum (2020); Country
  Private Sector Diagnostic (2021)) highlight tourism, along with the agribusiness and digital sectors, as having some of the
  best prospects for promoting private investment and job creation in the country.
  4. But those same export-led industries that were strong contributors to Madagascar’s economic growth have been
  hard hit by the COVID-19 pandemic. Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) have been particularly affected by the crisis
  in all three project sectors (tourism, agribusiness, digital). Prior to the crisis, the agriculture sector (including livestock
  and fisheries) represented 23.2 percent of national GDP and nearly 64 percent of employment, while accounting for 40
  percent of total export earnings, of which around 50 percent come from vanilla proceeds. Prior to the crisis, the tourism
  sector accounted for approximately 44,000 direct and 300,000 indirect jobs. While currently generating a revenue
  estimated at US$440 million (about 3 percent of GDP) and employing a labor force of about 22,600 people, the digital
  sector was expected to grow significantly, with the Business Process Outsourcing (BPO) sub-sector potentially employing
  up to 100,000 people by 2030.
  5. The Economic Transformation for Inclusive Growth Project (PIC3) PIC3 has supported the economic recovery from
  the COVID-19 crisis, particularly in the tourism, agribusiness and digital sectors. The project PDO is to increase the
  growth of small and medium enterprises in target regions in Madagascar. It has a regional focus on 9 regions in the north,
  east and south of Madagascar as well as a sectoral focus on tourism, agribusiness and digital. The selection of the regions
  of interventions was based on (i) alignment with government priorities; (ii) the existence of a natural extension of
  previous PIC zones and/or of key value chains; (iii) clear synergies with key World Bank or development partner funded
  infrastructure programs, and in particular road and other large infrastructure works; (iv) presence of anchor investor(s)
  and/or sizeable cluster(s) of competitive export-oriented firms in the target sectors; and (v) a strong public and private
  interest in partnering with the World Bank Group to promote private sector development, employment and shared
  prosperity.

D. Project Description

  6. The Economic Transformation for Inclusive Growth Project (PIC3) is the fourth in a series of integrated growth pole
  support projects that the World Bank has been financing since 2005. PIC 3 was approved in July 2021 and became
  effective in November 2021. The original approved financing amount was USD 150 million, and the current financing
  amount is USD 129.8 million.
  7. PIC3 is structured around mutually reinforcing components that support economic recovery after the COVID19 crisis
  and which will not be changed for the AF: (1) Attracting and Retaining Private Investment and Removing Key Constraints
  to Support Economic Recovery; (2) Removing Constraints to Private Investment and Enhancing Local Economic
  Competitiveness to Support Recovery in Target Regions and Target Sectors; (3) Supporting SMEs and Entrepreneurship
  Recovery and Growth in Target Sectors and Target Regions; (4) project implementation; and (5) A zero dollar contingent
  emergency response component (CERC).
  8. The project is progressing well with a rating of Moderately Satisfactory. The Mid Term Review (MTR), conducted in
  November 2024, found that the project has achieved a number of important results – outlined in the paragraphs below.
  The project’s work continues to be well received by beneficiaries, including citizens in the project's intervention regions,
  the private sector and public sector authorities, at both local and national levels.
  9. PIC3 is preceded by a series of Madagascar Integrated Growth Poles Projects (PIC 1, PIC2.1 and PIC2.2) that have
  contributed to the development of private sector in Madagascar in targeted regions in the tourism, agribusiness and
  digital sectors. More than $40 million of private investments were enabled, a $1 billion FDI mobilized, and more than
  47,000 formal jobs were facilitated from previous PIC activities. In terms of direct support to firms, more than 700
  startups and small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) benefited from technical assistance and financial resources to
  support their growth and expansion. More than 35,000 agricultural producers working in selected cash crops or
  aquaculture have been supported by the projects to improve their productivity and market access. The projects also
  contributed to an increase in the value of exports from the main agribusiness value chains supported (including cocoa,
  pink pepper, sea cucumber, seaweed, (cape) peas, pink pepper, vanilla, and honey). The smallholders supported by the
  projects in these value chains had a 45% increase in their revenues.

  10. PIC3 cross-cutting results: PIC3 has supported the improvement of the investment climate, particularly in the three
  key sectors. Across all components, PIC3 has so far supported the capacity-building of 38 governmental and partner
  institutions. In addition, the governance component has worked to strengthen the capacities of 162 communes in the
  PIC3 regions. PIC3 support enabled the adoption of 11 investment climate reforms, including a new investment law and
  3 decrees to ensure its implementation, 4 regional decrees to guide the operation of major agri-food sectors such as
  vanilla, cloves and pink berries, and 1 interministerial decree on tourist investment zones in national parks. PIC3
  supported the creation of 733 direct jobs, while facilitating the creation of over 10,000 indirect jobs in agribusiness and

     11. PIC3 results in the tourism sector: To date, PIC3 has supported maintenance and minor improvements, the
     preparation of development plans and improved management arrangements at nine tourism sites. At some of the sites
     supported, the project's interventions have contributed to an increase of around 130 percent in the revenue generated
     by these sites. The project has also fostered a steady increase in the number of hotel rooms available in the target regions,
     with the addition of around 4,000 rooms since 2022. Thanks to the Miatrika Training Program, over 16,000 tourism
     workers and guides have benefited from training sessions, and a database of over 300 tourism trainers has been created.
     By supporting the control and formalization function of the Ministry of Tourism and Handicrafts (MTA) at national and
     regional level, the project has contributed to a 35 percent increase in the number of formal tourism entities registered
     in the target regions. Through support for tourism promotion at national level, over 130 private operators in the tourism
     sector were able to take part in international tourism industry events, and more than 250 outbound operators took part
     in in-country educational tours. In 2024, Madagascar benefited from the addition of a new long-haul air route to the
     country, operated by Emirates. The Confédération du Tourisme de Madagascar (CTM), supported by the project,
     contributed to the dialogue and lobbying for the establishment of this new route with other stakeholders. Overall,
     Madagascar has seen a rebound in international tourist arrivals after COVID-19, with tourist arrivals in 2024 reaching the
     levels of 2019. The support provided by PIC3 to Madagascar Airlines has ensured the company's operation throughout
     2024 and its contributions to the tourism sector in particular.

     12. PIC3 results in the agribusiness sector: Project activities in the cocoa sector have consolidated the gains made in
     terms of structuring the sector through the Conseil National Cacao (CNC); the International Cocoa Organization (ICCO)
     has endorsed for the third consecutive time the 100 percent fine cocoa label for Malagasy cocoa in April 2024. The Project
     promotes the establishment of solid partnerships between public and private players; public-private dialogue platforms
     are beginning to be set up in the clove, pink pepper and essential oil sectors. These collaborations have, for example,
     helped to positively influence European regulations on nicotine residues in spices. Thanks to a stronger presence at
     international agri-food trade fairs, Malagasy SMEs have signed contracts worth US$25 million and attracted new
     investment, as witnessed by the opening of the first industrial chocolate factory in Ambanja. The value of targeted exports
     from intervention zones rose by over 50 percent. The incomes of direct beneficiary producers have risen by 45 percent
     for products targeted by the project. More than 15,000 producers benefited from capacity building and almost 2,000 of
     them were able to establish new direct commercial relationships with exporters, notably through the Madagascar
     Business Investment Facilitation (MBIF) Agribusiness Program.

    14. Challenges. During 2023-2024, the project suffered from a delay of at least one year in most of its activities,
    particularly those related to Component 3 - that provides direct support to entrepreneurs and companies. Delays were
    mainly due to country-wide budget regularization problems that delayed payments and activities. Additionally, there
    were performance issues and processing delays at the Project Implementation Unit (PIU) level. The selection process for
    the new national coordinator took a long time, and the uncertainty associated with the change of management had a
    negative impact on the motivation of PIU staff. Project management issues were related to practices of (i) staff
    recruitment and management, (ii) procurement and contract management for staff and activities, (iii) financial
    management, (iv) E&S risk management, including the operationalization of the Grievance Mechanism (GM), and (v)
    project monitoring and evaluation. The issue related to the budget regularization was addressed at the country portfolio
    level and resulted in simplification of disbursement procedures agreed with the Government. Enhanced hands-on
    support as well as a series of proactive actions, including weekly supervision of activities, have led to an increased
    implementation pace of project activities, mainly for components 1 and 2, and the disbursement rate improved from 5%
    in Dec 2023 to 64% currently. The March 2025 supervision mission concluded that significant efforts have been made to
    improve project management especially in the areas of human resources management and procurement and E&S risk
    management. A protocol for results measurements has also been finalized and a report on the functioning of the GM has
    been delivered providing information that the project has received a total of 221 complaints, 93% (205) of which are
    currently treated and closed. However, there is a need for close sensitization on GM related to the treatment of sensitive
    issues like SEAH cases.

    15. The project has faced M&E challenges, reflected by declining ratings in this area since 2023. Reporting of results
    has been inconsistent due particularly to a lack of M&E capacity in the PIU as well as the absence of a clear protocol of
    indicators. The PIU M&E specialist function has only been filled since January 2025Since the MTR and as part of the
    restructuring, the World Bank team has worked with the PIU to clarify the methodology for each indicator and finalize
    the protocol. A final version of the protocol of indicators was agreed upon in April 2025. Results for 2024 as well as the
    adjusted targets for all project indicators are based on the protocol. The World Bank team will continue to closely
    supervise the M&E function, and an M&E consultant will be added to the World Bank supervision team as of July 2025
    to ensure consistent implementation of the protocol.

Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
In accordance with the World Bank’s Environmental and Social Framework (ESF), the environmental and social risk
classification (ESRC) for the parent project and this AF remains rated as Substantial. To effectively manage the
environmental and social risks and impacts associated with the project, (08) eight relevant Environmental and Social
Standards (ESSs) have been identified and applied under both the parent project and the AF : ESS1 (Assessment and
Management of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts); ESS2 (Labor and Working Conditions); ESS3 (Resource
Efficiency and Pollution Prevention and Management); ESS4 (Community Health and Safety); ESS5 Land Acquisition,
restrictions on land use and involuntary resettlement; ESS6 (Biodiversity Conservation and Sustainable Management of
Living Natural Resources); ESS8 (Cultural Heritage); ESS10 (Stakeholder Engagement and Information Disclosure). It is
expected that the ongoing Parent Project and AF activities will have essentially positive environmental and social
impacts. Potential adverse environmental and social risks and impacts are expected to be site-specific, local, reversible,

Institutional and Implementation Arrangements
  20. The project’s institutional and implementation arrangements will remain unchanged for the Additional Financing.
  The project is implemented by a Project Implementation Unit (PIU) based in Antananarivo with 11 branches in the
  targeted regions. The PIU is responsible for overseeing the flow of project funds for each component and sub-component,
  M&E, management of the various windows of SME and Entrepreneurship support, and ensuring project reporting
  between agencies, the PIU and the Ministry in charge of Economy and Finance. The PIU reports regularly to and seeks
  guidance from the Steering Committee on strategic aspects of the project. The PIU staff in the regions, act as regional
  focal points, overseeing implementation and leading and reporting on dialogue with local stakeholders. The Analamanga
  region is covered by the central PIU.

  21. Given its multi-sectoral approach and multi-region scope, the project is led by a Project Steering Committee (PSC).
  The Ministry in charge of Economy and Finance chairs the Project Steering Committee. As per the institutional decree
  adopted in 2022, the PSC is composed of 13 Ministries including high-level representatives of relevant Ministries in charge
  of Finance; Agriculture; Tourism; Commerce; Digital Development; Spatial Planning, Public Works; Decentralization;
  Culture; Energy; Mining; Environment and Water. This high number of members reflects the large interest of the
  Government in the project but has increased the risk of political interference. The Steering Committee meets at least
  once every 12 months to perform a strategic review of Project Progress Reports, reviews yearly reports, annual work
  plans and budgets and provides oversight and support for effective project implementation, including project grants.

  22. The Project Steering Committee is supported by a Technical Committee (TC). The Technical Committee is comprised
  of focal points designated from each of the Technical Implementing Ministries and Agencies as represented in the
  project’s steering committee. The Technical Committee meets regularly with the PIU and the World Bank to review,
  discuss and address issues related to implementation progress and achievement of results. The focal points play a key
  role in ensuring coordination of project activities, including support to the process of extension of Project Grants and
  complement the role of the PIU. Communication flow between the TC and PSC was not very effective at the beginning of
  project implementation, contributing to slow implementation reflected by for example the delay in the approval of the
  Annual Work Plan and the high number of requests which did not always fall under the scope of the project. To overcome
  this, the PIU has reinforced the involvement and communication with the TC in all aspects of project implementation and
  has been more proactive in engaging with relevant Ministers when needed.

  23. The Regional Validation Committees (Comités de Validation Régionaux CVR) ensure the coherence of the
  entrepreneurship and SME program within the regional and national economic context and priorities and maximize
  transparency in the screening and selection of applications. CVRs have been generally supportive to the project, especially
  by reviewing the grants-application evaluation results submitted for their validation.
