Page 9 of 151 The World Bank Eastern Africa Regional Statistics Program-for-Results (P176371) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Regional Context 1. Sub-Saharan Africa remains the region with the largest relative and absolute number of poor, with only modest progress in the past and an uncertain outlook in the future. The poverty rate in Sub-Saharan Africa was 40.2 percent in 2018, using a poverty line of US$1.90 per day. No other region comes close to this level, with extreme poverty rates for all other regions remaining in the single digits.1 Sub-Saharan Africa is also of particular concern as the region with the highest absolute number of poor people at 433.4 million. Growth is a major driver of poverty reduction, and throughout the 1990s and early 2000s many poor managed to escape poverty thanks to relatively high growth rates. The Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita growth in Sub-Saharan Africa has been weak and erratic, averaging around 2 percent and has been falling in recent years. 2. Challenges to reduce poverty are manifold in the region, exacerbated by COVID-19 and conflict. Demographic and social factors like high fertility rates and limited investments in human capital make poverty reduction particularly challenging in Sub-Saharan Africa. High fertility rates perpetuate cycles of poverty by keeping dependency ratios high and limiting female participation in the workforce.2 Inequality remains high in the region, and poverty is often concentrated among specific groups with multiple overlapping social and economic constraints. While jobs and entrepreneurship can provide a sustainable pathway out of poverty, markets are often small or hard to reach making it difficult for individuals to take advantage of potential opportunities. Many of the countries in the region are experiencing challenges related to climate change, including unpredictable rainfall, rising temperatures, and sea levels, and an increase in extreme weather events, which are expected to worsen in the future. The economic and public health impacts of the COVID-19 global pandemic have caused a contraction in global per capita GDP growth of 3.4 percent in 2020, leading to the first increase in global poverty since the 1998 Asian financial crisis.3 Between 26 million and 40 million additional people are pushed into extreme poverty in the Sub-Saharan Africa. 3. Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania are key countries for Sub-Saharan Africa’s poverty reduction agenda, accounting for 53 million of the region’s poor population of 433 million. Poverty rates in all these economies are high. Using the poverty line of $US1.90 per day, poverty rates in Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania are 37.1 percent, 56.5 percent, and 49.4 percent respectively.4 Of this trio, the countries with lower poverty rates have very large populations, meaning that all three have a large poor population. The absolute numbers of poor in Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania are 18.2 million, 6.8 million, and 27.8 million respectively. This represents more than a tenth of poor people in Sub-Saharan Africa. 4. Regional integration is key to accelerate inclusive growth and poverty reduction. Countries with access to larger, regional markets provide more opportunities and are more attractive to both foreign and domestic investors. Firms can reach full potential of their comparative advantages leading to specialization and gains in productivity.5 In fact, higher intra-regional trade leads to higher growth through diffusion of technology, managerial know-how, and competitive 1 World Bank. 2020. While Sub-Saharan Africa has made recent gains in economic integration, its pace is limited with only few firms taking advantage of regional and global value chains. Sub-Saharan Africa’s intraregional trade has doubled since 2000 accounting for almost a quarter of all exports in 2018. The African Union launched the African Continental Free Trade Area to establish a single market for goods and services across the continent, allowing the free movement of business travelers and investments, and creating a continental customs union to streamline trade and attract long-term investment. Regional economic blocks like the East African Community (EAC) work towards deeper integration. However, the pace of integration has lagged that of East Asia and the European Union (EU) while Sub-Saharan African countries remain significantly under- represented in regional and global value chains. Weak implementation of continental and regional decisions at the national level due to a lack of evidence and knowledge about benefits and costs of regional integration have contributed to this. 6. Absence of harmonized data limits the ability of governments and businesses to make strategic decisions and undermines the goal of regional integration. Harmonized data is needed to inform decision makers about progress as well as the benefits and costs of regional integration, further advancing the regional integration agenda, while providing evidence for monitoring and policy-making at the regional and national level.8 Good statistics improve governance by increasing transparency and accountability. Increased availability of comparable trade statistics is essential for discussions on establishing a Common External Tariff, while more comparable data on inflation is important for adopting the right policies for convergence towards a monetary union. Firms benefit from regionally harmonized and transparent data by gaining a better unbiased understanding of regional markets and their opportunities. Individuals benefit from more and better opportunities as well as improved policies ranging from labor policies over education to health policies. Specific groups and communities affected by regional or national challenges can be better protected by targeted social safety net measures. However, such data is currently lacking, limiting further regional integration. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 7. Sub-Saharan Africa made substantial progress in the production of statistics over the last 20 years. From the early 2000s, strategic planning through National Strategies for the Development of Statistics (NSDS) has been adopted, with most African countries having implemented one or more strategies by 2021.9 These have helped countries improve their legal framework with new statistical laws and strengthen the status of their National Statistical Offices (NSOs). Several countries use the 2008 System of National Accounts (SNA). The geographic coverage and timeliness of the Consumer Price Index (CPI) has also improved. External trade statistics are harmonized in many countries, and most of the countries use the same software, EUROTRACE. The number of household surveys that collect data on monetary and nonmonetary dimensions of poverty has increased. This is based on the World Bank’s support as well as donor-funded programs such as the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and the Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (MICS). 8. Data is often not available or if available, not harmonized across countries limiting its use and relevance for policy making. Policy making and monitoring of national and regional development plans as well as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) require a minimum list of available and harmonized data. Under IDA19, the World Bank committed to a Data for Policy10 (D4P) initiative to improve availability of a core set of statistics. This core set of statistics includes household-based surveys and population censuses, firm-based surveys and registries, agricultural statistics, price data, and administrative data as well as national accounts and big data. The D4P package is aligned with the SHaSA2 initiative in aiming to help countries producing data for policy making, but also to improve the quality of statistics through international best practices and standards as well as harmonization. In sub-Saharan Africa, only half of the internationally recommended data are produced with sufficient frequency (D4P index of 53 percent in 2019).11 As the World Development Report 2021 points out, data generated and managed by governments based on transparency and equity principles are key to establish a social contract for data where all stakeholders including civil society, the private sector and academia both contribute to and benefit from a data system generated by all stakeholders under rules that guarantee people’s protection. 10. Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania are no exceptions. Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania reach only 58.5 of the Statistical Capacity Index (SCI) with data availability measured by the D4P index at 75 percent. They have Statistics Acts granting various degrees of autonomy to the NSO, often combined with a mandate to coordinate the National Statistical System (NSS). Realizing the mandate remains weak especially in the context of coordinating the NSS and using administrative data. Funding for statistics remains inconsistent despite some improvement in the past. A regional approach to statistics is needed to produce and disseminate better harmonized data at the national, regional and international level. A regional approach will close the gap between regional guidance on harmonization and national production of statistics. Only a regional approach involving regional organizations and countries will be able to meaningfully advance the production of harmonized statistics by funding NSOs’ data production for harmonization of statistics. The implementation of the D4P package at a regional level will close data gaps with harmonized data compliant with international standards contributing to effective policy making and monitoring of progress, regional integration and achievement of global targets by improving quality and availability of internationally comparable gender and disability statistics. A regional approach will facilitate coordination among NSOs by introducing and expanding innovations, peer- to-peer reviewing, and learning that can generate positive externalities. Prevention, mitigation and recovery of regional and global crises like COVID-19 also require a regional concerted approach to setup emergency observatories establishing rapid data collection capacity and big data capabilities. 12. Using a modular approach embedded in a World Bank (WB) framework for statistical capacity building in Africa, the operation will include Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania taking advantage of similarities and complementarity at the regional and national level. Funding for the implementation of a harmonized minimum data package for Africa in WB’s Regional Integration program is expected to reach nearly US$1 billion in the next couple of years. However, African countries are very diverse, e.g., in location, geography, language, level of development, economic integration and fragility. Hence, no one-size-fits-all regional operation would be able to create meaningful progress towards improved harmonization of high-quality and timely statistics. Instead, multiple regional operations are contributing to the overall agenda.12 Kenya, Rwanda and Tanzania are ideal candidates for a regional operation since they are neighbors as well as peers pursuing further economic integration while having a commitment to a regionally harmonized approach to statistics with dissemination in the public domain.13 The countries will be supported by the African Union Institute of Statistics (STATAFRIC) and at the sub-regional level, the Statistics Department of the East African Community (EAC) Secretariat to improve harmonization, quality and dissemination of socio-economic statistics. The PforR instrument has distinct advantages for effective and sustainable implementation of national programs, which are essential to reach the regional objective. The PforR instrument, especially compared to the use of an IPF with Performance-Based-Conditions (PBCs), has several advantages to support NSOs in implementing the national programs to reach the regional objective sustainably: (i) implementation is facilitated by working through government budget, financial management, and procurement systems; (ii) working through national programs for statistics improves the efficiency of expenditures and can be a key factor to institutionalize government support for statistics; (iii) the Program can build institutional capacity to administer and execute the NSOs programs; (iv) due to the direct relationship between the Program results and the disbursements, the PforR instrument allows for a direct incentive-driven approach to achieve the Program Development Objective (PDO); (v) scalable disbursement-linked indicators incentivize NSOs to release data in a timely manner; (vi) it facilitates potential scalability of the Program by drawing in additional stakeholders and being better suited to draw in financing from multiple development partners supporting the Program; and (vii) the Program allows for shifting the focus of World Bank’s engagement from upstream technical advice and financing of inputs to supporting specific and verifiable improvements of the statistical system. 19. Theory of Change 26. The Operation will help to overcome the main challenges for better regional harmonization, dissemination and use of statistics to improve regional integration for economic prosperity. The ability to monitor progress of regional development is limited by data gaps, non-comparability of economic and social statistics, weak financial capacity, low human resources and limited dissemination of data and statistics. These factors also constrain monitoring of national development, exacerbated by limitations in the availability of data using out-of-date methodologies and weak integration of data (e.g., administrative data). A lack of necessary technological, physical and statistical infrastructure aggravates the situation. The Operation will eliminate these challPfenges by (a) improving harmonization, quality, dissemination and use, (b) improving availability of data and statistics as well as tapping into new data sources, and (c) enhancing infrastructure and institutional development. Page 17 of 151 The World Bank Eastern Africa Regional Statistics Program-for-Results (P176371) 27. The Operation will utilize strong country buy-in for harmonizing statistics combined with regional capacity building to allow NSOs to apply harmonization guidelines for data production while fostering demand for harmonized statistics. STATAFRIC and the EAC Secretariat have the mandate to develop harmonization guidelines in coordination with member countries but most data are produced by NSOs. NSOs and their respective countries have participated in the discussion and approved harmonization guidelines, signaling their strong ownership. However, NSOs face challenges in terms of capacity and competing demands and priorities so that their implementation is often postponed. The Operation will build the required capacity with a focus on regional learning and will link the adoption to funding for the production of data while fostering demand for harmonized statistics within countries and across the region. 28. The Operation will harmonize and disseminate high-quality statistics, while improving the infrastructure and institutional setup of regional and national statistical systems. The regional statistical organizations will produce regional manuals and enhance guidelines for higher quality and harmonized statistics, including a compliance framework to measure where each country stands in terms of compliance with each of the guidelines expressed as a compliance score. The guidelines, including the compliance frameworks, are being discussed within specialized technical working groups with NSOs, and are typically adopted by consensus. The compliance frameworks (scores) will be used by this Operation to assess progress towards regional harmonization. Training and peer-to-peer learning will build more capacity at beneficiary NSOs, as well as at STATAFRIC and the EAC Secretariat, to implement the manuals and guidelines. With Program funding for the production of statistics, the existing guidelines together with the increased human capacity will help to make available high-quality and harmonized statistics. This will be supported by upgraded IT and statistical infrastructure as well as an improved institutional setup. 29. Harmonized and timely disseminated statistics in a user-friendly format combined with advocacy and improved statistical literacy will lead to enhanced use of statistics, enhancing the sustainability of the development objectives. Policy analysis and use of statistics for evidence-based planning are beyond the mandate of NSOs. However, as one of the main data producers, NSOs are instrumental to the construction of a national integrated data system by disseminating data in a timely and user-friendly manner as well as advocating for the use of statistics and building statistical literacy beyond the NSO and NSS. Consistent with the supported national programs for statistics, the Operation includes incentives in the form of Disbursement-Linked Indicators (DLI) milestones for timely dissemination of statistics, e.g., according to advanced release calendars, in a user-friendly format, e.g., through setup of one-stop-shop data portals and translation into local languages. Similarly, DLI milestones incentivize advocacy and statistical literacy, e.g., outreach programs, communication strategies, infographics competitions and literacy training. This will encourage better use of statistics, which will be measured with a PDO-level results indicator tracking the proportion of strategy and policy documents using statistics. The increased use of statistics will, in turn lead to demands for more statistics creating a positive feedback loop enhancing the sustainability of the Operation’s objectives and impacts. Technical (including Program economic evaluation) Strategic Relevance 58. The Program activities are strategically relevant and timely. The economic, social, and policy trends in recent years in the three countries covered in this Program highlight the relevance of investing in a solid and reliable statistical system capable of producing periodic, timely, and high-quality data, in addition to the position these countries are in to make such an investment in their own statistical capacity. As current or aspiring middle-income economies, these countries recognize the economic returns and timing for a big-push investment in statistical capacity and the opportunity to produce harmonized data that can be readily compared across the region to monitor results and increase accountability. Moreover, transparency is gaining importance to facilitate access to international capital markets and reduce borrowing costs. Both at the continent and regional level, there is awareness of the necessity to produce a wide range of comparable, accurate and timely statistical information for policymakers and the public, as is apparent in the Second Strategy for the Harmonization of Statistics (SHaSA2, 2017-2026), which addresses constraints in statistical production, harmonization and availability to promote the regional integration agenda. Technical Soundness 59. The Program takes a regional approach to address the gaps between regional requirements and aspirations on harmonization and national production of statistics. The African Union’s and EAC’s overarching strategies for Page 30 of 151 The World Bank Eastern Africa Regional Statistics Program-for-Results (P176371) harmonization of statistics provide a framework and guidance. However, the ultimate results rely on the ability of the member country NSOs to produce the necessary data in a harmonized and timely manner while meeting a minimum quality standard. A regional approach will facilitate strengthening the technical capacity of regional bodies while lifting production and dissemination obstacles at the country level. Progress on harmonization of statistics will be assessed according to compliance frameworks for each of the guidelines. 60. The Program will contribute to the enhanced use of harmonized statistics to deepen regional integration and accelerate growth and poverty reduction in the region. At the regional level, activities will center on improving the comparability of existing statistics and developing further harmonization standards and protocols for member country NSOs. With the production of more harmonized statistics, the evidence-base at the national and regional level will be expanded to inform policy makers’ decisions and increase their accountability. Better information for firms is also critical to increase investment demand and promote productivity growth. To reduce poverty and inequality, social and economic statistics are crucial inputs to the design of efficient and sustainable social policies. A regional Program will also facilitate cross-fertilization, create economies of scale as training can be conducted more efficiently in regional centers of excellence and regional schools, reducing training costs substantially. This will also expand the pool of skills in the region as more statisticians receive training on common standards, methods, and tools. 61. The Program will strengthen the technical capacity and institutional quality of regional and national statistical systems while supporting the production, harmonization, and timely dissemination of statistics at the national level. The Program will create incentives for STATAFRIC, the EAC Statistics Bureau and the beneficiary NSOs to apply harmonization guidelines, and the respective compliance frameworks, for data production to disseminate better and more timely statistics. Training and peer-to-peer learning will build more capacity at STATAFRIC and the EAC Statistics Bureau as well as beneficiary NSOs. The Program will also support investment in human capacity (staffing, training) and in physical infrastructure, such as upgraded IT systems. Finally, the Program will support an improved institutional setup, for example establishing advanced release calendars for D4P statistics to ensure timeliness and increase the demand and use of statistical information. 62. To ensure the Program is technically sound, activities will follow international best practice and established principles as well as regional harmonization standards and compliance frameworks. More specifically, the Program’s activities will be guided by the World Bank’s Statistical Performance Indicators (SPIs) and Data-for-Policy (D4P) scores. The World Bank’s SPI is a composite score assessing the performance of a country’s national statistical system on three dimensions: statistical methodology, source data, and periodicity and timeliness. Increasing the SPI and D4P scores are explicit goals of the Program. Technical quality and comparability of statistics will be guaranteed through adherence to international best practices and regional harmonization standards and compliance frameworks, as stated in the SHaSA2. Technical Risk Rating 66. There are diverse risks that could affect the implementation in each country. Public investment in statistics has a strong economic rationale. Regional integration as well as the process of planning, policy-making, and monitoring and evaluation of regional and national policies require a wide variety of development, social, and economic statistics, which only the public sector has an incentive to provide. While the private sector can and does collect specific statistics, they are unlikely to invest in a complete statistical system. Left to the market there would likely be significant underinvestment in statistics, hereby foregoing the benefits of better statistics. In addition, statistics are a public good. Once produced, statistics can be used by different levels of regional organizations, governments, citizens, and businesses to facilitate evidence-based decisions in their spheres of interest. Public investment in statistics thus creates positive externalities. B. Fiduciary PforR 68. However, the capacity to manage e-waste in participating countries are weak and needs strengthening. Broadly, drawing on experience and lessons from the implementation of environmental and social risk management systems for other ongoing or completed World Bank-funded PforRs in host countries, and the ESSA prepared for this Program, the host countries’ regulatory systems show that social risk management systems are not as well developed as the environmental management systems. The overall risk of the Program is rated as Moderate. Environmental risks of the Program activities shall be confined to small rehabilitation or new construction initiatives for branch offices, installing and or upgrading data servers, and possibly supply of electronic equipment for field data collection. The most adverse impacts are limited to the disposal of e-waste from the Program. These risk and impacts are site specific in nature and well known to be mitigable by enforcement of good environmental and social systems. Potential negative social impacts of the Program include but are not limited to (i) safety and misuse of data collected, as Vulnerable groups may be placed at risk if data is collected or shared inappropriately, (ii) Lack of transparency and trust by local communities due to poor consultations and stakeholder engagement with project beneficiaries, vulnerable groups, and marginalized communities/ Traditional Local Communities and (iii) politicization of decision making due to data collected. Because of geographical scope of the Program in various countries, the weak legislations in management of electronic waste within the participating countries, and different scales of proposed investments, and the capacity of the PIUs and other institutions to manage environmental and social risks, the environmental and social risk is rated moderate. 82. The Program will incorporate measures to address environmental and social risks in the Program design, enhanced by integration into the overall Program Action Plan and legally incorporated into the financing agreements of the Program. The action plans for the Program are grouped into three areas. First, are actions to strengthen the environmental and social management systems (ESMS), through screening of projects, and preparation of the Impact Assessment as per the host countries’ national requirements, excluding certain categories of projects that are high risk and generate significant negative impacts that are sensitive, diverse, irreversible, or unprecedented on the environment and/or affected people and developing a set of principles that will act as measures to minimize sub-project risks at the concept level. Second are actions to strengthen implementation and monitoring of the environmental and social management of sub-projects by developing ESMS procedures outlined in the Program Operation Manual (POM), incorporating contractual responsibilities and obligations with appropriate mechanisms for addressing non-compliance of environmental, health, and safety issues of the sub-projects (by the subcontractors/suppliers and the client) and the E-Waste Management Plan as part of the ESMS. Third is strengthening capacity of Implementing Agencies to enhance environment and social management performance of the Program by providing targeted capacity building and requiring the PIUs to have a safeguard specialist. Along with increasing debt levels over the last decade across the continent, also the beneficiary countries have accumulated substantial amounts of debt. COVID-19 led to an economic slow-down depriving the Government of fiscal revenues. At the same time, emergency expenditures were necessary to mitigate economic pressures as well as preparing the health system to deal with the crisis. Thus, the fiscal space of the beneficiary countries is narrow. In addition, the further evolution of COVID-19 in these countries and its socio-economic impact is uncertain. Given the mitigation measures already in place, the risk is assessed as substantial that the macro-economic situation deteriorates. 90. The fiduciary risk rating is assessed as substantial. The Program involves four country-level institutions (two of them in Tanzania) and 2 regional-level organizations. Given the involvement of these multiple agencies, it is expected that the coordination and streamlining of the fiduciary arrangements and requirements will involve extensive discussions and dialogue. Even though the national level institutions have experience in working with World Bank financed support in the past, this Program is unique and is likely to bring with it other fiduciary risks and challenges, such as assigning adequate and qualified fiduciary staff by the various agencies for this Program, coordination of fiduciary reporting requirements, timely fund flows, delays in procurement processing, and auditing arrangements. To minimize and mitigate against some of these challenges and risks, clear procedures will be put in place for each identified risk area. These risks will be mitigated by analysis and recommendations to revise the relevant legal frameworks (results area 3), depending on the country context, and by actions to improve data security and usage included in the Program Action Plan. 92. The Program activities are strategically relevant and come at the right time. The economic, social, and policy trends in recent years in the three countries covered in this project highlight the relevance of investing in a solid and reliable statistical system capable of producing periodic, timely, and high-quality data, in addition to the position these countries are in to make such an investment in their own statistical capacity. As current or aspiring middle-income economies, these countries recognize the economic returns and timing for a big-push investment in statistical capacity and the opportunity to produce harmonized data that can be readily compared across the region to monitor results and increase accountability. Moreover, transparency is gaining importance to facilitate access to international capital markets and reduce borrowing costs. 2. Regional integration has been a policy priority for African countries since their start as independent states. In the early 1960s, with the creation of the Organization of African Unity (OAU), countries sought to establish pan-national institutions to promote a development model based on sovereignty, unity, and solidarity amongst African States, and to better coordinate economic, security, and international relations policies. In September 1999, African leaders recognized the need for regional efforts to focus on economic cooperation and integration to enhance growth and economic development. In that goal, the African Union (AU) was officially launched in July 2002, and today it counts 55 member countries. Two important mandates of the AU are to coordinate and harmonize policies, and to develop and promote common policies on trade, among others. Likewise, countries have launched several initiatives to facilitate the implementation of the African integration agenda, such as the New Partnership for Africa’s Development (NEPAD), the Comprehensive Africa Agriculture Development Program (CAADP), the African Solidarity Initiative (ASI) and the Program for Infrastructure Development in Africa (PIDA), among others. Within Africa, several regional bodies have been established to strengthen the economic integration of neighboring countries, each one with differing roles and structures. Generally, the purpose of the Regional Economic communities (RECs) is to facilitate regional economic integration between members of the individual regions and through the wider African Economic Community (AEC). Today, the AU recognizes eight regional economic communities, including the East African Community (EAC).21 3. Both at the continent and regional level, there is awareness of the necessity to produce a wide range of comparable, accurate and timely statistical information for policymakers and the public. Policymakers recognize that for the many initiatives that have been implemented to unite the continent and provide better living conditions for people, their impact could not be established due to lack of reliable and harmonized statistics. Indeed, building statistical capacity has featured in various continental development and integration initiatives. Already in 1990, Conference of African Ministers responsible for development adopted the Addis Ababa Plan of Action for Statistical Development. More recently, the Cape Town Global Action Plan for Sustainable Development Data (CTGAP), the Strategy for the Harmonization of Statistics (SHaSA), and Regional Statistical Development Strategy (RSDS) have provided the strategic support to the development of National Strategy for the Development of Statistics (NSDS). It covers the production of better-quality and harmonized statistics, improved cooperation and coordination between African statistical institutions, building sustainable institutional capacity and enhancing use of statistics by promoting a culture of quality decision making. 4. STATAFRIC, the African Union Institute for Statistics was created in 2013 to coordinate the production of quality statistics for Africa. It is underpinned by the African Charter on Statistics and the UN Fundamental Principles on Official Statistics. It is tasked with creating frameworks to harmonize and improve the quality of statistics in Africa, based on some of the strategic documents mentioned earlier. It has prepared frameworks and technical guidelines for improving harmonization and quality of statistics on various dimensions in the African Statistical System, including poverty, process, administrative data use, big data and new technologies, vital statistics, education, health, and most recently on statistical governance. It is also responsible for preparing terms of reference for Specialized Technical Groups (comprised of national statistical agencies’ delegates, as well as thematic experts) in different statistical areas. STATAFRIC is also in charge of supporting the design of Regional Statistical Development Strategies for several sub-regional groups. Finally, it provides technical support to individual NSOs to design their National Statistical Development Strategies. Importantly, STATAFRIC has developed strategic partnerships with regional and external institutions, including the African Union, AfDB, UNECA, and the African Capacity Building Foundation, the EU (EUROSTAT), World Bank, Paris21, IOM, ILO, UNSD, and National Statistical Offices from China, France, Sweden and Norway. 5. At the sub-region level, the East African Community (EAC) also points to accurate, reliable, timely, and comparable statistics as key to guide regional integration and development processes . Within EAC, the building blocks towards economic, social and political integration, namely the Customs Union, Common Market, Monetary, and Political Federation all rely on having multiple common indicators to track progress and highlight priorities. In this regard, EAC has formulated a Statistics Policy, established a Sectoral Committee on Statistics, defined an EAC Statistical program and passed a Statistics Bill to create the EAC Statistics Bureau as an autonomous institution of the EAC.22 Its work is guided by the RSDP II and SHaSA2, as well as the CTGAP and member countries’ NSDS. The EAC has also generated guidelines on compilation of Harmonized Consumer Price Indices, Government Finance Statistics, and Public Sector Debt Statistics. 6. The Program will support the regional and country efforts to improve data harmonization, production, dissemination to improve regional integration for economic prosperity. The ability to monitor progress of regional development is limited at the national and regional levels by data gaps, non-comparability of economic and social statistics, weak financial capacity, low human resources and limited dissemination of data and statistics. Both for STATAFRIC as well as within EAC, data challenges include institutional, organizational, infrastructural, methodological, capacity, and financial gaps. Data coming from member countries has reliability, timeliness, and accompanying metadata issues. While Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania perform above the African average in terms of basic statistical production, important data gaps also remain in these countries. In addition to the lack of harmonization, main data gaps for the three countries are economic censuses as well as agricultural statistics. Technical Soundness 7. The Operation takes a regional approach to address the gaps between regional requirements and aspirations on harmonization and national production of statistics. The African Union’s and EAC overarching strategies for harmonization of statistics provide a framework and guidance; however, the ultimate results rely on the ability of the member country NSOs to produce the necessary information in a timely manner and meeting a minimum quality standard. A regional approach will facilitate the resolution of production obstacles at the country level, while strengthening the technical capacity of regional bodies. The IPF components with the AU and EAC will offer technical assistance and facilitate peer to peer learning on common needs and areas related to data harmonization and data comparability, while other country specific needs can be addressed through individual consultations and country specific twinning arrangements with NSO from advanced countries. 8. The Program will contribute to the enhanced use of harmonized statistics to deepen regional integration and accelerate growth and poverty reduction in the region. At the regional level, activities will center on improving the comparability of existing statistics and developing further harmonization standards and protocols for member country NSOs. With the production of more harmonized statistics, the evidence-base at the national and regional level will be expanded to inform policy makers decisions and increase their accountability. Better information for firms is also critical to increase investment demand and promote productivity growth. To reduce poverty and inequality, social and economic statistics are crucial inputs to the design of efficient and sustainable social policies. A regional program will also facilitate cross-fertilization, create economies of scale as training can be conducted more efficiently in regional centers of excellence and regional schools, reducing training costs substantially. This will also expand the pool of skills in the region as more statisticians receive training on common standards, methods, and tools. 9. The Program will strengthen the technical capacity and institutional quality of regional and national statistical systems while supporting the production, harmonization, and timely dissemination of statistics at the national level . The Program will create incentives for STATAFRIC, the EAC Statistics Bureau and the beneficiary NSOs to apply harmonization guidelines for data production to disseminate better and more timely statistics. Training and peer-to-peer learning will build more capacity at STATAFRIC and the EAC Statistics Bureau as well as beneficiary NSOs. The Program will support the production of harmonized statistics complemented with investments in human capacity and in physical infrastructure, such as upgraded IT systems. Finally, the Program will support an improved institutional setup, for example establishing advanced release calendars for D4P statistics to ensure timeliness and increase the demand and use of statistical information. 10. To ensure the Program is technically sound, activities will follow international best practice and established principles as well as regional harmonization standards. More specifically, the Program’s activities will be guided by the World Bank’s Statistical Performance Indicators (SPIs) and Data-for-Policy (D4P) scores. The World Bank’s SPI is a composite score assessing the performance of a country’s national statistical system on three dimensions: statistical methodology, source data, and periodicity and timeliness. Increasing the SPI and D4P scores are explicit goals of the Program. Technical quality and comparability of statistics will be guaranteed through adherence to international best practices and regional harmonization standards, as stated in the SHaSA2. In Kenya, the Government has shown significant commitment to invest in the production of statistics, evidenced by the successful implementation of the Kenya Statistics Program-for-Results (KSPforR).23 Kenya’s 2010 Constitution defines statistics as a shared function between the National Government and County Governments. The Fourth Schedule of the Constitution identifies national statistics and data on population, the economy, and society as a primary function of National Government. County Governments are mandated to carry out county planning and development including statistics in their respective counties. This constitutional provision stipulates that the Kenya National Bureau of Statistics (KNBS) is the agency responsible for developing statistics, coordinating the National Statistical System (NSS), and setting standards for official government statistics at both national and county levels. Within this mandate, the KSPforR supported improvements in data production and strengthening KNBS’ capacity. Among other results, surveys funded by the KSPforR have been used by the National Government to monitor and report progress towards meeting targets in national development plans, and by lawmakers in assessing the performance of the economy during the budget-making process. 12. The Program will selectively support activities from the Kenyan Strategy for the Development of Statistics 2019/20 – 2022/23 that are aligned with SHaSA2 themes. The Kenyan Strategy for the Development of Statistics supports four strategic areas: strengthening the national statistical system; improving data quality; ensuring adequate infrastructure for statistics; and enhancing statistical advocacy. The program will support a subset of activities under the first three of these strategic areas. The supported activities will leverage the experience KNBS gained in implementing the KSPforR, complement the support provided by other donors, and focus program support to areas key for achieving the outcomes of harmonization, quality production, timeliness, and usage of data and statistics. As a result of the supported activities, Kenya will have regularly produced, harmonized and high-quality data and statistics in the areas of poverty monitoring, labor market indicators, sectoral statistics, price and macroeconomic statistics. The production of some of these statistics will leverage big data, geospatial data, and apply machine learning techniques. The data and statistics will be disseminated via the Program’s support for the harmonized and streamlined data dissemination portals, which are user-friendly and interactive. Finally, the program will support Kenya’s efforts for county-level development planning and address the increased demand for county-level economic statistics. The program will support the preparation and dissemination of Gross County Product reports covering economic size of counties, sectoral structure, and growth rates. 13. The Government of Rwanda recognizes the importance of timely, reliable and good quality official statistics that are used for evidence-based decision making and planning, and to monitor national, regional and global development programs such as the first National Strategy for Transformation (NST1), the Rwanda Vision 2050, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). In this context, the National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda (NISR) was created in 2005 with a clear mandate of producing and disseminating official statistics through surveys and censuses. In addition, NISR was given the mandate to coordinate the National Statistical System (NSS) to ensure that all statistics requests are fulfilled from different data sources. The Government of Rwanda through NISR has increased its investments to increase the frequency of key statistics, including monthly CPI and PPI, quarterly GDP and labor force statistics, seasonal agriculture survey, annual business statistics, and establishment census and the household living conditions survey every 3 years. A reliable but flexible verification process will ensure that the Program delivers on agreed results. While the results matrix is measured at the Program-level, DLIs are defined for specific countries simplifying the verification procedure while avoiding inter-dependency of disbursements. All DLIs towards a result will be verified by a verification agency, which are selected based on reputation and relevant expertise. With the initiation of the Program, the two international verification agencies –UNECA African Centre for Statistics (ACS) and DataFirst at the University of Cape Town (UCT)– will be contracted through the IPF component by the EAC to carry out verification of DLRs under results areas 1 and 2. The verification process will require the implementing agency to request initiation of verification by the RPCU. The implementing agency will provide all required documents and give access to datasets/portal as per verification requirements outlined in Annex 2 of this document. The RPCU will submit within 5 working days the request to the corresponding verification agency, which will verify results within 4 weeks of receiving the request. Once a verification agency receives a request for verification, it will communicate directly with the implementing agency in case additional information is required and to make the necessary arrangements to proceed with the verification. The verification agency will share the verification results simultaneously with the implementing agency, the RPCU and the World Bank. Upon satisfactory verification, disbursement can be requested. In case of delays exceeding 2 months –provided the implementing agency has promptly availed all required documentation, access, and additional information requested – the implementing agency can request verification directly from an alternative organization with relevant expertise after Page 118 of 151 The World Bank Eastern Africa Regional Statistics Program-for-Results (P176371) approval by the World Bank. The costs of this alternative arrangement, if any, would be covered by the requesting implementing agency. To minimize delays in verification: (i) the procurement of verification agencies by the EAC is eligible for retroactive financing to allow the EAC to hire the verification agencies before Program effectiveness; (ii) terms of reference for the verification agencies, including a detailed description of verification criteria (based on the verification procedures described in Annex 2 of this document), will require no-objection from the PMU and the World Bank; and (iii) contracting of verification agencies can be completed before Program effectiveness based on agreed terms of reference. The verification agencies will be fully independent in the verification process. The EAC will only manage the procurement and contract management of these agencies and transmit the requests for verification as per agreed timelines. 35. Verification of DLRs under results area 3 will be the responsibility of the implementing agencies. Depending on the nature of specific DLRs, public agencies or private firms can be commissioned, with possession of relevant expertise and high credibility as the key selection criteria. Public investment in statistics has a strong economic rationale. Regional integration as well as the process of planning, policy-making, and monitoring and evaluation of regional and national policies require a wide variety of development, social, and economic statistics, which only the public sector has an incentive to provide. While the private sector can and does collect specific statistics, they are unlikely to invest in a complete statistical system. Left to the market there would likely be significant underinvestment in statistics, hereby foregoing the benefits of better statistics. In addition, statistics are a public good. Once produced, statistics can be used by different levels of regional organizations, governments, citizens, and businesses to facilitate evidence-based decisions in their spheres of interest. Public investment in statistics thus creates positive externalities. Page 120 of 151 The World Bank Eastern Africa Regional Statistics Program-for-Results (P176371) ANNEX 4. The Environment and Social Systems Assessment (ESSA) for the East Africa Regional Statistics Program (EARSP) has been undertaken to ensure consistency with six “core principles,”32 outlined in the World Bank’s Operational Policy and Directive – Program-for-Results Financing. The ESSA examines host countries’ existing legal, regulatory, and institutional framework guiding the Program’s environmental and social management systems and defines measures to strengthen the systems and integrates these measures into the overall Program. The key findings of ESSA on environmental and social systems are: 2. The participating countries have well developed and robust legislation, regulations, and systems to manage environmental, health, and safety risks. The national Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) systems have well-defined guidelines covering project registration and screening, Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) process (scoping, alternative analysis, impact assessment, mitigation measures, management plan and consultation), monitoring and auditing, and decommissioning. However, there are no equivalent legislation or systems to manage distinctly social risks. The countries do not have well developed and defined Social Risk Management (SRM) System – there are different organizations handling social issues with poor coordination mechanisms. 3. The assessment identified the weakness in supervision/monitoring and poor enforcement which should be strengthened to address the potential environmental and social challenges that might be encountered during the implementation of the Program. The implementing units will need to be strengthened to manage potential environmental and social risks along with a framework for monitoring the same. The Program's implementing units at Implementing Agencies do not have Environment and Social specialists with experience in PforR operations. The capacity to manage e-waste in participating countries remain weak, leading to poor management of e-waste, including poor enforcement by the authorities. The host countries have some few private sector players in e-waste management, with limited capacity to handle all the e-waste generated. Kenya and Rwanda have Data protection laws, but Tanzania is in the process of drafting such legislations. There is weak annual environment and social performance verification audit procedures for minimum conditions related to environmental and social safeguards under existing PforR Programs within the participating countries. The annual audit for the Program needs to be well developed to ensure E&S risks are captured well in the audit process. 4. The Program will have mostly positive social benefits. The Program interventions will set up a robust statistical system and infrastructure in the participating countries which will result in, among others, regional harmonization, quality control and dissemination of correct statistical data for policy and development. The Program will increase effectiveness and efficiency of regional governments, strengthening transparency and accountability by improving access to information through the use of information technology, for improved use of development data for decision-making. The investment menu options under the operation are likely to have low to moderate environmental and social impacts, which vary depending on the context, countries priorities, and investment choices of each Country. However, the Program will exclude high risks projects (projects that have significant and high negative environmental and social impacts that are sensitive, diverse, irreversible, or unprecedented). There are potential adverse environmental impacts from the Program but the majority of adverse potential impacts can be prevented, are reversible, and can be mitigated with standard operational procedures and good construction management practices. The potential impacts include (i) improper and indiscriminate disposal of E-waste by the organizations may result to soil and water contamination through the release of heavy metals (lead, arsenic, and cadmium, (ii) air pollution by release of hydrocarbons in the atmosphere through melting of materials if not properly managed, (iii) transformer and backup generator equipment may potentially contain used oils, Polychlorinated Biphenyls (PCBs) which can pollute water and soils, while cooling equipment may contain refrigerants (potential Ozone Depleting Substances [ODSs]), (iv) water, and soil pollution from the accidental spillage of fuels or other materials associated with construction works and (v) Environment Risks occupational health and safety incidents through injuries or accidents to the workers at construction sites and/or managing/maintaining IT equipment and infrastructure. To mitigate these risks, all project investments under the Program will be subjected to screening, and preparation of the Impact Assessment as per the host countries’ national requirements. The screening procedure will be done during the sub-project appraisal and will be guided by the Host country’s Guidelines which will be outlined in detail in the Environment and Social Risk Management Manual and highlighted in the Project Operations Manual (POM). While no high-risk projects are expected under the Program, the screening process will include criteria to exclude projects that are high risks and have significant negative impacts that are sensitive, diverse, irreversible, or unprecedented on the environment and/or affected people . The subprojects are expected to be site specific, therefore screening and well-defined mitigation and monitoring measures will be developed for each subproject through the ESMP. 7. The nature of the activities in the Program does not suggest that specific vulnerable and marginalized communities/ Traditional Local Communities could be harmed by the Program hence the potential negative social impacts are those typically worksite specific risks related and limited to the construction phase or relate to misuse of data. The design of the Program aims to foster the protection and integration of vulnerable and marginalized communities/Traditional Local Communities into the Program design, by having data that will benefit the said communities. Potential negative impacts of the Program includes but not limited to (i) safety and misuse of data collected, as Vulnerable groups may be placed at risk if data is collected or shared inappropriately, (ii) Lack of transparency and trust by local communities due to poor consultations and stakeholder engagement with project beneficiaries, vulnerable groups, and marginalized communities/ Traditional Local Communities and (iii) politicization of decision making due to data collected. 8. In addition to screening, the Program has developed a set of principles and minimum standards that will act as measures to minimize project risks at the concept level. Digital security needs to be considered in relation to both data protection and cyber-security, which could affect community security. The ESMP will evaluate risks to community health and safety associated with potential electronic waste management. The PIU will establish an effective and adequately financed project-specific grievance redress mechanism that is accessible to all project beneficiaries and put measures to prevent and respond to incidents of gender-based violence within the context of the Program’s investment projects. Due consideration to the cultural appropriateness of, and equitable access to, Program benefits, giving special attention to the needs or concerns of vulnerable groups and the rights and interests of VMGs/ Traditional Local Communities will be incorporated in project implementation. This component supports the implementation of the SHaSA2 2017-2026 that the of the African Union together with its member states adopted to produce, harmonize, benchmark, and make readily available critical country statistics. SHaSA2 is a continent-wide strategy is anchored in four strategic themes: (1) producing quality statistics for Africa by (1.1) expanding the statistical information base; (1.2) transforming existing statistics for comparability; and (1.3) harmonizing the standards and methods of statistical production; (2) coordinating the production of quality statistics for Africa by (2.1) establishing effective coordination and collaboration mechanisms; and (2.2) defining statistical priorities for the implementation of integration and development agendas; (3) developing sustainable institutional capacities of the African Statistical System (AfSS) by (3.1) reforming and enhancing National Statistical Systems; (3.2) reforming and enhancing regional and continental statistical systems; (3.3) developing sustainable statistical capacities; and (3.4) establishing an effective technological environment; and (4) promoting a culture of quality policy and decision-making by (4.1) driving evidence-based decisions through the increased use of statistics; and (4.2) improving the communication of statistical information.
