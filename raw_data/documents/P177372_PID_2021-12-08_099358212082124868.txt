Introduction and Context Country Context Gabon, a central African country located on the Atlantic Ocean borders Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, and the Republic of Congo. Sparsely populated, with a population of 2.2 million (2020)1 and forests covering 91%2 of its territory, it nonetheless has one of the highest urbanization rates in Africa; more than four in five Gabonese citizens live in cities. The urbanization rate shot up from 20% in the early 1960s to 89% in 2017, and is expected to rise to 92% and 95% by 2030 and 2050, respectively3. The capital, Libreville, and Port Gentil—the economic capital of the country—are home to 59% of the population. One in two Gabonese citizens is under the age of 20, and according to the 2012 Second Demographic and Health Survey, the fertility rate in urban areas is four children per woman and six in rural areas. Gabon is one of Africa’s few upper-middle-income countries. The gross domestic product (GDP) per capita is estimated at US$ 15,190.64, driven by extractive industries (oil, wood, and minerals). Rich in natural resources, it is the fifth largest oil producer in Africa and has had strong economic growth over the past decade, driven by its production of oil and manganese. The oil sector has accounted for 80% of exports, 45% of GDP, and 60% of fiscal revenue on average over the past five years. However, as the country is facing a decline in its oil reserves combined with a drop of oil prices globally since 2015, the Gabonese government decided to diversify its economy. Gabon’s fiscal position worsened in 2015, with the country recording a fiscal deficit for the first time since 1998. Despite the government’s attempts to rein in expenditure and offset the decline in oil revenue, Gabon’s economy stalled and was projected to grow by 0.8% in 2018 compared to 0.5% in 2017. With a Human Development Index (HDI) of 0.703 in 20205, Gabon is today in the high human development category— positioning it at 119 out of 189 countries and territories. Between 1990 and 2019, Gabon’s HDI value increased from 0.613 to 0.703, an increase of 14.7 percent. However, when the value is discounted for inequality, the HDI falls to 0.544, which shows the inequal distribution of human development across the population at the National level. In addition, the most recent survey data publicly available for Gabon Multi-dimensional Poverty Index refer to 2012. The country’s unemployment rate is around 20.5%, with youth and women the most affected. Gabon performs relatively well in terms of its poverty rate, but poverty is not evenly distributed across urban and rural areas. The country seems to have almost eradicated extreme poverty, using the global measure, with only 3.4% of the population under $1.90 per person per day in 2017. However, using the international poverty line for upper middle- income countries, 32.2% of the population is considered poor. The depth of poverty is twice higher in rural areas than in urban zones, as 59.5% of the rural population lives in poverty, compared only 29.4% in urban areas (21.2% in Libreville and Port-Gentil and 38.2% in other urban centers). Even if poverty has declined over the past decades, the pace of decline was slower than population growth, resulting in an absolute increase in the number of poor. Most of this increase accrued to urban areas, leading to a higher concentration of urban poverty. While urban areas outside Libreville and Port-Gentil experienced the fastest decline in poverty rates, they also experienced the largest increase in the absolute number of poor: 78%, compared with 26% in large cities and 4% in rural areas. While about one-quarter of the poor remain located in Libreville, their numbers are increasing rapidly in other urban cities too, particularly in the eastern urban regions. To address those multiple economic and social challenges, the Government of Gabon launched in January 2021 the Transformation Acceleration Plan (Plan d’Accélération de la Transformation - PAT) for the period covering 2021-2023. Among other objectives, the PAT aims at reducing the weight of the oil sector in Gabon GDP to below 20% by 2025, compared to 33% today. The PAT also plans to bring the percentage of the population living below the poverty line to less than 25% of the population by 2025, compared with 35% today. The PAT follows the previous Economic Recovery Plan (Plan de Relance de l’Economie – PRE) for the period covering 2017-2021. Its objective was, among others, to strengthen the competitiveness of the other growth sectors, improve the business environment and optimize the revenue and financing of the economy to mitigate the effects of the oil crisis. Both Plans are part of the Gabon Strategic Development Plan (Plan Stratégique Gabon Emergent) launched in 2012 with the main objective of transforming its economy into an emerging economy by 2025 by improving the country's growth and competitiveness6. Financial management constitutes a major challenge in Gabon public sector management and such that the inherent risk of the Public Financial Management (PFM) system is rated High. Recent analytical studies (PEFA 2016 and PER 2018) revealed major issues and called for transparent and efficient management, particularly in a tight fiscal situation. Weak budget control and fiscal risk management including debt management has led so far to frequent deviations from the approved budget, and accumulated arrears. In 2015, a new harmonized PFM framework embedded in the CEMAC PFM directives was transposed into national law. It introduces a logic of performance and an obligation of results, in administrations previously managed according to a logic of means. The budgetary and accounting provisions which it institutes also allow Parliament to strengthen its role in approving and reviewing public expenditure hence reinforcing accountability for the achievement of the objectives assigned to public action. However, that reform has not yielded concrete results so far. The reform requires technical adjustments and the implementation of numerous tools and measures in all areas of public finance management as well as a new management approach. The current shortcomings make it only a de jure process allowing to have an Initial Finance Law (LFI) and a Corrective Finance Law (LFR) apparently in conformity with the harmonized framework of CEMAC. However, the accountability of the managers of public services at all levels, or the transparency by sincere execution reports addressing the substance of the expenditure by its performance, is absent or not well enforced. The cause is that the reform requires in-depth reforms of the structures of each ministry, and the implementation of new processes and the appearance of new actors / functions (program manager, manager of 6 PAT report Oct 12, 2021 Page 4 of 12 The World Bank Gabon Urban Development Project (P177372) operational program budget, head of operational unit, etc.), which cannot be improvised but must be harmonized both at national and decentralized levels. A continuous reform of budget, treasury, financial reporting, and audit systems is required. Overall improvement in PFM areas will be aiming at opening the way for a gradual use of national PFM system for Bank-financed projects in Gabon. GoG has committed to the implementation of a new strategic plan “Plan d’Accélération de la Transformation�? that gives a clear idea of the government's needs and a set of priorities actions aimed at supporting the government of Gabon in its response to economic and social challenges, particularly in a post-covid context. Two components of the plan are devoted to improving the management of the public sector through actions such as improving the management and clearance of arrears, collection of domestic resources, accelerating the digitization of public services, debt management, optimization of the wage bill, optimization of the management of public enterprises and optimization of the management of public investments. Despite Gabon having taken important steps forward on gender equality with the recent legal reforms, gender gaps are strong in the country. According to Gabon Systematic Country Diagnostic, June 26, 20207 there is a higher incidence of poverty among urban women-headed households (33.7 percent) than urban male-headed households (27.6 percent). Women-headed households have fewer adult members, less education and work less in qualified jobs and productive sectors than men headed-households. As per the 2017 Gabonese Survey on Poverty Evaluation and Monitoring (EGEP), 24.1 percent of women aged 15 years and older had no education at all and 21 percent of those who have some education did not go beyond primary, compared with, respectively, 19.7 and 15 percent of men. About 19 percent of women are unemployed, compared to 11 percent of men. Also, around 62 percent of employed women work in low profile jobs (such as household workers or self-employed accountants), compared to 46 percent of working men. Women are confronted with several forms of violence (National Strategy for Reducing Gender Inequalities of Gabon, 2020). Women represent 90 percent of sexual violence victims, 64 percent of physical violence, and 83 percent of economic violence. Social discrimination against women remains high, although recent legal reforms updated the Civil Code, the Criminal Code, and the Labor Code, and a law to eliminate violence and discrimination against women was issued on September 6, 2021 (Law No. 006/2021). These legal reforms aim to strengthen women’s rights to property and job -related benefits and enable women’s access to credit, e.g. allowing spouses to open a bank account. Sectoral and Institutional Context The country's urban population is distributed in sparsely populated and poorly connected urban areas. Apart from Libreville (578.000 inhabitants), Port-Gentil (109.000 inhabitants), and Franceville (129.000 inhabitants), all the other cities in the country are small or very small (18 cities with populations of between 5,000 and 45,000) 8. They are scattered over a relatively large territory of 266,667 km2. The population density is 8.6 people per km2. In the absence of substantial investment in transport infrastructure, connectivity between and within cities represents a major challenge for Gabon's urbanization and the country's economic development. The low geographic density is also reflected in a low economic density, with nearly 60% of economic activity concentrated in the two largest cities, Libreville (50%) and Port-Gentil (10%). The city of Franceville contributes 7% of GDP to the economy, concentrating, along with the northern regions around Makokou, most of the country's agriculture. The oil industry is mainly based in Port-Gentil and Owendo, and the mining industry in Mouanda and around Franceville. The rest of the national territory represents a marginal part of the country's economic activities. 7 Increasing Economic Diversification & Equalizing Opportunity to Accelerate Poverty Reduction. 8 https://worldpopulationreview.com/ Oct 12, 2021 Page 5 of 12 The World Bank Gabon Urban Development Project (P177372) The decentralization process is progressing very slowly in Gabon. Although the objective of advancing the decentralization agenda has been present in political discourse for many years, little tangible progress has been made up to date. Law No. 15/96 of June 6, 1996 was updated by Law No. 001 /2014 of June 15, 2015. Nevertheless, this goal has not been translated into tangible progress on the ground. The decentralized local entities established, still do not have autonomy in the planning and management of their development. As a result, the implementation of urban and regional planning policies at the local level continues to be the direct responsibility of the respective sectoral ministries. Municipalities are also very poorly endowed in terms of technical staff and equipment. Currently, the only technical competence transferred to them is the solid waste management sector within their respective territories. Their financial resources are mainly provided by (i) dividends on State tax revenues (mainly personal income tax), a proportion of which is transferred to local government entities, (ii) State subsidies to finance solid waste management, (iii) their own-source revenue from local taxes. Finally, local municipalities have the legal option of borrowing from national banks or financial institutions, but this arrangement is not effective because municipalities’ financial management and capacity do not provide the necessary guarantees for bank loans. Resources are therefore largely insufficient to finance necessary investments and meet the need of improving the living conditions of the urban population. Less than 9% of public spending in the state budget is devoted to the urban sector (including housing and community facilities construction, transportation, water and electricity supply, public sanitation, and housing finance)9, resulting in substantial deficits in basic infrastructure and services in the cities. In recent decades, investments have not been able to keep pace with the rapid and uncontrolled urbanization of the country. This resulted in the proliferation of informal settlements in the cities, characterized by poor access to basic services, poverty, poor connectivity, lack of drainage systems, insecure tenure, and exposure to risks. Lack of planning, enforcement, and underinvestment in infrastructure has resulted in development often taking place in flood-prone areas, swampy lowlands, or hillsides unsuitable for construction. These informal settlements, mostly occupied by poor households, account for 36.6% % of urban population nationwide10. 89.3% of the urban population has sustainable access to basic drinking water services11, 90.7% of total population has access to electricity, while road density increased from 3.4 in 2007 to 5.3 km per 100 km212. Concerning sanitation, only 50.9% of the urban population have access to at least basic sanitation, and the solid waste collection coverage rate for Gabonese cities is 57%13. Uncontrolled urbanization, ineffective urban planning and poor urban management prevent cities from functioning efficiently and playing a driving role in the country's economy. Given the lack of strategic planning, proper control of land use and electricity, drinking water, efficient urban and interurban transport, and telecommunications networks, the productivity of Gabonese cities is relatively low. Cities are mostly of low-density and are under-equipped, making them unable to fulfil their role as engines of growth in the country's economy. More investment is needed to strengthen this role, particularly with a view of achieving territorial balance. In addition, the rapid population growth in large cities and peri-urban areas, exacerbated by the problems caused by climate change, requires that inclusiveness and resilience be resolutely integrated into urban planning and management systems. To address these issues, several interventions from the GoG in the urban sector have started to have positive effects, including with support from the World Bank. Among other achievements, ILDP2 supported the elaboration of urban master plans (SDAU) for the eight provincial capitals apart from Libreville. Successive urban projects have contributed to improving the situation of urban planning in general in the country. They have also contributed to strengthening the municipal and national technical capacities in urban management, environmental and social M&E of works, and the collection and analysis of urban field data, through various technical assistance. However, it is still necessary to improve effectiveness of municipal technical services, and to develop all the operational documents needed for a more effective urban planning in the country. The national urban strategy lacks the required capacity and coordination to effectively manage the spatial development of Gabonese cities. There is no recent urban development policy in Gabon, and urban development has been unfolding without coherent development plans. While the national Urban Development and transports strategy was defined through the PAPSUT (with World Bank support in 1996–2001), its implementation was very weak, mainly because of the poor mobilization of resources and the complicated implementation of neighborhood upgrading operations in under- served areas14. The previous LIPD2 supported the update of the Master Plans (Schéma Directeur d’Aménagement et d’Urbanisme - SDAU) for the nine provincial capitals, as well as the elaboration of Neighborhood Development Plans (Plan d’Aménagement Concerté de Quartier - PACQ). However, the approval process for the SDAUs is underway at the ministry level, and it will therefore be necessary to develop land use plans for eligible cities to operationalize them. Gabon’s high urbanization could be an opportunity for the country’s economic growth, but without planning, this rapid urbanization could be a huge challenge. Currently, the lack of planning has a strong impact on the development of cities. Gabonese cities are sprawled and sparsely populated, with an average urban population density of 4212 inhabitants per km2 in 201515. Up to 36.6% of the urban population lives in informal settlements, and most of major cities are struggling with issues related to DRM, land use tenure, sanitation, access to basic services and jobs, housing, and urban mobility. Addressing these issues through efficient planning is essential if the country is to better leverage its urbanization16. Municipalities lack financial and technical capabilities to develop and implement urban plans and invest in urban infrastructure. This situation has contributed to a lack of spatial management and a development characterized by urban sprawl which, in turn, poses high difficulties for municipalities who need to equip and manage their territory with limited resources. Consequently, urban management suffers from chronic issues such as inefficient land use and dysfunctional land markets, lack of maintenance of exiting assets, proper prioritization, and enforcement of regulation. A recent decentralization law was passed in 2015, and its practical implementation is expected to improv e cities’ capacities to achieve their functional potential, especially if it is coupled with better resource allocation and more effective management by local governments. Implementing decrees are greatly needed but for the moment, cities are struggling to execute their decentralization mandate to deliver services. Indeed, while provincial capitals have substantial revenues compared to other cities in the region, investment consumes only 10-30 percent of total resources because 54-87 percent of municipal budgets go to recurrent costs, mostly payrolls17. Most cities are overstaffed, and their personnel are inadequately qualified despite the strengthening of municipal technical services supported by the previous LIPD2. Even major cities are left with limited financial and technical resources to provide adequate services and invest in infrastructure. Flooding, including coastal, urban, and riverine floods, is a particular high hazard for Gabon, potentially damaging and life-threatening. River floods are expected to occur at least once in the next 10 years. Surface flood hazard in urban and rural areas is also expected to occur. Natural disasters, such as flooding, in the coastal area is also expected to cause significant economic losses for coastal infrastructure and related livelihoods. For example, the Port of Port-Gentil is only four meters above sea level. Although floods currently affect on average only 0.21% of the total population, there is likely to be extreme weather events occurring in Gabon in the near to medium term19. While the country is vulnerable to the impact of climate change through rising seas and changing precipitation patterns, it is also vulnerable to increasing temperatures resulting in drought events. Approximately 21,000 people are affected by drought each year in Gabon, with a potential annual impact of US$185 million20. These climate-related risk will put significant pressure on the country’s economy, urban infrastructure, and vulnerable groups such as elderly and the poor. In urban areas, although there is no data available on the female to male ratio of time spent on unpaid care work, it is customary in Gabon that women care for the children and the housework and the men take care of the finances (Emery, 201321). Poor infrastructure and services might also constraint women’s participation in the labor force. Only 49% of households had access to basic sanitation services in urban areas (UNICEF, 2017). Households headed by men disposed of more adequately their wastewater (38.5 percent) than female-headed households (33.9 percent) because of better services (Global Results of the General Census of Population and Housing in Gabon, 2013). Inadequate wastewater management causes malaria, the leading cause of sickness and death in Gabon, affecting primarily children. Women are the care providers for their sick children. Furthermore, the quality of roads in Gabon is bad, ranked in 112th place out of 138 countries analyzed in 2017 (Global Competitiveness Index). Roads may not facilitate women more frequent travel to perform housework chores, usually shorter commute distances, more non-work-related, and chain trips. Relationship to CPF The proposed project is well aligned with the upcoming World Bank Group (WBG) Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Gabon, currently under preparation, and advances key Systematic Country Diagnostic (SCD) policy priorities. Building on the findings of the June 2020 SCD (Report No. 150048-GA), a new CPF is currently under preparation. The new CPF is expected to feature continued support for vulnerability and resilience. In particular, the upcoming draft CPF for Gabon seeks to address Gabon’s needs as it confronts the COVID-19 pandemic as well as laying the foundations for its longer- term development. It reflects the joint contribution of the WBG with an enhanced IFC strategy. The CPF comprises two objectives of (i) better infrastructure and public services in cities and (ii) increased private investment in economic diversification. These objectives represent the goals that the WBG will seek to achieve during the forthcoming CPF and their contribution to two pillars thereof: (i) increased urban resilience and (ii) job-rich private sector development. The proposed project will also advance the following SCD priorities: (i) Improve urban management and inland connectivity to reduce economic distance, increase density, and reduce disparities in living standards; (ii) Reduce economic distance through universal provision of essential public services; (iii) improve connectivity to main urban centers and facilitate market accessibility. Concept Description The proposed Project comprises four components: (1) Improved access to urban services in cities and underserviced neighborhoods; (2) Capacity building in municipal management for target cities and relevant stakeholders; (3) Management, coordination, and monitoring and evaluation of the Project; and (4) Contingency Emergency Response Component. Component 1: Improvement of urban infrastructures and access to urban services in underserviced neighborhoods Priority investment program: improving connectivity and urban services in 4 target cities This component finances primary urban infrastructures in cities to improve connectivity and investments in underserviced neighborhoods thus improving their integration into the urban fabric, and access to urban infrastructure and services. Proposed investments could include (i) city primary infrastructures such as upgrading of selected urban roads, drainage, water and sewerage and other municipal infrastructure and (ii) investments to favor urban mobility and initiate an urban upgrading dynamic within underserviced neighborhoods such as upgrading of selected urban streets, including small public facilities (footbridges, stairs, pedestrian ways, public lighting, access ramps), public places, municipal equipment, green spaces, etc. The priorities will be identified by the neighborhoods’ population and municipality in accordance with their City Development Plan once up to date. To ensure the actual involvement and impulsion of the local communities in the project, a community consultation process will be developed at the outset with the support of a qualified urban operator. It will support the relevant implication of population in their neighborhood’s development and ensure project implementation through technical assistance tailored to the city needs. To ensure a smooth implementation despite municipalities’ low capacities, the investments will be prioritized, starting at first with infrastructures which are easier to implement, such as streets paving, drainage, public places, etc. Ideally two or three main types of investments per city will be financed, including street upgrading, to avoid dispersion and facilitate maintenance by the municipality. While financing urban infrastructures for 4 prioritized cities to be confirmed according to their technical and environmental and social readiness, the project will prepare similar investments for the remaining 4 cities by carrying out preparatory studies of capital investments to enable their subsequent bidding and implementation. These investments will be financed during the second phase of the project, and once experience from the first phase has been gained. To promote job creation and respond to the authorities' concern to initiate a local economic dynamic, the project will explore Oct 12, 2021 Page 9 of 12 The World Bank Gabon Urban Development Project (P177372) different options within procurement rules such as identify and finance infrastructures and services, wherever possible, with a labor-intensive angle in its tender documents and contracts and seeking bidding joint-venture with local companies. The main benefits from this component will stem from improved access to services and living conditions and from job opportunities from labor-intensive construction activities. The local construction sector, especially SMEs, will also benefit from training and contract opportunities. Component 2: Strengthening the capacity of target cities and national government in municipal management This component aims to strengthen the capacity of targeted cities and other stakeholders in municipal management. The subcomponents are divided along the two specific groups. Subcomponent 2.1: Municipal Capacity Building: This sub-component will finance activities to improve the administrative, financial, and technical capacities of the 8 targeted municipalities to enable them to carry out their mandated functions. The activities financed under this sub-component include, among others, the operationalization of the Urban Master Plan (SDAU) produced by LIPD2 through an elaboration of Land Use Plans (POS) or associated relevant document in target cities. To avoid delays in implementation, investments financed through component 1 will not be dependent on a Land use plan elaboration, these processes will advance in parallel. However, a preliminary analysis to be conducted as a part of project preparation will allow the identified investments to be in accordance with future land use plans and in accordance with the cities’ integrated urban vision. Other activities to be financed under this sub-component will include operational and pragmatic training for municipal, departmental, and national agents - if possible, jointly to allow exchange of views and networking. The main focus will be on building capacities related to investments’ implementation and operation and maintenance of the infrastructure constructed, through technical assistance and MDOD (Maîtrise D'Ouvrage Déléguée) tailored to the needs. Subcomponent 2.2: National Government capacity building: This sub-component will finance activities related to capacity building of central government and deconcentrated ministerial departments in the areas of: (i) decentralization diagnosis studies, including on municipal finance (finance transfers from government and local resource mobilization); (ii) capacity building in urban planning rules and regulation development; (ii) support in updating the national urban development strategy. Component 3: Management, coordination, monitoring, and evaluation of the Project This component will ensure smooth implementation of all project activities in accordance with the Bank’s policies and guidelines. It will support the Borrower in the areas of project coordination, supervision of works, hiring an independent consulting firm as the ‘supervising engineers’, FM, procurement, monitoring, and evaluation (M&E), communication, audits, and preparation and supervision of implementation of the safeguards’ instruments, preparation of related surveys, including through the provision of training, operating costs, goods, and services. Component 4: Contingency Emergency Response Component (US$0 million) This component is a ‘zero-assignation’ CERC that will provide funding for immediate response in the event of an eligible crisis or emergency, defined as an event that has caused or is likely to imminently cause a major adverse economic and/or social impact associated with natural or man-made crises or disasters.
