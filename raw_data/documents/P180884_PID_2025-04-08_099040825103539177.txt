Introduction and Context 1. Cameroon is endowed with abundant resources and economic potential, but its development is hampered by significant and multifaceted challenges. As a lower-middle-income country with a Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita of $1,7361, Cameroon has experienced limited and volatile economic growth over the past four decades, underperforming its structural peers in the region and its aspirational peers globally2. By 2022, the country’s population living in poverty had risen to over 10 million out of the total population of 29 million3. Nearly 87% of all jobs in Cameroon are informal4. Urban areas, where the majority of formal jobs and the greatest economic potential are located, face significant challenges. Fundamental challenges include: (i) a severe deficit in urban infrastructure, exacerbated by rapid population growth, unplanned urban expansion, and climate change; and (ii) dysfunctional land administration as bottlenecks for infrastructure and private sector development. 2. Ongoing crises such as conflict and climate change have undermined livelihoods, escalated forced displacement, and worsened the country’s economic prospects and livability. The United Nations Office High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) estimated more than 2.1 million people were forcibly displaced in Cameroon as of February 2025, mainly due to the Boko Haram insurgency in the Far North region, the socio-political crisis in the anglophone region of the Northwest and Southwest, and the influx of over 400,000 refugees from the conflicts in the Central African Republic (CAR) and Nigeria5. Cameroon has faced increasingly high climate and disaster risks from floods, droughts and landslides, leading to increased vulnerability of poor and at-risk households, dwindling resources, rampant food insecurity, and rising social tensions. The combination of these security challenges and climate change impacts has highlighted the need for investment in public infrastructure, greater resilience, and improved land administration services. 1 World Bank, Country and Lending Groups, 2023 2 World Bank, Country Scan for the Cameroon Country Economic Memorandum, 2023 3 National Institute of Statistics (NIS), Household Surveys ECAM-5, 2024 4 NIS, Survey of Employment and Informal Sector, 2021 5 UNHCR, https://data.unhcr.org/en/country/cmr, retrieved on March 31, 2025 Page 2 The World Bank Cameroon Sustainable Cities and Land Project (P180884) C. D. Project Description 5. In total, the project will benefit an estimated of 1.8 million people in Cameroon. The main beneficiary groups are as follows. First, residents and businesses in urban neighborhoods using the physical investments in Yaounde and Douala will benefit directly or indirectly from improved access to urban infrastructure. Second, people in Wouri and Mfoundi, where Douala and Yaounde are located, will benefit from the issuance of land titles with improved tenure security, including an increased percentage of female beneficiaries. Third, people and institutions involved in urban development and land administration will benefit from increased capacity, efficiency, transparency and accountability. Fourth, additional benefits will come from more and better jobs, such as labor-intensive civil works and jobs related to sustainable urban and land administration services, as well as induced benefits to the private sector and citizens. In addition, an assessment of gender and vulnerability gaps and measures will maximize the benefits for women, girls, and the vulnerable population groups, including the poor and internally displaced people. 6. The project adopts a multi-sectoral approach to invest in urban development and land administration while adapting to the challenges of climate change. The integrated project invests mainly in climate-resilient infrastructure and land administration services. The project has four integrated components. The first component invests in critical urban infrastructure, accompanied by policy and technical support and capacity building focusing on priorities and operationalization. The second component complements the first to address the critical, chronic land administration bottlenecks that hinder sustainable urbanization and private sector growth. The third and fourth components invest in project management and emergency preparedness, respectively, to ensure effective project implementation and rapid response to emergencies. In addition, the project incorporates multi-stakeholder processes and actively involves citizens and beneficiaries. 7. Component 1. Transformative, Climate-Resilient Urban Infrastructure (US$170 million). Sub-component 1.1. Improvement of Urban Infrastructure (US$160 million) will be coordinated with other relevant urban infrastructure projects and directly contribute to the cities’ visions7. Sustainable, resilient8, and inclusive measures, including citizen engagement, will be integrated into the sub-projects9. Sub-component 1.1 will include the following types of investments in Yaounde and Douala: i) Inclusive and resilient urban transport infrastructure; ii) Resilient urban disaster risk management infrastructure with a focus on drainage; iii) Inclusive and sustainable socio-economic facilities and spaces; iv) Sustainable and resilient urban asset management; and v) Development and implementation of Resettlement Action Plans (RAPs). 9. Sub-component 1.2. Policy and Technical Support and Capacity Building for Sustainable Urbanization (US$10 million) will support i) the formulation of the National Cities Modernization Program, including preparation of prioritized urban infrastructure investments for follow-on operations; ii) operationalization of the National Slum Upgrading Strategy; iii) preparation of actions and investments for the conservation and sustainable management of mangroves and coastal areas in Douala; and iv) development and operationalization of control mechanisms of one-stop shops for urban planning and construction management in Yaounde and Douala. 10. Component 2. Improvement of Urban Land Administration Services (US$20 million) employs a three-pronged approach to strengthen MINDCAF’s capacity for efficient and service-oriented land administration: i) land policy and regulatory framework; ii) modernization of land administration services; and iii) strengthening urban land tenure security. By strengthening MINDCAF’s capacity, specifically to better manage land cadaster and registry records, the project will enable the GoC to improve urban land use planning and enforcement, enhance its effectiveness in taking climate actions, support business and economic growth, and contribute to improving women’s land rights. 11. Component 3. Project Management (US$10 million). This component will finance inter alia the operating costs of the Project Implementation Unit (PIU) for coordination10, project management, procurement, financial management including audits, environmental and social risk management, monitoring and evaluation (M&E), and relevant technical expertise and reviews. 12. Component 4. Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC) (US$0 million). In recognition of Cameroon’s significant vulnerability to shocks, and in line with the Bank’s Investment Project Financing Policy, an initially unfunded CERC is included in the project. This component will enable the GOC to respond promptly and effectively to an eligible emergency or crisis that is a natural or man-made disaster or crisis that has caused or is likely to cause significant adverse economic and/or social impacts. 14. The overall environmental and social (E&S) risk is rated as High. 16. Environmental risks are rated as Substantial. The project will support activities in Yaounde and Douala such as: i) construction and rehabilitation of urban roads, ii) management of urban drainage and disaster risks, iii) building social- economic infrastructure, and iv) improvement of maintenance of selected urban roads. The proposed civil works within existing urban areas are expected to result in various environmental impacts and risks. Construction and rehabilitation activities, particularly those related to urban flood management and transportation infrastructure, may disrupt local ecosystems, contaminate soil and water, and lead to inefficient resource use. These interventions can alter natural drainage conditions, expose workers to hazards and occupational health and safety (OHS) risks, generate construction debris, and produce solid and hazardous waste. In addition, these activities are likely to contribute to noise and air pollution, affecting both the urban environment and local communities with associated hazards and traffic congestion. 17. Over time, Component 2 could lead to long-term to environmental challenges due to potential changes in land use, such as increased soil degradation, disruption of natural drainage systems, and increased risk of flooding and erosion. All physical interventions under Component 2 will be carried out outside environmentally sensitive areas such as mangroves and forests, which will be subject to more specific developments within the framework of technical support under the component. However, the size of the activities is neither large nor complex. All potential environmental impacts and risks are predictable, localized, temporary, reversible, and small in magnitude. They can be easily and predictably mitigated using standard mitigation measures in line with the Bank’s Health and Safety Guidelines and the Good International Industry Practice (GIIP). 18. The social risk is rated as High. This risk is associated with six potential groups. i) Negative and mostly permanent impacts caused by the land acquisition and resettlement under Sub-component 1.1, which, although mainly resulting in economic displacement, will be carried out in very densely populated areas, including informal settlements hosting the vulnerable people in Yaounde and Douala. ii) Downstream negative social impacts that could result from the outcomes of the policy and technical support activities under Sub-component 1.2 and Component 2. Activities may unintentionally and inadvertently result in the impairement of existing legal rights of some of the most vulnerable people. Risks include, for example, the inability to register some forms of tenure such as communal customary rights, inabilty to register land in areas with informal forms of land tenure, and issuance of titles that may result in the disqualification and potential eviction Page 5 The World Bank Cameroon Sustainable Cities and Land Project (P180884) of current occupants of land parcels. iii) Sub-component 2.1 activities and outcomes may generate downstream negative impacts on Indigenous Peoples/Sub-Saharan African Historically Underserved Traditional Local Communities (IP/SSAHUTLCs) lands and natural resources if appropriate measures are not taken by the project. iv) Poor workers’ conditions, including Sexual Exploitation and Abuse (SEA), and Sexual Harassment (SH) in the workplace, communities, and transport systems. v) Insufficient participation of vulnerable people in project consultations and access to project benefits. vi) Limited capacity and experience of the Borrower in managing these environmental and social risks under the ESF, particularly on land acquisition and resettlement. Universal accessibility will be included in infrastructure design to enhance inclusiveness. 19. E&S instruments. In addition to the ESMF which will guide specific E&S studies and plans for infrastructure investments during implementation, a Labor Management Procedures (LMP), a Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) with its Grievance Redress Mechanism (GRM), and a GBV: SEA/SH Action Plan and the project's Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP) were prepared, consulted upon and disclosed at the Bank’s and the GoC’s websites prior to project appraisal, and redisclosed by April 4, 2025. Similarly, a RF was prepared, consulted upon, and disclosed on April 3, 2025. This RF fills the legal gaps in the national legislation with ESS5 provisions (i.e. eligibility of non-titled land such as customary lands and loss of livelihood and income, and determination of the value of affected assets at full replacement cost) and simplifies the lengthy institutional process of expropriation in Cameroon. This RF was developed building on the experience from the second group of PDVIR RAPs, which applied the mayor-led negotiations with affected people prior to the expropriation based on RAPs approved by the Bank as described in the ESCP. Implementation of the RAPs will be monitored by external concurrent audits. Compensation for resettlements will be financed from IDA credit proceeds to ensure sufficient and timely compensation to affected people. Draft ESIA-ESMPs and RAPs for priority subprojects, including roads in Yaounde and drainage in Douala have been prepared and consulted upon, and were publicly disclosed by April 4, 2025. These E&S instruments will be finalized during project implementation as foreseen in the project's ESCP. Institutional arrangements include E&S specialists in the PIU and MLTU at MINDCAF, as well as E&S focal points in Douala and Yaounde LTLUs, including a capacity building program as described in the project's ESCP. Finally, if canceled PDVIR works are included in the project, they will comply with the ESCP requirements. Similarly, fin the event that the IsDB confirms its parallel financing of urban infrastructure sub-projects in Douala or Yaoundé, the Bank will work with the GoC and the IsDB on the assessment and management of environmental and social risks to determine whether or not they are associated facilities of the Bank's financed investment and use a common approach where relevant. 20. OP 7.50 applies to this project, as the project may finance activities that may use or risk polluting waters from tributaries of the Sanaga River which is considered an international waterway. The exception to the riparian notification requirement under paragraph 7(c) of the Policy applies because the activities financed by the project are limited to the use of water from tributaries located exclusively in Cameroon and Cameroon is the lowest downstream riparian country. The exception to the notification requirement was approved by the Bank’s Regional Vice President on April 3, 2025. E. Implementation 21. A high-level Project Steering Committee (PSC) will be established to provide oversight and inter-agency coordination for project implementation. The PSC will bring together representatives from key relevant ministries and government agencies to i) make high-level decision related to the project; ii) provide strategic direction and oversight of project implementation; iii) provide inter-agency and multi-stakeholder coordination given the multi-sectoral nature of the project; iv) approve annual work plans and budgets; and v) review progress and audit reports. The details will be elaborated in the Project Operations Manual (POM). 22. The Project Implementation Unit (PIU) will be hosted at MINHDU. The project will be implemented under the overall responsibility and coordination of MINHDU, which has experience in implementing Bank-financed projects namely Page 6 The World Bank Cameroon Sustainable Cities and Land Project (P180884) the Urban and Water Development Support Project (PDUE - closed) and Resilient and Inclusive Cities Development Project (PDVIR). The PIU will be responsible for the overall project implementation including the coordination with the PSC, technical liaison units (TLUs, as described below), relevant government agencies at the national and subnational levels and key stakeholders, as well as reporting to the Bank and liaison with development partners. The PIU will comprise qualified staff members who will be recruited on a competitive basis and whose effective mobilization will be required for project effectiveness, according to national legislation. 23. A Ministerial Technical Liaison Unit (MTLU) will be established in MINDCAF for implementing Component 2. The MTLU will take the technical responsibility for the execution of all the Component 2 activities, as well as stakeholder engagement and grievance redressing for the component. The MTLU will prepare the technical documents as specified in the POM and submit to the MINDCAF Minister, coordinate and monitor the execution of activities for which the MTLU is responsible, and support the PIU in the implementation in accordance with the POM. 24. Similar to PDVIR, subprojects in Yaounde and Douala will be implemented based on the City Contracts signed by the Mayors and MINHDU. The City Contracts will define the reciprocal engagements including roles and responsibilities of MINHDU and the CUs in the project. Local Steering Committees (LSC) will be organized in Yaounde and Douala. The LSCs will be responsible for coordinating and monitoring the implementation of City Contracts, presided by the Mayors for advancing project implementation. 25. A Local Technical Liaison Unit (LTLU) will be established in each city of Yaounde and Douala for implementing Component 1. Each LTLU will be responsible for coordinating with the respective LSC, and for the technical implementation of the activities including ES as foreseen in the City Contract on behalf of the CUs and CAs.
