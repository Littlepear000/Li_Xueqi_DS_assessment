The Federal Government of Somalia (FGS) has made remarkable progress toward consolidation of peace, political stability and rebuilding the economy over the past three years. Despite the progress, economic growth remains too low to keep up with the population growth and per capita income was US$339 in 2019. Sixty-nine percent of the population lives on less than US$1.90 per day per capita, in 2011 purchasing power parity. Poverty is even more prevalent and deeper among hard to reach populations such as rural residents and nomads. Given their remoteness and mobility, their poverty is also multidimensional due to lower access to basic services such as education and improved water and sanitation. The levels of socioeconomic achievement in the country remain low in many domains. Only one third of primary school-aged children are enrolled in the education system, half of the population is literate, and women have less educational attainment than men across nearly all age-groups. Natural disasters, conflict and displacement affect large fractions of the population, leaving internally displaced persons to face unique challenges beyond poverty including tenure insecurity and spatial dislocation from social networks and former livelihoods. With most Somalis vulnerable to many forms of shocks, especially those related to climate change, building resilience is essential. 2. Due to high and unsustainable debt levels and limited access to international financial resources, enormous challenges lie ahead for poverty reduction and shared prosperity in Somalia. Somalia has been heavily indebted with US$5.2 billion of external debt.1 Debt had restricted Somalia from borrowing from International Financial Institutions for projects that can produce lasting positive development change. The FGS recently prepared the National Development Plan 9 (NDP9) for the period 2020–2024, which served as an Interim Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper and is an important step for debt relief under a World Bank and International Monetary Fund (IMF) initiative known as Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC). 3. The FGS recently made significant strides towards accessing international resources aimed at promoting economic growth and reducing poverty. The FGS cleared its arrears to the International Development Association (IDA) in March 2020. This clearance of IDA arrears allowed Somalia to strengthen its financial relationship with the World Bank Group, fully reestablished its access to new resources from IDA and paved the way to receive debt relief under the Heavily Indebted Poor Country (HIPC) initiative and the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative.2 4. The successful preparation and implementation of a Poverty Reduction Strategy Paper (PRSP), which is necessary to obtain full debt relief, requires a sustainable and reliable statistical system to track the poverty reduction strategy, macroeconomic and structural reform programs. However, the existing National Statistical System (NSS) is weak – eroded by years of conflict – fragmented and lacking coordination while the statistical infrastructure is missing thus jeopardizing Somalia’s aspiration for full debt relief if not addressed. A strong and well-coordinated statistical system will be better placed to generate the basic statistics necessary for policymaking that will support conditions for inclusive growth and poverty reduction. 5. A sustainable and reliable statistical system is also needed to support the understanding of the current coronavirus disease (COVID-19) crisis and inform its post-crisis recovery and growth. An outbreak of COVID-19 caused by the 2019 novel coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) has been spreading rapidly across the world since December 2019. In mid- March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared a global pandemic as the virus rapidly spread across the world and had resulted in over 100,000 cases and over 5,000 deaths across 139 countries. As of June 8, 2020, there were 2,368 confirmed cases of COVID-19 in Somalia.3 However, the virus that causes COVID-19 appears to be highly contagious, suggesting Somalia may continue to report an increase in cases. COVID-19 is expected to have significant health, social and economic impacts at the household and business levels and for the overall economy that will further exacerbate poverty and vulnerability in the country. Having an efficient statistical system, infrastructure and capacity in place to generate reliable data to monitor the wellbeing of households and firms will offer a wealth of information to policymakers and decisionmakers for effective long-term planning and capacity for policy responses based on evidence, especially within an economy that is susceptible to multiple shocks. This needs to be complemented by improved links of data from NSS to the national planning, monitoring and evaluation and project and program implementation. For instance, statistics that are produced and disseminated by the Directorate of National Statistics (DNS) are crucial inputs for the National Planning Department and the Monitoring and Evaluation Directorate (MED) within the Ministry of Planning, Investment and Economic Development (MoPIED) to plan, monitor and evaluate public programs (Error! Reference source not found.). Quality statistics are also essential for agencies responsible for financial and economic management to support policy and decision-making. Three areas of deficiencies constrain these institutions’ core functions: (1) low capacity of the NSS to produce, disseminate and enhance the use of statistics; (2) weak links between the statistical system and policy- and decision-making processes; (3) insufficient capacity to generate the evidence needed to inform the policy and budgeting processes. 7. Years of civil war and ongoing conflict in parts of the country have considerably eroded the Somali statistical system, infrastructure, and capacity leading to gaps in statistical information that is vital for national development. After the fall of the Government in 1991, much of the statistical infrastructure and information was lost. Over the years, the Somali statistical authorities continued to collect some economic and financial data through some surveys, although with minimum capacity and scope. Despite re-establishing a federal government in 2012 and making effort to reestablish some statistical foundations and services, Somalia is still far from a functioning NSS needed to produce official statistics. The FGS is aware of these weaknesses and has enacted a new Statistics Act. The new Act received the President’s assent in February 2020. When operationalized, the new Statistics Act will strengthen the existing mandate of the DNS, leading to the establishment of a Somali National Bureau of Statistics with the necessary authority for the collection, processing and dissemination of official statistics as well as the coordination of official statistics activities undertaken within the NSS. It will also allow the inclusion of statistics information from new actors, such as the private sector and non- governmental organizations (NGOs), into the NSS. 8. The existing NSS is weak, fragmented and lacks coordination institutionally and technically, creating a difficult context to produce statistics. The weak statistical capacity is reflected through the low score in the World Bank’s Statistical Capacity Indicator (SCI).4 In 2017, Somalia ranked the lowest among all countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) with an overall score of 30, which is far below the SSA average of 62 (Figure 2).5 This score is below the average performance along the three dimensions of the indicator, i.e. collection (source data), availability (periodicity and timeliness), and practice (methodology). Somalia scored 20 out of 100 points on both the source data and methodology, below the 56 and 49- point average for SSA respectively. The weakness of methodology is due to the country’s limited adherence to internationally recommended standards, methods and data reporting practices in social and macroeconomic statistics. The weakness of source data is, among others, due to outdated population and agricultural censuses, limited poverty- related surveys and limited data from administrative systems. The production of national accounts statistics is hampered by a myriad of challenges such as weak coordination among data producers, lack of statistical data and infrastructure and inadequate human resources. National accounts are mainly limited because the source data and methodology underpinning the estimates are weak. The DNS relies on the expenditure approach to compile estimates of GDP. It does not compile production-based estimates of GDP. Further, even the only available estimates of GDP are based on imperfect and scarce source data combined with weak assumptions. These estimates are heavily based on only one household consumption survey conducted in 2017 and proxy estimates are often used to fill data gaps.6 Since one data point is used to generate the GDP series, GDP is driven by changes in factors other than consumption, which is unrealistic. The compilation of the production-based estimates of GDP is constrained because there is no comprehensive and up-to-date statistical data and infrastructure—such as a business register of establishments to provide a sampling frame for the economic surveys, statistical sources (household, agriculture, establishment and price surveys, and administrative data), statistical classification. Limitations in the administrative data sources in terms of sectoral and geographic coverage, scope, indicators, timing of collection, and inconsistency of definitions undermine their suitability for national accounts compilation. For instance, there is currently no structured compilation of agricultural statistics and existing records are limited in scope, coverage and quality. Records from the Revenue Authority (excise duty and value-added tax (VAT)) are either incomplete or missing. Information from the Central Bank, Ministry of Finance and umbrella bodies are not systematically available. In addition, MDAs generally do not apply international standards and methodology (classification) for the collection and compilation of data. Therefore, the available data are inconsistent and unreliable. Staff constraints have also made the compilation of national accounts statistics very difficult. The Somali statistical authorities only recently began the compilation of GDP. With little or no prior experience and formal training in national accounts, the DNS needs assistance in designing an appropriate national accounts compilation framework as well as in identifying suitable data sources and deflators, as a start for the compilation of GDP. 12. Somalia has no nationally-representative consumer price index (CPI). This impedes the ability of decision-makers to track inflation, which is a key economic indicator for all countries and is widely used in determining the purchasing power, for taking monetary policy actions, and generally for its impact in decision-making especially on investment and consumption. The accuracy of statistics that incorporate price statistics, including poverty statistics and macroeconomic statistics (such as real GDP), are also undermined. The current challenges to producing a CPI for Somalia include incomplete geographical coverage of price data collection, lags between collection, processing and dissemination, lack of an up-to-date CPI basket and weighting of the commodities in the basket, limited know-how of price data compilation, especially among statistical departments in some of the FMSs. 13. Household surveys of adequate representativeness to support the monitoring of monetary and non-monetary dimensions of poverty are few. Although the Somaliland Household Survey (SLHS) and Somali High Frequency Survey (SHFS) provided much needed indicators including poverty estimates, they had some limitations. The SLHS conducted in 2013 did not cover Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) and nomads. The first wave of the SHFS conducted in 2016 excluded large areas of Somalia that were insecure areas as well as nomads which resulted in the sample representing 40 percent of the Somali population. The second wave of the SHFS (2017) improved on representativeness, expanding geographic and population coverage to include additional areas, IDP settlements and nomads, but weaknesses remained and need to be addressed to produce more accurate and comprehensive figures on poverty. In addition, since 2015, the World Bank has committed to supporting low income countries to conduct at least one household survey every 3–5 years, thus there is need to continue improving the availability of household level data. Further, there is need to create a sustainable means of tracking poverty and other key indicators, which would require building the capacity of the Somali statistical authorities to plan, design, implement and process surveys. SHFS was conducted and processed by a private firm with support from the World Bank and the NSO. SHFS also employed an innovative questionnaire design but requires relatively complex estimation methods for consumption. 14. There is also lack of relevant gender data and statistics which hinders the country’s effort to track progress on gender inequality over time and design policies to address it. Somalia faces tremendous gender inequality. The United Nations Development Programme’s (UNDP) Gender Inequality Index places Somalia as the fourth most unequal country in the world in terms of gender. Gender statistics must reflect the many areas where women and men may not enjoy the same opportunities. For example, the gap in employment between men and women is substantial (20 percentage Page 11 of 79 The World Bank Somali Integrated Statistics and Economic Planning Capacity Building (P171160) points).7 Moreover, the maternal mortality rates are among the highest in the word, at 829 per 100,000 births in 2017.8 To help guide countries produce gender statistics, the minimum set of gender indicators was prepared by the Inter- Agency and Expert Group on Gender Statistics . However, gender statistics produced by Somalia’s Directorate of National Statistics fall short of this recommendation due to data gaps. As a result of these gaps, the country cannot produce some key gender statistics such as sex-disaggregated agricultural land ownership and firm ownership and management. In order to address this gender data gap, there is need to support the statistical system to produce (through improved establishment and household survey) and disseminate more gender statistics, specifically the indicators listed under the minimum list. An improved statistics system used for informing policymaking would have benefits to women in access to basic services, finance, public health, and other services. 15. Emerging challenges require a modern and adaptive approach to data collection, but there is currently no data collection system in place to respond quickly to new and more immediate data needs for policy. Although an essential output of the statistical system, traditional surveys require significant amounts of time for preparation and are less agile to quickly respond to new data needs. The 2017–2018 drought and more recent floods highlighted the need to be able to respond quickly to shocks and disasters. Insecurity in part of the country also continue to hinder data collection requiring less conspicuous methods of data collection. Somali policy makers are operating in a volatile context with very limited data, thus a frequent and rapid monitoring system that can provide almost real-time data even in emergency situations is important to support timely policy responses. Timely and reliable data can support effective allocation of resources at federal state and local levels, and proper design, implementation, and evaluation of public programs. 16. Somalia has taken steps to improve the statistical system in the last five years, and awareness of the importance of quality statistical data for policymaking and progress monitoring is growing. The period 2014–2016 was marked by reforms to the legislative and regulatory frameworks for statistics aimed at improving the organizational structure and operation of what can be considered as a well-functioning NSS. Steps to improve the human and logistical capacities were also taken. In 2018, the Somali National Strategy for the Development of Statistics (NSDS) was designed, providing a strategic vision for the development of statistics in Somalia.9 Furthermore, some statistical infrastructure and policies for dissemination of statistical data were developed. New household-based surveys were implemented, in addition to the 2017 SHFS—providing updated socioeconomic statistics—like the 2014 Population Estimation Survey (PESS), and the 2018 Somali Health and Demographic Survey. The proposed project, fully in line with the objectives of the NSDS, will support the ongoing institutional reform process and aims to sustain the ongoing efforts. 17. The ability of the Monitoring and Evaluation Directorate (MED) within the FGS Ministry of Planning, Investment and Economic Development to fulfill its core functions remains constrained by lack of human resource capacity, quality data and appropriate ICT tools. At present, MED has prepared a national monitoring and evaluation (M&E) policy; developed a rudimentary IT M&E platform and toolkit used for the collection and analysis of M&E data from MDAs and FMSs. However, MED faces substantial challenges which hinder its role of monitoring and evaluating implementation progress of public projects, programs and policies outlined in NDP9. Sub-component 1.2. Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (US$6 million equivalent) 44. The proposed sub-component will support the design and implementation of the Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey (SIHBS) for monitoring welfare, providing macroeconomic baselines and establishing a baseline survey for a frequent data collection system. The primary objective of the SIHBS is to collect detailed information on household expenditures and consumption incurred on goods and services to monitor household welfare and measure poverty. In addition to welfare data, SIHBS will collect other socio-economic information relevant for monitoring the living conditions of private Somali households such as access to basic assets, facilities and services. Data collected through the SIHBS will also be used to improve the GDP estimates and for the compilation of the commodity basket and commodity Page 18 of 79 The World Bank Somali Integrated Statistics and Economic Planning Capacity Building (P171160) weightings used in the construction of a new CPI. Furthermore, the survey will serve as baseline for a rapid monitoring system. 45. The SIHBS will be designed and implemented taking into consideration i) the key objectives of the survey, and ii) the constraints of operating in a complex and insecure environment such as Somalia’s . To that end, to collect as much relevant information as possible, with a certain degree of accuracy, and without jeopardizing the safety of the enumerators, the design of the survey instrument will prioritize the crucial modules needed to measure poverty and key priority living standard indicators. Urgently needed data to complete other household-based economic activities needed for compilation of national accounts will also be added. In this regard, in addition to the consumption module, it is expected that the questionnaire includes an agricultural module, and a household establishment module. The results from these modules will be essential to support the estimation of poverty, inequality, as well as for the compilation of national accounts and CPI. The data collection approach for collecting consumption data will be using a full consumption module capturing data based on a seven days’ recall approach. The integration of the agriculture module, to gather information on agricultural production of household units—which is currently lacking for Somalia—is of critical importance in national accounts (production-based GDP) especially in countries where agriculture is household-based as in Somalia. Due to limited financial resources and DNS capacity, it is thought more appropriate to include an agricultural module in SIHBS instead of carrying out a separate agriculture survey. The inclusion of the household-based establishment module, to gather information on non-agricultural household-based production—which again is lacking for Somalia—is also of critical importance in national accounts, since economic activities are largely informal and this module will help capture most of these activities. The questionnaire and survey methods will be reviewed and modified to address gender data gaps in relevant areas like education, employment, business ownership. 46. During the implementation of the SIHBS survey, special focus will be devoted to ensuring full coordination between the FGS and FMSs- from the sampling to the analysis- as well as to addressing comparability issues with the SHFS (2017) that may arise from the changes in the sampling frame, questionnaire design and timing of the survey. Special attention will also be made to gather the telephone numbers of respondents to allow for interview follow-ups. If needed, a complementary survey in markets will be implemented to address the issue of nonstandard local units. After the survey, sampling of some SIHBS households is needed for multiple follow-ups on select questions for a brief period to support the monitoring of poverty and living standards. Since the frequent monitoring system would be based on SIHBS, there is need for an agreement with the National Statistics Office to assure confidentiality in the use of information shared from SIHBS. 47. The SIHBS will build on lessons learned from previous statistical operations conducted in the country and will follow international best practices to yield quality data. As such, the SIHBS will make use of an updated sampling frame created from satellite image that will include rural, urban, nomadic and IDP populations. The sample will be designed to produce statistics representative of accessible rural and urban areas in Galmudug, Hirshabelle, Jubbaland, Puntland, Somaliland, and South West as well as urban areas in Banadir. The sample will also feature nomads and IDP households located in the above geographic areas. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners 90. Somalia is a country affected by fragility, conflict and violence (FCV) that has endured decades of conflict with limited financial resources and international support. The conflict has damaged not only the physical infrastructure like roads, school and health facilities but also the statistical infrastructure with equipment, statistical records and human resources destroyed. As a result, even basic statistical information is lacking, and the amount of resources needed to rebuild the national statistical system is so huge that the support from many development partners is crucial to help revive the statistical system. In this regard, as one of the leading agencies supporting the statistical capacity building in developing countries, the Bank involvement is needed to support the ongoing process to rebuild the capacity of the Somali National statistics offices and for production of a minimum set of data crucial to inform the rebuilding of the economy. The Bank’s engagement in Somalia also needs a minimum set of information to inform the decision making. 91. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 96. Statistical capacity building requires integrated, coordinated support. Experience from many countries have shown that fragmented, uncoordinated programs of support do not result in sustainable improvements in statistical production and dissemination. By basing its support on the NSDS, this project ensures that support is provided to key areas of the statistical system in a coordinated way and the activities are well sequenced. The project also supports specific measures to strengthen coordination, collaboration, networking, and information sharing among stakeholders. 97. Projects are often not sustainable once Development Partners support ends. Reviews of past statistical development projects show that the beneficial impacts of projects tend to be limited to the lifetime of the project. Thus, activities and human resource capacity enhanced by these projects often fail to be sustained due to the absence of a long-term strategy and a well-planned integration mechanism. This project reflects this lesson by assisting the relevant governments to implement the institutional and legal reforms contained in their respective national statistics strategies; both strategies have broad ownership by providers and users of official statistics, a supportive legal and institutional framework, and an implementation plan and governance arrangements that emphasize transparency, managerial accountability, and results. 98. Limited demand and quality data. Due to prolonged conflict and lack of central government, data supply for users in Somalia has been limited. Without strong and independent NSO responsible for not only the production of official statistics but also the supervision and coordination for the national statistical system, there is a danger of an overlap in the statistical data collection and compromised quality of data by lack of consistency and harmonization of data collection process and procedures. UN agencies and NGOs with the support of local public and private institutions have been providing basic services in Somalia since the fall of the Central Government in 1991. As a result, they provide data for humanitarian activities and projects. However, these data are not coordinated nor standardized in the context of Somalia. The long-term sustainability of the project’s intervention depends on both the technical and financial sustainability of the NSS and political situation in the country. The project will contribute to the technical sustainability by strengthening the human capacity of statistics, national M&E activities, macroeconomics and planning, as well as fiscal Departments through traditional learning programs and through learning by doing. Strengthening national statistical capacity is about enhancing the ability of a country to produce and disseminate data that are ‘fit for purpose.’ In addition to trainings, main elements of NSS that need to be enhanced to ensure sustainability of systems include statistical legislation, standards and classifications, frameworks, statistical methods, guidelines, business register, sampling frames, quality assurance, database structures, GISs, and statistical policies. The project’s activities include many such elements of sustainability. The project will establish some of the foundations of the NSS and has the potential to be scaled-up upon successful completion of the first phase and if there are increased financial resources (either through IDA or other donors). Regarding the financial sustainability, creating user demand for statistics within and outside the Government is expected to add pressure for sustained budgetary support from the Government. Following years of sustained effort to re-establish basic economic governance, Somalia has initiated a debt relief process by reaching a decision point for the HIPC Initiative. The project will strengthen the capacity of the Government to support evidence-based policy, through the improvement of the National Statistical System to fill statistical data gaps; monitor the implementation of poverty and vulnerability reduction programs. The economic analysis for this project considers the counterfactual without a World Bank-funded statistical project. While the government and other partners’ demand for improved statistical capacity and filling data gaps is clear, it is the combination of financing and capacity building that makes this proposed project a cost-effective solution to address this demand. In absence of this project, the capacity of the Somali NSS, as well as economics and planning units will remain low, existing major data gaps in areas such as the CPI, poverty measurements, and national accounts, will take considerably longer to fill, or may even widen. With support from the project, not only will these data gaps be filled on time, but also the respective statistical operations will be undertaken more cost-effectively because of building technical capacity within DNS and statistical units in MDAs, which will allow for the adoption of more efficient and cost- effective methods and techniques (for example, CAPI-based data collection in surveys). The timely availability of the data will lead to evidence-based assessment of the socioeconomic situation in the country, will provide data for a more effective M&E of projects and programs included in NDP9 and other sectoral plans, and help to design more appropriate policies, planning and programs. 112. The project will also strengthen the capacity of various statistics, M&E, economics, planning and fiscal functions in MDAs with the view to produce better tools and instruments for better informed decision making about the rebuilding of the economy. This will also provide effective means that will open space to improve projects and programs formulation based on evidence, which, in turn, is expected to improve the quality and well-being of the Somali population. 113. The provision of statistical data is a public good. While its economic quantification of the benefits of the project and the recovery of the funds invested is not possible, the public good nature of the availability of reliable and timely statistics is key for accountability, transparency and efficient public management. A trustworthy national planning and monitoring system, including civil society, donors, government agencies and citizen in their construction, reduce transaction costs among socio-economic actors for economic transactions and the implementation of public policies. Additionally, a clear framework and strategy to reduce poverty will be developed and public projects and programs will be regularly monitored and adjusted to attain goals defined. This project will strengthen the capacity of MDAs to measure efficacy and efficiency of their public intervention, as well as the preparation of regular reports of the performance of public management. Beneficiaries of the project include the regional integration process, the public and private sectors, civil society, development partners, and the public at large. One of the challenges facing Somalia is to prioritize and fast track the strengthening of the NSS so that it can provide the needed statistical data to support national recovery and reconstruction efforts while at the same time protecting the confidentiality of the individual data providers (i.e. persons and business establishments that provide statistical data). Best practice internationally requires that this is done in a holistic and coordinated manner by developing a legal and data sharing framework. Strengthening of the country’s Statistics Act is a fundamental prerequisite for an effective NSS as it provides legitimacy and transparency to statistical operations and provide public confidence in official statistics. 2. Somalia has initiated steps towards strengthening its statistical confidentiality and micro data sharing framework. When operationalized, the new Statistics Act will enhance microdata access, while at the same time protecting confidentiality of the data providers. The new Statistics Act (Chapter 9) adequately addresses this issue and will also establish the Somali National Statistics Bureau as an autonomous national statistical authority to make production and management of official statistics in the country more efficient and effective. 3. The FGS is also in the process of developing data dissemination policy that will be used to increase microdata access, while at the same time protecting confidentiality of statistical data maintained by various producers of official statistics. A draft policy has been prepared in line with international best practice, and therefore will be able to facilitate user access to data. The Somali statistics authorities have also agreed to set up a mechanism to strenghten coordination of statistical activities across the (NSS) by engaging all producers of official data. Sector Statistics Working Groups (SWGs) will be formed, where data producers will plan and coordinate policy and activities relating to their sectors. Formation of SWGs are an important forum for the national statistics office to fill its role as national coordinator of statistics, and to ensure that statistic products based on administrative data systems are being produced and disseminated according to international best practice. 4. The recent past years have been marked by significant reforms including legislative and regulatory framework of statistical activities that are mentioned above. More than 4,500 trained data collectors and over twenty data analysts are available across the country; National Statistics Office has increased its number of staff, including professional statisticians, economists and demographer. With these developments and continued effort by the statistics authority to engage and coordinate stakeholders at all levels of government, NSS is expected to take shape during the implementation of this project. 5. The project will support activities to strengthen data management and dissemination practices to ensure statistical confidentiality and microdata sharing. In future projects, various coordination tools can also be developed and promoted for use across the NSS including: (i) Code of Practice: to be designed and promoted across the NSS to ensure that trustworthy statistics are produced across the NSS; (ii) Sector Statistics Plans; to encourage sectors not having them to design and implement these plans aligned to the NSDS. Page 71 of 79 The World Bank Somali Integrated Statistics and Economic Planning Capacity Building (P171160) ANNEX 7: Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey 1. The Somali High Frequency Survey provided a key dataset for monitoring poverty and contributed to other key statistical indicators. The World Bank designed the SHFS system to monitor poverty and other indicators for the Somali population. The first wave was implemented in spring 2016 in collaboration with a contractor and Somali statistical authorities. The survey filled an important data gap and allowed for the first estimation of poverty for the Somali population, but important gaps remained. The rural sample frame was designed ad-hoc and coverage was limited excluding large insecure areas as well as the nomadic population. This resulted in the sample representing 40 percent of the population. The second wave of the SHFS—implemented in December 2017—improved significantly on representativeness by expanding coverage to additional geographic areas and by including the nomadic population, which represents one quarter of the population according to the 2014 PESS. 2. The Somali Integrated Household Budget Survey is a continuation on the SHFS that will provide more precise estimates of consumption and poverty due to lower levels of commodity disaggregation and a larger sample size. The SHFS estimates consumption using a Rapid Consumption Methodology that combines an innovative questionnaire design with standard imputation techniques. The abbreviated nature of the household consumption module in the SHFS saves time in survey administration and makes it easier to obtain updated poverty estimates. However, the design implies the consumption values of some items are either not captured or must be imputed for the rest of the sample when captured for some sub-groups. The SIHBS will instead employ the traditional full consumption survey to obtain information on a larger set of products which will provide higher levels of recorded consumption. The SIHBS will be administered over a relatively larger sample size which would produce estimates of population parameters of higher precision. SIHBS special characteristics 3. SIHBS will be conducted and processed by the National Statistics Office (NSO) with some technical assistance from Statistics Sweden. This is a stark difference from SHFS, which was conducted and processed by a private firm with support from the World Bank and the NSO. To prepare for a full PRSP, it is critical to create a sustainable system to track poverty and other key indicators in Somalia. To make the monitoring sustainable, SIHBS will be managed by the NSO. 4. Unlike SHFS, SIHBS will include a full consumption module. To reduce interview time, SHFS reduces the number of items in the consumption module and fills the gaps by a statistical imputation technique based on Machine Learning. However, such an imputation technique is rare in the developing world and implementation of the technique is too complex to sustain. Therefore, SIHBS will adopt a more traditional data collection approach–collecting consumption data using a full consumption module. It will take more interview time than SHFS but requires enumerators with much more standard data collection skills, who can be relatively easily built even in Somalia. 5. SIHBS will integrate an agricultural module to gather the necessary data for the compilation of GDP by production approach for the household sector. Agriculture is an important economic activity in Somalia for food production, employment and income generation. Capturing information on the production of subsistence agriculture is of critical importance in national accounts especially in developing countries where households produce most food for the domestic market. However, information of agricultural production at the household level is currently lacking for Somalia. Due to the NSO’s limited capacity, instead of carrying out a separate agriculture survey, it is thought more appropriate to include the agricultural module in SIHBS. An advantage of including the agricultural module in SIHBS is that it will help us verify consumption from own production in the consumption module using data from the agricultural Page 72 of 79 The World Bank Somali Integrated Statistics and Economic Planning Capacity Building (P171160) module. Since SIHBS will not cover agricultural production by enterprises, it is important to supplement the data from SIHBS with additional data collection from agricultural firms.22 SIHBS will include modules for livestock, fisheries and forestry. 6. SIHBS will include a household enterprise module to gather information necessary for the compilation of GDP by production approach. In countries such as Somalia, the formal sector is nascent and has a limited absorption capacity, thus most of the workers are absorbed in the informal sector. While agricultural activities are far more important for rural households, in urban areas, non-agricultural household economic activities in the informal sector are likely to be of most importance. Since much of this production is likely to be conducted within a household not by a formal enterprise, household surveys are more likely to capture the activities than business surveys. 7. SIHBS will include IDPs and nomadic populations in addition to private Somali households based on the sampling frame created from the satellite images. The sample will be designed to produce representative national and sub-national statistics for major geographical areas and areas of residence.23 8. In the absence of a recent population census, the sampling frame will be based on a combination of urban primary sampling units demarcated for the purposes of PESS and rural primary sampling units derived from an updated sampling frame based on satellite imagery. Somalis have endured a long spell of absence of a population census which would normally provide a basis for a sampling frame. The last census was conducted during 1985/86, but the results were never released to the public. The last publicly available information is from a census in 1975. Somalis have not had a comprehensive sampling frame in over four decades. 13. This activity will update the sampling frame, from which a sample will be selected using a standard two stage sampling method. A sampling frame is necessary to ensure the household survey is representative of the population from which it was drawn. While the PESS provides a sampling frame for urban areas, its sampling frame overestimates the population in some enumeration areas and is incomplete with respect to rural areas. Also, PESS does not include the nomadic population or up to date IDP settlements. This project will update the sampling frame for urban areas and rural areas to address the shortcomings of the PESS sampling frame and add the sampling frame for nomads and IDPs. The sampling frame for IDPs will be created from the satellite images and household registries for IDP camps in Central Regions, Jubaland Administration, South West Administration, Galmudug, Puntland, Somaliland as well as in Banadir. Nomads represent an estimated 25 percent of the Somali population according the PESS and their inclusion in the sampling frame is necessary to ensure representativeness of future surveys. The tracking data generated from the second wave of the SHFS can be exploited to construct the sampling frame for nomads. 14. A standard two stage sampling method will be used to design the sample from the sampling frame and before selecting sample households, a ground truthing exercise to verify the satellite imagery and a listing of households in selected primary sampling units will be conducted. The sample design will be based on a multi-stage stratified random sampling to produce nationally and sub-nationally representative samples. This is an efficient and economical method that provides the desired level of precision. Representativeness can be guaranteed and estimates of population parameters can be produced for each sub-population. The population will be divided into distinct groups of interest (or strata) based on geographical location. The proposed first level of stratification is by pre-war region. The second level of stratification is by area of residence within the regions i.e. urban areas, rural areas, IDP settlements and nomadic “rest points” at the time of the survey. Following stratification, Primary Sampling Units (PSU) will be selected in the first stage with probability proportional to size then households will be randomly selected within each PSU. The sample size for the SIHBS will be determined by the accuracy required for the survey estimates for each stratum, as well as by the logistical, timing and resource constraints . Given the challenge of collecting accurate information on household expenditures and income, the overall sample size should be manageable for quality and operational control purposes. The sample size is however expected to be larger than that of the SHFS. Wave 1 of the SHFS surveyed 4,117 households and Wave 2 is expected to survey 4,500 households. The sample size for Wave 2 was determined to be sufficient to retrieve consumption estimates with less than 20 percent of relative standard errors for the first level stratification (regions) and less than 5 percent for the second level stratification (urban, rural, IDPs and water point areas which are the usual dwelling points for nomads). Since a smaller sample error is indicative of more reliable results, the goal for the SIHBS would be to lower the relative standard errors for both levels of stratification keeping in mind that the diminishing returns to increased sample size. Questionnaire Design 16. The questionnaire design will reflect the traditional and central purpose of a Household Budget Survey, which is the study of household expenditures. SIHBS will use a standard consumption module for collecting household expenditure data. SHFS covers 236 items (110 food items, 89 non-food items, and 37 durables) and to save time, each household does not have to report around 20 items, expenditures of which will be imputed using a Machine Learning technique. SIHBS includes around 300 items so that the data can be used for providing budget shares for CPIs and does not adopt the imputation approach used in SHFS. The consumption module will be finalized after discussing with the CPI team of the NSO. 17. The SIHBS questionnaire will adopt the content and structure of the SHFS questionnaires except for new modules on agriculture production, and household enterprise. The content of the questionnaire will be driven by the purpose of the survey and policy needs. SIHBS is a multi-topic household survey and to maintain some comparability with SHFS, it will adopt the questionnaire of the SHFS in case that is possible. However, as discussed above, SIHBS needs to collect some additional information to provide data for improving the GDP data, and will therefore include new modules for agricultural production and household enterprise. Data collection will involve face-to-face interviews using CAPI. CAPI is software for designing questionnaire to collect data with Android handheld devices like smartphones or tablets. With CAPI, data collected are uploaded to the Page 75 of 79 The World Bank Somali Integrated Statistics and Economic Planning Capacity Building (P171160) server of NSO in STATA or Excel format whenever enumerators get the access to WiFi or 3G or a higher level of connection. CAPI has some useful features to improve the quality of data collection. For example, CAPI can include built- in validation that prompts alerts to interviewers when they enter potential outliers or commit mistakes. CAPI can also automate skip patterns and filters and include auto-fill features. All of these features minimize the risk of entering wrong answers or incorrectly providing data entry for the wrong case. Furthermore, supervision is facilitated by CAPI since the devices can be integrated with GPS tracking and early data transfer allows supervisors to monitor results and offer faster feedback. 20. A field pre-test will be conducted before the implementation of the fieldwork to evaluate the survey questionnaire and test the CAPI system. This quality control procedure helps to fine tune field procedures and the instrument. Potential logistical problems can be identified early and mitigated. Testing of the data entry program in a field setting will help reveal overlooked quality checks. Prior to the pre-test, the survey design and the questionnaire contained on the handheld devices will be subjected to several checks—including range checks, skip checks, consistency checks, typographic checks, and checks against reference data—which will again be refined after the field test if needed. Some preliminary statistical analysis may also reveal areas where field staff need re-training. 21.
