Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Developing El Salvador’s abundant renewable energy (RE) resources is key to boosting inclusive and sustainable economic development and supporting increased climate resilience. El Salvador is endowed with a wealth of RE resources, including geothermal, the production of which can help lower electricity costs and increase resilience to climate change. The Government’s National Integral Development Strategy aims to accelerate investment in resilient and sustainable infrastructure, including clean energy projects.1 Increasing the country’s geothermal power production will help secure more affordable electricity for the population and businesses, supporting growth and job creation. The direct use of geothermal heat for productive applications can also spur local economic development. In addition, increased geothermal electricity production reduces vulnerability to high oil prices and contributes to climate change mitigation and adaptation by reducing GHG emissions and creating a more diversified and resilient electricity matrix. 2. In recent years, El Salvador has worked to modernize its institutions and improve security, but significant development challenges persist. The COVID-19 pandemic hit El Salvador hard, with Gross Domestic Product (GDP) dropping 7.9 percent in 2020 despite major fiscal outlays. The country continues to face a delicate macroeconomic context linked to the debt burden exacerbated by the COVID-19 crisis which contributed to the still fragile fiscal 1 Consejo Nacional para el Desarrollo Sostenible (CNDS) (2023). “Estrategia Nacional de Desarrollo Integral (ENDI)�? Economic recovery following this crisis was impacted by high inflation stimulated by rising global commodity prices, including fuel prices. While the country has made progress in recent years in reducing poverty and crime, there are still significant challenges that need to be addressed to ensure the sustainability of these gains. Investing in increased geothermal power capacity to help lower electricity costs and reduce vulnerability to fuel price volatility will be key to sustaining economic recovery. 3. The country has made progress reducing overall poverty and boosting shared prosperity, but the impact of the pandemic and subsequent price shocks presents a risk to sustaining this progress. The percentage of Salvadoreans living with less than US$6.85/day (2017 PPP) dropped from below 52 percent in 2000 to below 28 percent in 2022. Nevertheless, a World Bank poverty assessment released in December 2024 shows that, based on country estimates, approximately 30.3 percent of the population is estimated to be living in poor households. This is 3.5 percentage points higher than before the pandemic (2019). The situation is being somewhat mitigated by remittances, without which extreme poverty would likely increase. Remittances, which accounted for over 24 percent of GDP in 2023, are key for Salvadoran families to improve living conditions and development.2 However, they also have counterproductive effects, like lower labor force participation rates and hours worked by the beneficiaries.3 Boosting human capital across the country, including in technical fields like the energy and geothermal sector, would contribute to more sustainable and inclusive economic development. Creating more domestic employment opportunities in the renewable energy and geothermal sector, including for women, and economic opportunities for local communities near geothermal projects can also help to increase labor force participation in El Salvador. 4. El Salvador’s GDP grew by 3.5 percent in 2023 fueled by public investment and consumption. Real GDP per capita has grown at a modest rate averaging 2.1 percent between 2000 and 2023, and per capita GDP in absolute terms was US$5,344 in 2023. The country’s GDP is expected to grow 2.9 percent in 2024, and afterwards moderate to 2.5 percent by 2026 largely due to limited measures to address fiscal and external imbalances as well as other structural obstacles. The country faces various macroeconomic challenges tied to debt sustainability. The overall fiscal deficit is expected to be around 4.5 percent of GDP in 2024. The primary fiscal deficit is forecast to decrease slightly in 2024. These macroeconomic challenges have reduced the amount of public funds available for critical infrastructure investments, including in the geothermal sector, which could help sustain economic growth. 5. El Salvador is also highly vulnerable to climate change-related hazards, incurring annual losses of around 2.5 percent of GDP, which threatens the country’s economic growth and development. Located in the Central American dry corridor, or “Corredor Seco�?, climate change impacts are expected to aggravate the current seasonal dry periods and cyclical droughts resulting from El Niño. Worldwide, El Salvador ranks second highest for risk exposure to two or more hazards, and the highest for total population at a relatively high risk of mortality.4 The weather-dependent nature of various sectors, including the power sector (hydropower), agriculture, and fisheries, which are essential to sustaining the country’s growth and poverty reduction, is a critical challenge that the country must tackle. Greater diversification of the electricity matrix so that it is less dependent on hydropower generation, including through increased investment in geothermal power generation capacity, is critical to enhancing power system resilience to climate risks. El Salvador’s geothermal potential for electricity generation is estimated at 500-700 MWe, of which 209 MWe is currently being used at the geothermal fields Ahuachapán (95 MWe), Berlin (109 MWe), and San Vicente (5 MWe). Installed geothermal power capacity generated 1,478 GWh in 2023, contributing to approximately 20 percent of the country’s electricity needs.6 Further tapping into the 300-500MWe7 of undeveloped geothermal resources in El Salvador would significantly contribute to meeting the government’s objectives to scale -up renewable electricity generation, lower costs, and reduce emissions. 7. El Salvador’s electricity sector is vertically unbundled with an active private sector. The electricity sector is governed by the 1996 General Electricity Law and regulated by the General Superintendence of Electricity and Telecommunications [Superintendencia General de Electricidad y Telecomunicaciones (SIGET)]. In 2022, the government consolidated various agencies to create the General Directorate of Energy, Hydrocarbons, and Mines [Dirección General de Energia, Hidrocarburos y Minas (DGEHM)], that is responsible for overall energy policy. The electricity market is made up of 113 participants, including generators, distributors, and energy traders. There are 36 generators (3 are state-owned), and 8 distribution companies in the country, only one of which belongs to the state (DEC). The state-owned National Electric Grid Entity [Ente Nacional de Transmisión Eléctrica (ENTE)], oversees the power transmission network while the state-owned conglomerate Comisión Ejecutiva Hidroeléctrica del Río Lempa (CEL) operates the public hydropower facilities. LaGeo, an enterprise in which CEL is a majority stakeholder, operates the country’s geothermal power plants. El Salvador has a competitive wholesale electricity market where both private and public entities participate. 8. Electricity supply in El Salvador comes from a mix of renewables and fossil fuels and electricity imports. El Salvador had a total installed power capacity of 2,914 MWe in 2023, derived from a combination of hydropower, geothermal, biomass, solar PV, wind, and thermal sources. Between 2011 and 2016, the share of total electricity production generation from hydropower plants declined significantly as a share of total electricity production. To meet electricity demand, which has grown at an annual rate of 2-3 percent, the country imported up to a quarter of its power needs from the regional electricity market (MER) through the SIEPAC interconnected grid. However, starting in 2016, domestic generation was ramped up, first with the addition of hydro and solar power capacity (adding over 800 MWe between 2016 and 2023) and more recently with the addition of a 390 MWe natural gas power plant. These additions have helped reduce imports and made El Salvador a net exporter of power in 2023. However, the country is now using fossil fuels (natural gas and oil) to meet 42 percent of its electricity supply needs. 9. Climate change is expected to negatively impact rainfall patterns in El Salvador, increasing the intensity and frequency of droughts and reducing the volume and reliability of hydropower generation. While El Salvador still generates a significant amount of electricity from renewables (56 percent), many of these sources (solar, wind, biomass, and hydro) provide variable supply given hourly and seasonal variations in resource availability. In addition, hydropower generation (responsible for 20 percent of electricity supply in 2023), is being affected by a marked decrease in rainfall patterns exacerbated by climate change, especially in the country’s Dry Corridor. Reduced hydropower supply during the dry season was especially apparent during the El Niño event in 2023, where lower water availability combined with new gas-fired power generation reduced the share of hydropower in the electricity generation mix from 36.8 percent in 2020 to 20 percent in 20238. Project Description 13. The proposed project will support the development of the Chinameca Geothermal Field to achieve renewable power generation, create new economic activities for local communities using geothermal heat, and build greater technical capacity in the country. The components of this project are the following: (a) Component 1: Drilling campaign to confirm geothermal resources (IBRD US$80m) – This component will fund a drilling campaign to further confirm the capacity of the geothermal reservoir in the Chinameca field and to secure steam production for a power generation plant that will tap into the larger resource potential of the geothermal reservoir. The drilling campaign will improve the understanding of the geothermal resource and allow for better estimates of the maximum commercially viable power generation capacity in the Chinameca geothermal field. The component will also support technical assessments to confirm the overall steam- production capacity of the geothermal field. (b) Component 2: Investment in Geothermal Electricity Generation Capacity (IBRD US$ 59.6m | LaGeo US$10.7m) – This component will finance the construction of a new geothermal power plant in the Chinameca field with an estimated capacity of 20 – 25 MWe, the steam gathering system, and the connection to the electric substation. This power plant will use the new steam production that will be secured through the drilling conducted under Component 1. LaGeo plans to enter into an Engineering-Procurement-Construction (EPC) contract for building the power plant, that will be supervised by LaGeo’s technical team. The construction of the steam above the ground gathering system (SAGS) will be financed in part using counterpart funding from LaGeo. Page 5 The World Bank El Salvador Geothermal Energy for Sustainable and Inclusive Development (P506109) (c) Component 3: Social and environmental programs and geothermal center of excellence (IBRD US$ 5.3m | LaGeo US$4m) – This component will support the execution of social programs and investments that create local economic opportunities in communities near the power plant, environmental mitigation plans, and studies to support the creation of a geothermal center of excellence. The component will finance both studies and a processing facility that uses residual geothermal heat for processing products for agribusiness or other types of productive economic activities so that local communities can benefit from the geothermal heat. It will also support compliance with environmental and social mitigation and management measures outlined in the Environmental and Social Impact Assessments and related management plans, to facilitate compliance with the World Bank Environmental and Social Framework and national environmental permitting requirements. The component will also finance undertaking studies to inform the creation of a Geothermal Center of Excellence. (d) Component 4: Technical Assistance and Project Implementation Support (IBRD US$ 4.7m | LaGeo US$ 3m) – This component will support LaGeo in strengthening its technical and management capacity to implement the project. This will include hiring expert personnel to provide technical assistance and to support management and supervision tasks, as well as for project monitoring activities. Furthermore, the component will finance external audits, and the implementation of citizen engagement mechanisms. The Environmental and social risks are High, in light of potentially significant direct and indirect impacts both from the directly financed project activities such as drilling of geothermal wells and the construction of a power plant, as well as from the construction of a transmission line and substation, an associated activity not financed under the Project. Additionally, a private sector contractor will construct another geothermal power plant in the Chinameca field for LaGeo in parallel to the Project. Although not considered an associated activity per ESS1, the private contractor will construct the powerplant in the same area of direct influence and will carry out similar activities, presenting some coordination and potential cumulative impact challenges for the Project. The primary environmental, health and safety impacts and risks are expected to include: (a) significant freshwater consumption requirements during the drilling phase, in settings where local communities already experience water shortages during the dry season; (b) deforestation, and resulting habitat fragmentation, from earthworks and civil works related to power plant development activities including installing networks of pipelines to carry steam, hot and cold process waters; developing and improving access roads; and constructing a transmission line; (c) potential for surface and groundwater contamination in the case of improper disposal of drilling muds; and (d) varied construction related impacts and risks typical of infrastructure and industrial projects, including noise, dust, road and traffic safety, occupational health and safety, and potential labor influx related issues (including the resulting additional stress on limited water resources) in the neighboring towns. Relevant positive environmental benefits are also expected as long-term outcomes associated with the operation, including among others, climate change mitigation and cleaner air. The potential social risks and impacts stem largely from: (a) land acquisition, Page 6 The World Bank El Salvador Geothermal Energy for Sustainable and Inclusive Development (P506109) most of which has been finalized prior to project preparation; (b) physical resettlement that is expected to begin in 2025 for 5 households that are identified and will need to be relocated within the same perimeter of their land; c) labor influx from other areas of the country, which may impact existing community dynamics, exert additional pressure on housing and public services, and increase the risk of social issues such as gender-based violence; (d) high expectations within local communities for addressing key concerns, particularly increased access for potable water and improvements in road infrastructure, which are beyond the scope of the Project; (e) high expectations for employment opportunities; and (f) potential misconceptions about project impacts, e.g., on water resources. The project will include social and environmental investments benefiting local communities under component 3. Mitigation measures for risks and impacts associated with geothermal development at Chinameca have been developed and integrated into an Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) with a comprehensive set of Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs) and a Stakeholder Engagement Plan for all planned geothermal related activities at the site. The ESCP includes commitments to undertake an environmental and social assessment for the center of excellence and the investments under component 3 still to be designed in detail, and to undertake an audit to assess compliance with ESS5 for land acquisition that has already taken place. For more information on the management plans and approach, please refer to the Environmental and Social Review Summary (ESRS). E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 15. LaGeo is the implementing agency responsible for executing the proposed project and has long-standing technical expertise in the development, operation and maintenance of geothermal power plants. LaGeo has skilled technical staff, as well as staff with expertise in financial and procurement procedures, and in environmental management with well-structured teams in these areas. The company also holds relevant experience in developing operations like those to be carried out under the project. LaGeo has conducted exploratory work in the Chinameca geothermal field previously, and preliminary investigations in other areas with geothermal potential. A project-specific Project Management Unit (PMU) will be created within LaGeo for the development and implementation of the project. The PMU’s capacity will be further strengthened under the project through training and technical assistance to strengthen the PMU’s capacity to implement the Project in accordance with the World Bank´s procedures and policies. The PMU will be staffed with LaGeo´s personnel and will be reinforced with new hires where needed. LaGeo will oversee implementation of all activities of the Project and will be responsible for results monitoring of project implementation.
