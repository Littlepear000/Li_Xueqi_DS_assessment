Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Peru remains one of Latin America’s most stable economies, underpinned by strong macroeconomic frameworks fiscal discipline, inflation targeting, prudent debt management, and ample reserves. Rapid growth during the 2002–2013 commodity super cycle (6.1 percent average Gross domestic product - GDP growth) has slowed to about 3 percent since 2014, reflecting maturing drivers and structural constraints. Despite this resilience, development is limited by high informality, uneven regional development, weak public investment execution, and political instability. 2. Poverty fell markedly from 58.7 percent (2004) to 20.2 percent (2019) but rebounded to 27.5 percent in 2023 post pandemic. The urban–rural divide is stark: 39.3 percent of the rural population lives in poverty compared to 24.8 percent in urban areas, lacking still from basic services. Spatial disparities, particularly between Lima and mining regions, fuel social tensions and erode trust. Consistent with the Systematic Country Diagnostic /Country Partnership Framework (SCD/CPF), well governed mining can help narrow these gaps via revenues, jobs, and infrastructure. 3. Mining is a strategic pillar roughly 10 percent of GDP and over 63 percent of exports; copper alone generated nearly US$23 billion in 2024. Fiscal linkages are significant, with mining transfers to subnational governments exceeding US$2.18 billion in 2024, financing infrastructure and social services. The sector supports formal employment of about 256,000 direct jobs in 2025 (a record), 71 percent via contractors and strong spillovers each direct job creates an estimated 6.25 additional jobs1, bringing total mining related employment close to 1.5 million. Yet private investment has slowed down amid permitting delays, overlapping mandates, and social conflict. Concurrently, the global clean energy transition presents a window of opportunity as the world’s third largest copper producer, Peru can supply critical minerals for renewables, electric vehicles, and grid infrastructure, and diversify into other critical minerals to deepen value chain integration. Capturing these opportunities requires investments that also deliver local spillovers shared infrastructure, skills, resilient supply chains, and inclusive territorial development aligned with climate and community goals. 5. Realizing these potential calls for reform and innovation. With the right enabling environment, Peru could attract around US$1 billion annually in private exploration and US$8 billion in mine development. Priorities include institutional modernization and digitalization; high quality, open precompetitive geoscience; streamlined, well- coordinated permitting across agencies; and robust, inclusive community engagement. These actions can restore investor confidence, reduce conflict risk, and convert Peru’s mineral endowment into sustained, inclusive prosperity anchoring the green transition and long-term territorial development. Sectoral and Institutional Context _For Official Use Only_ 6. Peru holds one of the world’s richest mineral endowments and a pipeline of 66 projects worth over US$63 billion, yet its potential remains underexploited. Regulatory bottlenecks, weak interagency coordination, and unresolved social conflicts have slowed project development and eroded the country’s share of global exploration budgets. Unlocking this potential requires reducing friction in the permitting system, improving institutional efficiency, and addressing trust deficits with local communities. Without these reforms, Peru risks falling behind competitors in attracting the investment needed to transform geological wealth into sustainable growth 7. High-quality geoscientific information is a critical foundation for competitiveness. Instituto Geológico, Minero y Metalúrgico (INGEMMET) has advanced in digitizing and disseminating geological, geochemical, and geophysical data, but important gaps remain in scale, coverage, and timeliness—especially in frontier areas. Compared to leading mining nations, Peru lags in airborne geophysics and thematic mapping that de-risk early exploration and enable land-use, water, and climate planning. Closing these gaps would not only attract junior explorers, who drive most global discoveries, but also strengthen territorial governance by integrating mineral data into infrastructure, environmental, and resilience planning. 8. At the same time, modernizing and digitalizing permitting is essential to restore investor confidence. Current processes are fragmented, unpredictable, and burdensome, with inefficiencies across environmental licensing, water rights, and consultation procedures. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has warned that these delays constrain Peru’s growth prospects and stall critical mineral projects. While some reforms have been introduced, deeper integration and digital solutions are needed to reduce approval times, lower transaction costs, and create a predictable investment climate. Strengthening environmental, social, and governance (ESG) alignment, particularly on cumulative impacts, carbon reporting, gender inclusion, and indigenous rights, will also be vital to secure responsible markets and unlock access to green finance. 9. Finally, the future of mining legitimacy in Peru hinges on two pillars: stronger territorial presence and inclusive participation. Social conflicts remain widespread, driven by weak state presence and unfulfilled commitments. Project Description 12. The project comprises three components aligned with the PDO: (1) Geological knowledge management; (2) Adequate management of mining investments; and (3) Project management. 13. Component 1 (US$163.91 million: US$126.41 million IBRD, US$37.50 million co-financing) will modernize Peru’s geoscience knowledge as an open public good and reinforce INGEMMET’s role by expanding high-quality, interoperable datasets for cross-sectoral use. • Subcomponent 1.1 establishes a national drill core library in Lima to centralize, preserve, digitize, and open access to dispersed cores and samples, including acquiring an urban plot, constructing a specialized facility, procuring advanced scanning and imaging equipment, cataloging collections under standardized protocols, integrating with Geoportal de Catastro Minero del Perú (GEOCATMIN), and training staff. • Subcomponent 1.2 finances precompetitive geophysical and geochemical data to close critical gaps in high- Andean areas through airborne surveys over 310,698 km² (at 500 m and 250 m line spacings), field mapping and sampling over 128,645 km², and advanced processing (mineral systems, predictive modeling, AI), with applications for groundwater, hazards, infrastructure, and soils; activities include contracting specialized Page 4 Official Use Only The World Bank Competitiveness and Sustainability in the Strategic Minerals Sector (P510607) services, systematic low-density sampling, integrating and publishing datasets, strengthening INGEMMET’s data capabilities, and disseminating via GEOCATMIN. • Subcomponent 1.3 creates a state-of-the-art geochronology laboratory at INGEMMET to deliver high- precision dating (e.g., U-Pb), reducing reliance on foreign labs; it will adapt lab space, procure MC-ICP-MS with laser ablation and cathodoluminescence imaging, implement quality control and staff training, define sustainable service and maintenance plans, and integrate outputs into GEOCATMIN. 14. Component 2 (US$77.30 million: US$53.20 million IBRD, US$24.10 million co-financing) strengthens Ministerio de Energía y Minas (MINEM)’s governance via internal digital transformation, an interinstitutional digital one-stop shop for permits (Ventanilla Única Digital Informativa del Sector Minería - VUD MINEM), and technical assistance centers for ASM, to reduce permitting times, improve oversight and coordination, and promote responsible mining. • Subcomponent 2.1 digitalizes and standardizes MINEM’s core procedures through an interoperable platform with real-time tracking, automated workflows for permitting and inspections, robust cybersecurity, data-driven decision-making, staff training, and transparency mechanisms. • Subcomponent 2.2 designs, pilots, and implements the VUD MINEM to interconnect more than ten permitting entities from exploration to closure, integrating modules for water-use authorizations, _For Official Use Only_ Indigenous Peoples prior consultation, social monitoring, and commitment tracking, based on administrative simplification, interoperability, and open data, with training and phased national rollout. • Subcomponent 2.3 expands Centros de Asistencia Técnica y Articulación Territorial (CATAT)s to improve productivity, environmental performance, safety, and inclusion in ASM, forging partnerships with regional governments, associations, and the private sector, establishing tracking and evaluation, mainstreaming gender and youth, and in four ASM-intensive regions financing diagnostics, operating and sustainability models, urban plots and facilities, equipment, staffing and training, tailored curricula, and pilot programs with clear targets. 15. The Project’s environmental risk rating is Moderate. Anticipated impacts are site-specific, predictable, and reversible, and can be addressed through well-established measures. Construction of the national drill core library and training centers (CATATs) may generate dust, noise, waste, traffic, and occupational health and safety risks, all of which will be managed through subcomponent-specific Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs), chance finds procedures, and contractor obligations to comply with robust safety standards. Aerial surveys and geochemical sampling are low risk but will explicitly exclude protected areas and be governed by occupational health and safety, emergency response, and incident management plans. The geochronology laboratory poses limited radiological risks, mitigated by sitting in a secure INGEMMET facility, employing trained staff, and applying radiation safety protocols. E- waste from digital modernization will be managed under a dedicated plan, while CATATs will implement efficient water _For Official Use Only_ use, wastewater, and solid waste management measures. 17. The social risk rating is Substantial, reflecting risks tied to geoscientific surveys, construction activities, and support to artisanal and small-scale mining. Key challenges include potential misinformation and unrealistic expectations at the community level, the potential exclusion of vulnerable groups, and inadequate communication regarding CATATs. Additional risks arise from labor practices, including informal work arrangements, discrimination, and risks of sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH)—as well as community health and safety during construction and fieldwork. For this Project, given the nature of the activities involved as well as the context in which they will be carried out, the SEA/SH risk is currently assessed as Moderate. To address these, the Project will implement a Stakeholder Engagement Plan with culturally appropriate and inclusive consultation, particularly with Indigenous Peoples in the Sierra. Labor Management Procedures will formalize employment standards, codes of conduct, and grievance mechanisms, while a GBV Action Plan will set out prevention and response protocols to be applied by contractors. Community health and safety will be reinforced through transport safety provisions, emergency preparedness, and strict contractual requirements. As part of preparation, a stakeholder workshop was held on August 15, 2025, at the Ministry of Energy and Mines in Lima. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 18. Project implementation will be anchored in a single PIU within MINEM, which will manage fiduciary responsibilities, overall coordination, and the implementation of Components 2 and 3, and will oversee Component 1 executed by INGEMMET through a specialized technical execution team. Oversight will be provided by a high-level Steering Committee chaired by the Vice Minister of Mines with participation from Ministry of Economy and Finance (MEF), MINEM’s Planning and Budget Office, and INGEMMET, responsible for approving work plans, budgets, and resolving disputes.
