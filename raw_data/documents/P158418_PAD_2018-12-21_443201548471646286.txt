STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Turkey achieved commendable economic and social development results starting in the early 2000s, raising it to the world’s 17th largest economy and establishing it as a global presence. Macroeconomic stability, broad social and economic reforms, and a transformation of a significant part of the economy away from agriculture into manufacturing and services were core contributors to Turkey’s growth. Turkey also weathered the global economic crisis of 2008/09 well: real GDP growth averaged 7.3 percent per year between 2010 and 2015 driven by favorable external financial conditions and strong domestic demand. However, Turkey’s development achievements have been challenged in recent years by a series of domestic and external shocks. Since 2015, Turkey has experienced several election cycles, a failed coup attempt (July 2016), a referendum on major Constitutional changes (April 2017), and a transition to a Presidential system (June 2018). The continued difficult geopolitical environment in the region, stemming from the Syrian crisis amongst other issues, also had a negative impact. While the economy remained resilient in 2015-2017, persistent imbalances driven by dependence on energy imports and insufficient domestic savings contributed to increased economic stress and uncertainty. The Government’s efforts to stimulate the economy in this period culminated in 2018 with very high inflation, a large current account deficit, intense exchange rate volatility and sharp currency depreciation. In the near-term, the economy is projected to slow down but make progress in rebalancing, whilst current account deficit and inflation are projected to gradually decline to lower levels. 2. Turkey’s current Government was formed in July 2018, following national elections held in June 2018. In September 2018, the Government launched the National Economic Program (NEP) which sets out Turkey’s macro-fiscal and structural reform strategy and is a comprehensive statement of the Government’s policy intent for the near-term. The NEP is being implemented while the Government prepares Turkey’s 11th Development Plan (for the 2019-2023 period) which will set out the longer term structural reforms to be pursued in order to address Turkey’s development challenges such as improving productivity, tackling a low female labor force participation rate that hampers sustained growth, and building the skills of its population to reap the benefits of greater global integration. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Irrigation Development: Turkey has 28 million hectares of arable land, and while the agricultural sector accounts for about two-thirds of the country’s water use, its overall contribution to GDP has steadily declined from 18 percent in 1990 to 8 percent in 2014. However, agriculture still employs 21 percent of the population, and accounts for 60 percent of the rural workforce. Poverty is higher in rural areas, affecting one third of the rural population, in contrast with the poverty incidence of 11 percent in urban areas. Extreme poverty in Turkey is mostly a rural phenomenon, with 80 percent of the extreme poor living in rural locations. The relatively high rural population (approx. 27 percent) in Turkey and its primary dependence on agriculture point to low levels of productivity, stemming mainly from fragmented land, arid and semi-arid agro-climatic conditions, and sub-optimal irrigation and agricultural practices. Agriculture in Turkey is heavily dependent on irrigation, which triples productivity compared with rain-fed agriculture. Over the past 40 years, successive Governments have tried to mitigate the uneven distribution Page 10 of 88 The World Bank Turkey Irrigation Modernization Project (P158418) of water resources and rainfall by increasing access to irrigation, as a result of which the gross irrigated area has grown from 2.3 million hectares in the 1970s to 6.2 million hectares today. Expansion and modernization of irrigation, along with other investments for increasing agricultural productivity, remain a priority agenda for Turkey. 4. Irrigation Performance: Despite large investments in irrigation expansion, Turkey has yet to reach its full efficiency and agricultural productivity potential. Most schemes are based on open channels, where water losses from leakage and evaporation are high compared to closed systems, especially if the channels are not well-maintained. Significant water loss occurs also at the field-level, since almost 90 percent of the irrigation area in Turkey has widespread use of inefficient surface irrigation methods (such as flooding, furrow, and border irrigation). The majority of Turkish farming enterprises are still small holdings or family farms (almost two-thirds are smaller than five hectares), and there is a high degree of fragmentation. Poor on-farm water management practices, land fragmentation, and inefficient crop patterns (often supported by subsidy mechanisms) are some of the constraints in translating irrigation provision into higher agricultural productivity and farmer incomes. 5. Water Resources and Climate Change: Turkey’s rapid growth of the last two decades has caused its environmental footprint to increase rapidly. The total GHG emissions rose from 208 MtCO2e (in 1990) to 496 MtCO2e in 2016, with agriculture sector accounting for 11 percent of the total emissions in 2013. Water is amongst the key resources that could become a binding constraint on growth. Due to its large population and already high levels of water resource use, Turkey faces a significant water security threat from climate change, which will manifest through potential drying associated with the rising temperatures, changes in precipitation patterns, and reduced seasonal snow storage. In general, shifting precipitation patterns from snowfall to rainfall and faster melting snow covers are expected to lead to water shortages in elevated areas where the snow storage plays a crucial role in regulating water supply throughout the year. According to a 2016 Climate Change assessment (Ministry of Forestry and Water Affairs, 2016), exploitable water resources would decrease from the estimated 1121 billion m3 to about 86 billion m3 by the 2050s, in the optimistic scenario. However, in a worst-case scenario, water demand may exceed Turkey’s exploitable water level as early as the 2030s. Irrigated agriculture, which consumes 73 percent of the country’s overall exploitable water, would be the hardest hit, which would in turn affect the country’s economy. Since Turkey’s concrete steps to encourage performance improvements in water supply and wastewater reuse would only offset demand by 1-2 billion m3, a significant effort is needed to increase irrigation efficiency and agriculture productivity. 6. Land degradation: Almost 59 percent of the agriculture land in Turkey is affected by different intensities of degradation, primarily linked to the challenges of poor water management and topography. The major causes of degradation of agricultural lands include unsuitable agriculture practices and inappropriate irrigation, leading to erosion, salinization, and desertification. In order to combat land degradation, Turkey has adopted voluntary targets for Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN), under the UN Convention for Combating Desertification (UNCCD). These targets include increasing the area under modernized and efficient irrigation systems. Improving irrigation in farmlands will, in addition to generating economic benefits, reduce desertification risk and improve water availability for other uses. 1 This is Turkey’s official estimate (From DSI: http://en.dsi.gov.tr/land-water-resources). Institutional arrangements: Irrigation systems have been well developed in Turkey, by Devlet Su İşleri (DSI), which is the state agency responsible for water resources planning, development, and management, including most of the irrigation schemes in the country. Since 1993, the Government has significantly accelerated the program for transferring the operation and maintenance (O&M) of irrigation systems to local irrigation institutions, which in most cases are Water User Associations (WUAs). Currently, the WUAs manage irrigation O&M in 89 percent of the total irrigated land in Turkey. While DSI was affiliated with the previous Ministry of Forestry and Water Affairs (MoFWA) and charged with the provision of common irrigation network, the previous Ministry of Food, Agriculture, and Livestock (MoFAL) was traditionally responsible for (i) land consolidation; (ii) supporting farmers in adoption of efficient on-farm systems; and (iii) providing agricultural extension and marketing services. As a part of the significant restructuring in June 2018 that reduced the number of ministries from 25 to 16, MoFWA and MoFAL have been merged into a single Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry (MoAF). These changes did not have any impact on the internal organization of DSI, including the regional and local structures, and it remains a state agency as before, but now affiliated with the newly integrated MoAF. 8. Irrigation Modernization: DSI started irrigation modernization in 2008, thereby focusing on converting the water delivery networks to pressurized, closed-channel (pipe-based) systems, instead of rehabilitating the traditional, open-channel systems. This approach reduces water losses in conveyance, and allows the use of high-efficiency on-farm irrigation systems such as drip and sprinklers. Irrigation modernization is being implemented on a demand-based model. To date, WUAs’ interest in irrigation modernization has been quite high, and 185 applications for modernization have been received since 2008. However, a number of WUAs suffer from low technical, managerial and financial capacity, leading to a vicious cycle whereby inadequate performance of the irrigation infrastructure and on-farm systems leads to low incomes, thereby constraining the farmers’ ability to make productive investments. 9. Irrigation in Turkey’s National Transformation Program: Improving irrigation provision and performance is a priority area for the Government of Turkey, and its 10th Development Plan includes a “Transformational Program” (2014-2018) for improving the efficiency of water use in agriculture. The targets of this program include: increasing the net irrigation area under operation by almost 30 percent, from 2.91 m ha to 3.75 m ha; raising irrigation efficiency from 42 to 50 percent; expanding the use of water saving modern irrigation systems by 10 percent per year; and decreasing the use of groundwater by 5 percent during the plan period. The transition to closed irrigation systems will be accelerated, to cover at least 25 percent of the irrigated lands under modern water-saving irrigation systems. The Action Plan prepared by the previous Ministry of Forestry and Water Affairs (MoFWA) for improving water use in agriculture identifies the following priorities: (i) Renewal of old canals and networks and replacement by closed systems; (ii) Expansion of water-efficient closed systems and modern irrigation methods in the existing and new irrigation projects; (iii) Increasing R&D studies and adoption of new technologies; and (iv) Acceleration of land consolidation in irrigation areas and increasing network efficiency. The Plan also includes significant investments and corresponding targets for land consolidation and promotion of good Page 12 of 88 The World Bank Turkey Irrigation Modernization Project (P158418) agricultural practices2. It is expected that these thrust areas would be maintained in the 11th Development Plan, which is currently under preparation. 10. Based on a request from the Government of Turkey, the World Bank aims to support the irrigation modernization agenda under the 10th and the 11th Development Plans. The project will focus on the irrigation schemes which are more than 30 years old, and which have suffered from decades of inadequate O&M funding and an accumulation of deferred maintenance works. Under-utilization and deterioration of networks have reduced the quantity and quality of irrigation delivery, and as a result, these schemes have experienced significant drops in effective irrigated area (within the commands) as well as productivity, thus causing a reversion from high-yielding and stable irrigated agriculture to low-yielding and risky rain-fed agriculture. Therefore, the project investments will benefit these rural communities by promoting agriculture-related income and economic growth. Furthermore, a key element of these irrigation schemes is that they already have a fixed water allocation dedicated for irrigation in the storage of the associated dam/reservoir, which is currently unused or partially used; therefore, the modernization investments will allow productive use of this water allocation, without adversely affecting the basin/sub- basin level water balance. C. Higher Level Objectives to which the Project Contributes 11. The World Bank’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Turkey covers the FY 2018-213 period and is aligned with the objectives of Turkey’s 10th Development Plan. The CPF has three focus areas: (i) Growth; (ii) Inclusion; and (iii) Sustainability. The design of the CPF is based on the findings of the Systematic Country Diagnostic4 (finalized in 2016) that highlighted water availability and sustainable use as being key challenges for Turkey’s future development. 12. The proposed project directly supports the three focus areas of the CPF and the World Bank twin goals of eliminating extreme poverty by 2030 and boosting shared prosperity of the bottom 40 percent, by assisting the key Government agencies in going beyond the routine irrigation infrastructure investments, to harmonization with agriculture productivity interventions and associated added value activities, for reaping the full potential gains of investments. As mentioned above, the project will target the older, deteriorated, and under-performing irrigation schemes, where the modernization investments and associated services will not only lift the incomes of the farmers engaged in low-value agriculture, but also contribute to general economic growth in these areas. Since agriculture is the biggest consumer of water in Turkey, improving irrigation efficiency and productivity will contribute directly to enhancing resilience and resource sustainability in this very important sector. Furthermore, this project marks the re-engagement of the World Bank with the most important water sector institution (DSI) in Turkey and will contribute meaningfully to the Bank’s broader efforts to support Turkey in the water and agricultural sectors. 13. Turkey’s Climate Change Action Plan (2011-2023) identifies a number of actions aimed at increasing the national capacity for managing the adverse impacts of climate change. While the project’s primary investments in irrigation modernization will provide both GHG mitigation and climate change adaptation benefits, the project also includes innovative pilots that have the potential to trigger large-scale adoption of improved water management practices and the use of renewable energy in irrigation. These pilots are funded with a grant from the Global Environment Facility’s (GEF) Land Degradation Thematic Area window, which aims to support efforts by eligible countries to combat land and forest degradation in rural production landscapes. Due to a combination of various factors (its location in arid and semi-arid regions; topography and soils; and centuries of anthropogenic pressures on forests, pastures and farmlands), Turkey is highly vulnerable to land degradation, and these GEF-funded activities will assist in controlling land degradation in areas of high production potential. II. PROJECT DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES A. PDO The objective of the project is to improve irrigation service delivery, through rehabilitation and modernization of distribution infrastructure in selected schemes, and through strengthening of capacity of WUAs. B. Project Beneficiaries 14. Approximately 17,300 water users (of which approximately 3 percent are women) will be direct beneficiaries of the irrigation modernization investments under Component 1. Direct beneficiaries of the GEF-funded activities include farmers at the nation-wide level (covering 3.08 m ha, and organized in 378 Water User Associations), who will benefit from improved water management at the field-level and higher capacity of WUAs. Beneficiaries also include approximately 100 staff from DSI’s regional offices and 600 staff from 378 WUAs, who will be equipped and trained in the use of the SUTEM irrigation information system, which will strengthen WUA’s capacity for O&M, water conservation, financial management, governance, M&E, and communications. Page 14 of 88 The World Bank Turkey Irrigation Modernization Project (P158418) III. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Components 16. Turkey has already embarked upon an ambitious expansion and modernization program in the irrigation sector, and DSI, as its main irrigation agency, has strong capacity for managing a large infrastructure portfolio. In this context, the proposed project will support incremental institutional advancements, related to improving the design and operations of irrigation systems through active upstream engagement with the beneficiaries, improved social and environmental management, piloting a model of sustainable groundwater use, and developing a systematic approach to strengthening of the WUAs. The bulk of project financing will go towards the modernization of selected irrigation schemes, which will serve as a platform for implementing and demonstrating the proposed improvements. Accordingly, the main motivation for this project is to develop a model of comprehensive irrigation modernization that is not limited to the improvements to the irrigation delivery infrastructure, but includes: (i) integration of measures to improve irrigation operations, beneficiary participation, and linkages to enhance agricultural productivity; and (ii) a systematic approach to strengthening the capacity of WUAs. 17. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 21. Better water management versus infrastructure expansion: The paradigm of solving water challenges by building more infrastructure is reaching its limits in most parts of the world. While Turkey still has ambitious targets for expanding the area under irrigation, there is a growing realization that improving the performance of existing systems has higher returns on investment, and therefore should receive high priority. World Bank’s irrigation engagement and the design of this specific project are accordingly focused on modernization of existing irrigation schemes. 22. Irrigation network improvements complemented with on-farm management: Experience in Turkey and elsewhere has shown that while improving the irrigation delivery is a critical input, the full value of investments can be realized only with concomitant efforts and investments in adoption of on- farm equipment, farmer training, improved cropping, and better linkages with markets. Since these two activities were previously housed in separate ministries (DSI/MoFWA and MoFAL), the project preparation process engaged MoFAL from the very beginning, with relevant MoFAL staff participating in project design as members of the Project Steering Group. The recent merger of the two ministries into an integrated MoAF is expected to further strengthen this coordination in the irrigation schemes financed by the project. 23. Impact of efficient irrigation systems on total water use: International experience shows that the adoption of drip and sprinkler systems, while beneficial for individual farmers, does not always lead to water savings at the basin level. In absence of controls on water allocation, farmers often increase overall water consumption by expanding land under irrigation, in response to the more productive technology. Therefore, the irrigation schemes to be financed under the project have been included in the respective basin management plans, which cap the amount of water that can be used for irrigation, while safeguarding the allocations for drinking water, environment, and other needs. 24. Using Pilots to Trigger Reform and Scale-up: Well-designed “proof-of-concept” pilots can be powerful drivers of regulatory reform and subsequent scale-up. There is strong client ownership for the two GEF-funded pilots in this project, which are linked to developing sustainable groundwater-based irrigation, and a national-level water monitoring system for the WUAs. Gender: The SIGA exercise shows that gender gaps in irrigated agriculture relate to lower access to assets and services, holding back women’s economic opportunities. Female water users tend to have smaller plot sizes, and represent between 7 to 18 percent of WUA members in the set of schemes where SIGA was conducted. Women reported lack of access to information and consultation platforms, and limited access to WUA training, despite stated interest in learning more about modern irrigation and farming practices. WUA membership does not translate into participation in decision-making, and less than 2 percent of WUA councilors were women (e.g. in Atabey). Eregli-Ivriz Scheme: The Eregli-Ivriz Scheme is within borders of the city of Konya, and includes the district of Eregli, the sub-district of Aziziye, and 34 villages. The scheme is located on the Eregli plains in Konya basin, which is a closed basin and one of the lowest rainfall areas in Turkey. The scheme area is in the watersheds of Ivriz and Cayhan creeks, surrounded by Toros mountains on the south and south- east, and Karacadag hills on the north. The annual precipitation is 295 mm, which is very low compared to the average for Turkey (650 mm/year). The local economy is based on agriculture, and the region is known as the “grain-bank” of Turkey. There is a long-established culture of irrigation in the area. The scheme has been operated since 1983. The Ivriz Dam was constructed in 1985 on Ivriz Creek, with a reservoir capacity of 80 MCM. The aim was to provide supplemental irrigation to 41,012 ha of agricultural land, with 115 MCM (of the total water requirement of 191 MCM) to be met by groundwater abstraction. The scheme includes 156 wells operated by DSI, in addition to approximately 3000 private wells. Irrigation modernization will reduce the irrigation demand to 154 MCM, and with 80 MCM supplied by Ivriz dam, the groundwater abstraction will be reduced by 33 percent. This is vital because the sustainability of groundwater resources is seriously threatened in the Konya basin. 5. The left bank of the scheme has 3070 total farmers, out of which 1620 are WUA members. On the right bank, there are 1818 farmers, out of which 1016 are WUA members. A third area has 525 farmers (371 WUA members). The lower WUA memberships indicate that a number of farmers are not receiving water, due to the facilities being old and dysfunctional. The current cropping pattern on the right bank mainly includes grains (33 percent), corn (34 percent) and sunflower (22 percent). The left bank cropping pattern is dominated by grains (51 percent), followed by corn (23 percent) and fruits (13 percent). 6. On average (2012-2016), the total water use in the left bank was 59.8 MCM, and 5200 ha were irrigated, out of 12,927 ha, with an irrigation ratio of 40 percent. Project Activities 17. Component 1. Irrigation Systems Modernization (~US$249 million): This component will finance investments to modernize DSI’s irrigation systems in the selected schemes. Brief descriptions of the proposed works in each scheme are presented in the following sections. (a) Eregli-Ivriz: Deformations and breakdown in existing canal and canalette structures has led to significant losses in the system leading to low irrigation efficiency. The modernization of Eregli- Ivriz aims at increasing the irrigation efficiency and improving the agricultural productivity by changing the irrigation system from open channels and canalettes to a closed system with drip and sprinkler irrigation systems. Use of groundwater is also expected to decrease after the modernization project. The project is expected to increase irrigation ratio from 40 percent to 90 percent and to increase the irrigation efficiency from 30 percent to 80 percent. DSI is already continuing with the modernization of the right bank of the irrigation scheme. (b) The project proposed for the left bank of Eregli Ivriz Dam includes changing the existing irrigation system from open channel to piped system. Theory of Change 22. DSI, established in 1911 as the General Directorate of Public Works, has a long history as the leading institution in Turkey for the development of irrigation systems across the country. The positive reputation it has built has led to highly qualified technical staff and strong institutional capacity for ensuring the technical soundness and quality of its projects. However, examples from around the world show that expanding beyond the technical excellence and incorporating stakeholder engagement throughout the project cycle significantly improves the effectives of such interventions. In addition, the introduction of innovative pilots that prove to be successful can spur policy changes to promote their scale-up at the national level. The proposed project will incorporate these two aspects for achieving the project’s objective of improving irrigation service delivery in the selected project schemes and strengthen the institutional capacity of the sector, which ultimately contribute to various higher-level sustainability targets as shown in Figure 1. 23. The irrigation modernization investments under component one will improve farmer’s access to pressurized irrigation water that is professionally managed for at least 2 years by the contracted construction company and then on by the relevant WUAs. The final design of the irrigation systems will incorporate feedback received from WUAs through the WUA consultations planned under the project. The WUA consultations will allow the incorporation of local knowledge into the system design which in most cases will lead to savings in costs, including O&M costs through the participatory development of O&M plans, and better social and environmental management leading towards stronger local ownership of the project. This will ultimately lead towards the provision of reliable on-demand irrigation to the farmers, contributing to the first PDO of the project and to increasing farming yields. 24. The GEF supported activities under Component 2 will allow the trial of two pilots that if successful can spur policy change and mobilize additional financing in this area. The WUA capacity-building activity, Page 46 of 88 The World Bank Turkey Irrigation Modernization Project (P158418) through the use of the GEF grant, will provide the necessary hardware to allow the mainstreaming of the irrigation information management system, SUTEM in all WUAs. The SUTEM is a comprehensive software with numerous modules that will increase WUA’s capacity for better monitoring, conservation and communication with farmers. The pilot on solar pumped groundwater irrigation in Bolvadin will mobilize GEF grant funding for the acquisition of solar panels that will reduce electricity costs at the cooperative level. This will free up cooperative resources for investments for better land and water management. The Bolvadin pilot will also introduce irrigation modernization and volumetric metering, along with caps on groundwater extraction. The success of these two GEF-funded activities will mean that WUA’s have better capacity and provide better service to farmers, it will lead to water savings, savings in energy costs and will introduce innovative methods for groundwater extraction controls. In addition to potentially triggering policy changes, both pilots will also lead to farmers increasing their production yields and foster changes towards higher value crops due to better WUA and farmer capacity. Ultimately, they will directly contribute to strengthening the WUA’s institutional capacity (which is the second objective of the project). Unsustainable agricultural land and water management practices (primarily due to inadequate irrigation by poor farmers lacking alternative livelihoods) cause soil erosion, salinization, compaction, and nutrient depletion, which ultimately lead to land degradation of agricultural lands. These effects hinder land productivity as it lowers the capacity of the soil to provide nutrients for crops and livestock, capturing carbon, and supporting water and nutrient cycles. In addition, this degradation also has an adverse effect on farm economics and practices, whereby decreasing production pushes farmers into increased use of chemical inputs and other unsustainable farming practices, thus deepening the spiral of ecological and economic impoverishment. In Turkey, land degradation currently affects 59 percent of the total agriculture land at varying degrees, with main drivers related to water management and Turkey’s natural vulnerability to desertification and droughts due to its various climate, soil and topographic characteristics. Given increasing demands for food and feed production due to population growth, there is a need to ensure sustainability of agro-ecosystems by balancing agricultural expansion and sustaining the productivity of existing agricultural and grazing land, without compromising ecosystem health. 6. Recognizing the negative effects of land degradation, the Government of Turkey reconfirmed in 2016 its voluntary targets for Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN), under the UN Convention for Combating Desertification (UNCCD). These targets, defined in Turkey’s National Report 2016-2030 on LDN, include increasing the area under modernized and efficient irrigation systems, mainly changing from open irrigation channels to pressurized, piped systems. The effort is aimed at promoting sustainable land management practices for the protection of agricultural lands with high production potential and integrity, through improved water management, land consolidation, agricultural land improvement services, land use planning, and the establishment of a land information system/data bases. Improving irrigation in farmlands will, in addition to generating economic benefits, reduce desertification risk and improve water availability for other uses. Given that climate change is one of the drivers of land degradation, Turkey’s Climate Change Strategy 2010-2020 also includes important targets towards increasing modernized and efficient irrigated land. Long-term studies in Turkey have shown that irrigated farmlands produce and store a higher amount of organic matter compared to rain-fed farmlands, thus generating an important climate change mitigation benefit through greenhouse sequestration and storage. 7. Local Irrigation associations, which in most cases are WUAs or agriculture cooperatives, play a Page 64 of 88 The World Bank Turkey Irrigation Modernization Project (P158418) crucial role in Turkey’s efforts to combat land degradation. Starting in 1993, they have gradually been transferred the operation and maintenance responsibilities of Turkey’s irrigation systems. There are many instances of successful WUAs that are competently operating large and efficient irrigation systems, and Turkey is considered one of the global leaders in Participatory Irrigation Management. As part of their operation and maintenance responsibilities, WUAs and agriculture cooperatives plan, monitor and control water efficiency measures by farmers, designate cultivation areas, assess and collect the fees charged for water use, and provide the necessary information to aid in the evaluation and auditing of their technical and managerial capacities. Irrigation associations are responsible for irrigation O&M in 959 schemes, covering more than 3 million hectares of irrigated land. Investing in capacity building and improving the operational efficiency and efficacy of WUAs and agriculture cooperatives would increase their ability to implement sustainable land management practices in their irrigation schemes. This could be supported by the implementation of innovative technologies and data collection and management systems in their daily operations, contributing to better decision making for reducing the drivers of land degradation. 8. Turkey’s Climate Change Strategy also sets targets for increasing the use of renewable energy in a variety of sectors. In irrigation, fossil fuel-based electricity is used in pumps for groundwater extraction and for distributing water through the irrigation scheme. Pressurized systems, although more efficient in terms of water use, usually require a higher energy input due to higher flow resistances. This leads to increased operational costs for WUAs and agriculture cooperatives. Introducing low cost clean energy alternatives for electricity generation, for instance by the introduction of solar powered irrigation, would lower the operational costs of WUAs and cooperatives, lower net greenhouse emissions and release resources that could be invested in additional activities contributing to sustainable land management. In recent years, power generation from renewable energy resources has been rapidly developing in Turkey. Solar energy use potential is very high, given Turkey’s high insolation location. At the same time, the use of groundwater for irrigation has increased significantly in Turkey, growing more than 3-fold between 1990 and 2014. As a result, the water table is dropping in many basins as the aquifers are over-exploited. Therefore, finding a model for stabilizing or reducing the use of groundwater in the over-exploited basins is also a high priority for sustainable agriculture and environmental management in Turkey. The Economic and Financial Analysis (EFA) is based on experience with similar irrigation rehabilitation projects and follows World Bank guidelines. To justify the project financing decision, the EFA aims to answer three questions: What is the project’s development impact? Is public sector provision or financing the appropriate vehicle? What is the World Bank’s value added? 2. Component 1 will finance investments to modernize DSI’s irrigation systems in four selected schemes. These include Atabey, Ereğli-İvriz, Alaşehir, and Uzunlu, with combined gross area of approx. 50,764 hectares. Component 2 will provide design and implementation support for the national program for Water User Associations Capacity Building and a pilot on sustainable energy and groundwater-based irrigation management. Component 3 will support project management. To assess the profitability of the project, the EFA will: (i) assess the financial viability of farming households to participate in the project and adopt improved irrigation and crop management practices; (ii) assess the financial viability of modernizing each irrigation scheme; and (iii) assess the project’s incremental net benefits and costs to the national economy, including public good benefits of climate change mitigation. The analysis is based on the data from DSI’s feasibility studies for each irrigation scheme. 3. The project is expected to provide private and public good benefits. The project is expected to achieve critical private benefits for participating households as well as important public goods. The modernization of the irrigation schemes will shift open canal irrigation systems to pressurized irrigation systems. The network improvements will be complemented by investments in adoption of on-farm equipment, farmer training on improved crop and water management practices, and better linkages with markets. These changes incentivize farmers to change cropping patterns towards higher value crops, enhance precision in applying irrigation and production inputs according to crop requirements, and thus enhancing productivity per hectare and overall production quantities leading to increased household income. Crop prices are expected to remain constant in the without and with project scenarios. In addition, public good benefits of mitigating greenhouse gas emissions due to energy efficient irrigation systems, improved fertilizer use, and enhancing soil carbon sequestration due to the adoption of improved crop management practices are quantified and included in the economic analysis valued at a Shadow Price of Carbon (SPC), as advised in a recent World Bank (2017) Guidance Note. The net carbon balance of the project is calculated with the Ex-Ante Carbon Balance Tool (EX-ACT) (see Annex 6). 4. Value added of World Bank's support. Turkey is an advanced middle-income country, and DSI is a well-respected agency with strong technical capacity and a large ongoing program of irrigation expansion and modernization. The World Bank’s value proposition is not in financing or engineering, but primarily in bringing process and capacity improvements such as integrating consultations and community feedback in engineering design, enhanced social and environment management, piloting use of renewable energy, developing a systematic program of WUA capacity support, and better field-level coordination with other agencies. 5. Rational for public sector financing. It is expected that WUAs recover the cost for the Page 74 of 88 The World Bank Turkey Irrigation Modernization Project (P158418) modernization of irrigation systems over a period of 30 years. The modalities of cost recovery can vary scheme by scheme11. The public sector thus pre-finances the irrigation schemes and provides supporting services, which are related to technical expertise, capacity building, consultations, establishing connections, and improving processes which will allow WUA and beneficiaries to enhance their benefits from modernized irrigation infrastructure. The support of the public sector on institutional capacity building will also enhance the achievement of public good benefits through this operation. Two types of environmental benefits are expected: (i) the project supports improved water management on a basin level by integrating irrigation schemes into a broader basin management plans. Furthermore, basin management plans cap the amount for water which can be used for irrigation and thus safeguards allocation for domestic and environmental use. (ii) In addition, the project will enhance the potential to reduce greenhouse gas emission and sequester carbon, due to energy efficient irrigation systems, improved fertilizer use, and adoption of improved management crop management practices, as mentioned above. 6. Development impact. The investment component of the project will improve the income of direct beneficiaries involved in agriculture. The financial analysis demonstrates the project’s potential to increase and stabilize net household income. By coordinating irrigation investments with value chains development and private sector involvement, the project enhances the potential to increase value added and tax revenue for the Government, contributing to the overall development impact. In addition, the project will improve the provision of ecosystem-related goods and services, which will enhance ecosystems, agri-food systems and households’ resilience.
