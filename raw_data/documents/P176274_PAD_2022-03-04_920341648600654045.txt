Page 8 of 77 The World Bank CAR Investment and Business Competitiveness for Employment (P176274) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Central African Republic (CAR) is one of the poorest and most fragile countries in the world. In 2019, per capita gross domestic product (GDP) averaged US$468 —much lower than the average of US$1,130 in countries affected by fragility, conflict, and violence (FCV) in Sub-Saharan Africa. The extreme poverty rate remained high at 71.4 percent in 2020. 2. CAR’s economy is small (US$2.2 billion GDP), driven by agriculture and extractives, and heavily dependent on imports. Economic growth has historically been driven by agriculture and extractive industries. These sectors have dominated the export market (consisting primarily of diamonds and rough wood). Asia and the EU are CAR’s main export markets (~90 percent), with China representing 54.3 percent of CAR’s total exports, while exports within Africa represent only 14 percent of total exports. Official exports in 2018 totaled US$99.4 million. Agriculture remains a major source of formal and informal employment - the sector accounts for 77.3 percent of employment and 39 percent of GDP. In recent years, the services sector share of GDP has increased and contributes approximately as much as agriculture to GDP. The agriculture and services sectors were the main supply-side drivers of economic growth in 2019, growing at 3.1 percent and 2.3 percent, respectively.1 3. CAR’s political and governance context remains extremely fragile. Throughout its history this landlocked country located in the heart of Africa has suffered from cycles of violence. While the adoption of the 2015 Constitution began a new process of stabilization, the December 2020 presidential election was marked by violence following the creation of a rebel coalition and the subsequent government counter-offensive. The Government had regained control over most of its territory by end 2021. While the country has recently seen important gains in terms of consolidation, fighting continues in some areas of strategic interest. Government troops and their allies are accused of human rights violations. Furthermore, the choice of bilateral partnerships in the security sector is creating tensions with CAR’s traditional political, development and humanitarian partners. The ability of CAR and the international community to establish an effective and sustainable arrangement to address chronic instability will determine the prospects for poverty reduction and shared prosperity and strengthen the resilience against state collapse. 4. The COVID-19 pandemic has had a negative impact on the economy. The pandemic drew significant government resources that could have been spent addressing the country’s challenges. With the onset of containment measures in April 2020, supply chains were interrupted which had a negative impact on all sectors, increasing costs and business risks. The resulting economic downturn, as in other parts of the region, provoked widespread unemployment, shrank domestic markets, and decreased liquidity. While the country received additional resources from donors during the pandemic and partially recovered through adaptation, the new post-election instability has resulted in backsliding. The country’s economic growth was 0.8 percent for 2020 (−1.1 percent in per-capita terms), 3.6 percent below pre-COVID-19 projections. The fiscal position is estimated to have deteriorated as tax revenues declined and public expenditures have risen due to the pandemic. The World Bank estimates that the pandemic pushed approximately 70,000 additional people into extreme poverty in 2020.2 1 World Bank, CAR Economic Update, July 2021. 2 Ibid. CAR is also highly vulnerable to climate change, ranked 180 out of 181 countries in the 2020 ND-GAIN Index3. CAR has a population of just over 4.7 million people (2019) with an annual population growth rate of 1.7 percent. Approximately 41.4 percent of the population lives in urban areas. This is projected to increase to 48 percent and 60 percent of the population by 2030 and 2050, respectively. The high levels of poverty and low-degree of development in CAR limit capacity of poor households and communities to manage climate risk, increasing their vulnerability to climate-related shocks. Impacts of climate change will affect the poorest and most vulnerable the most and hinder CAR’s development agenda and efforts at economic sustainability and political stability, further impacting poor and vulnerable groups. Adverse climatic events may destroy infrastructure, interrupt business and the functioning of the government. Floods are a recurring and devastating natural disaster and are responsible for the largest share of economic and human losses from natural disasters in CAR. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context Challenge 1: Unpredictable and Inefficient Environment for Investment 6. CAR is one of the most challenging places in the world to do business. Businesses lament the unpredictability of the business environment with respect to settling disputes, predatorial behavior of tax authorities and a casual respect for legal agreements. The Bertelsmann Transformation Index4 which examines economic governance in transitioning and developing economies classified CAR as weak (<3 out of 10) in key dimensions of its business environment including markets and competition, and protection of private property. Furthermore, CAR was ranked 151st out of 160 countries in the World Bank’s 2018 Logistics Performance Index. CAR also ranked 133rd out of 152 economies in the 2020 Competitive Industrial Performance Index of United Nations Industrial Development Organization (UNIDO) which measures how successful a country’s industries are at producing and selling their goods on domestic and foreign markets, and subsequently, how they contribute to structural change and development. Challenge 2: Key Institutions Supporting Private Sector Development Lack Capacity and Resources 7. Key ministries and public organizations entrusted to improve the business environment, implement reforms, and offer training, information and services to businesses lack capacity. The Ministry of Small and Medium Enterpreneurs (SMEs) and Private Sector Development (Ministère des Petites et Moyennes Entreprises et de la Promotion du Secteur Privé), and the Ministry of Commerce and Industry (Ministère de Commerce de l’Industrie) have limited resources to implement their respective workplans, and work with businesses and other stakeholders to understand and be responsive to their needs. These needs include facilitating import/export and investment promotion, market research, and information and facilitating implementation of regulations and economic reforms that help businesses. 8. Government’s efforts to address obstacles to private sector development have had limited impact, and the ecosystem consisting of public and privately funded services for SMEs is embryonic and functions poorly. The government established the SME and Artisan’s Business Center (Centre d’Assistance aux Petites et Moyennes Entreprises et à l’Artisanat - CAPMEA) to be a major implementor of its efforts to support SMEs. Under the Prime Minister, the Mixed Coordination Framework for Improving Business (Cadre Mixte de Concertation pour l’Amélioration des Affaires- CMCAA) is the main body responsible for public-private dialogue on business environment and economic reforms. With few resources, the CMCAA has been able to propose key laws and reforms, but adoption and reform implementation processes move slowly. The Chamber of Commerce (Chambre de Commerce, d'Industry, des Mines et d'Artisanat de Centrafrique) is responsible for registering businesses and providing official documents for traders but provides few other services that could help them. Most of the Chamber’s services have been transferred to the Single Window for Business Formalities (Guichet Unique pour les Formalités des Entreprises- GUFE). With donor support, the Chamber has been able to perform feasibility studies for the creation of a guarantee fund for SMEs and the Authorized Management Center (Centre de Gestion Agrée- CGA) that would provide accounting training to businesses. Overall, CAR would benefit from a higher profile economic reform agenda to which more resources, attention and effort are devoted to promote private sector development. 10. Professional associations, Central African Interprofessional Coalition of Businesses (Groupement Interprofessionnel de Centrafrique, GICA) which advocates for the few, formal businesses and the National Union of Businesses (Union Nationale du Patronat Centrafricain- UNPC) representing the interests of 7 national professional unions participate in the work of the CMCAA to inform the reform agenda. With its 149 members, the Central African Businesswomen’s Association (Association des Femmes d’Affaires Centrafricaines, AFA) is a prominent advocate for women-owned micro, small, and medium enterprises (MSMEs) and provides training to its members and others. Since 2013, provisions favorable to women have been adopted.6 These organizations and other businesses associations provide services such as networking opportunities, training, advocacy and information to businesses. Challenge 3: MSMEs have Significant Deficits in Business and Management Skills 11. Successive crises, and the 2013 Crisis in particular, have had a pernicious, negative impact on the private sector. Infrastructure, institutions, and social capital were destroyed or negatively impacted, all of which have made businesses less competitive, increased costs, and decreased availability of business support services. Many businesses closed, or lost assets. People, with the skills and means to do so, left the country impacting both the government and public institutions as well as the private sector. The majority of MSMEs have significant deficits in business and management skills. They do not employ adequate modern business practices (accounting, budgeting, planning, marketing techniques, technology), which impacts their ability to access credit, develop linkages with more sophisticated companies, access markets, and improve their businesses. 12. CAR’s informal sector represents approximately 60-80 percent of the output produced by the private sector. Extensive discussions across government ministries, businesses, banks, and business associations over the past 5 years revealed a range of causes for informality. The primary causes cited were the avoidance of taxes and predatorial behavior by officials, and the low value placed on business opportunities foregone by remaining informal such as accessing financing, recovery of value-added taxes, accessing tenders and using more sophisticated business arrangements and contracts. Also prominent were observations of the inefficiency of Page 11 of 77 The World Bank CAR Investment and Business Competitiveness for Employment (P176274) government agencies charged with processes related to formality like customs, tax collection, labor contracts, investment/corporate structuring, building permits, business registration and licensing. 13. A sample of 135 MSMEs, surveyed in a 2017 study that identified sectors of opportunity for MSMEs, identified constraints to their development and made recommendations to alleviate them.5 This diagnostic found that MSMEs face critical deficits in skills, access to finance and platforms for collaboration horizontally and vertically. While over 90 percent of MSMEs in Bangui surveyed had a bank account or an account with a microcredit institution, only 22 percent had obtained a credit of any kind over the past 3 years. For smaller cities, these figures are much worse. Estimated financing needs based on the repayment capacity of MSMEs in Bangui were US$48.7 million while the outstanding loans and other financing amounted to US$9.6 million (May 19, 2017). 14. Furthermore, there are challenges faced by women with respect to business and employment. Only 6.46 percent of firms have majority female ownership compared to 13.2 percent for Sub-Saharan Africa. Vulnerable employment7 among women was 96.5 percent and among men 88.8 percent in 2019, indicating a gap of 7.7 percent and has worsened among women since 1991. The rate of vulnerable employment is 5.9 percent higher for women in CAR than the average rate for women in Sub-Saharan Africa.8 Challenge 4: Lack of Access to Credit constrains Private Sector Development 15. CAR’s financial sector is underdeveloped. The financial system is concentrated in Bangui. The country has four banks,9 eight microfinance institutions (MFIs)10 and three insurance companies. The sector is supervised and regulated at the regional level by the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (CEMAC) regional institutions including the Bank of Central African States (Banque des Etats de l’Afrique Centrale, BEAC) (the regional central bank); Banking Commission of Central Africa (La Commission Bancaire de Afrique Centrale, COBAC) (the regional banking supervisor); and La Commission de Surveillance du Marché Financier d’Afrique Centrale, COSUMAF). The banking sector remains highly concentrated with one bank controlling close to half of the banking sector assets. Banks’ balance sheets are growing and represent now US$556 million, 17 percent more than last year. Deposits represent US$407 million, 22.9 percent more than last year. Credit is also increasing and represents US$297 million with approximately 15,000 active accounts. Orange offers basic services of cash-in and cash-out transactions on mobile money account, transfers between Orange Money (OM) accounts, and a few payment services. As of October 2019, OM transactions were US$6 million up from US$4.4 million in September 2019. 16. Access to finance remains one of the largest constraints for private sector development in particular for SMEs. Despite a regionally integrated financial sector, banks display poor intermediation indicators. Central African Republic (as well as other CEMAC member countries) does not have a private credit bureau. The public credit registry is not functional, and the payment incidents database is not fully operational. Although Organization for the Harmonization of Business Law in Africa (Organisation Pour L’harmonisation En Afrique Du Droit Des Affaires, OHADA) recently updated the secured transactions legislation to offer a broader range of financial products using movable property as collateral, CAR has not set up yet a modern movable collateral registry. Other SME tools to allow asset-based lending such as leasing or factoring or risk sharing facilities do not yet exist despite requests from the financial sector. SMEs are perceived as high risk by the financial sector due to several shortcomings including asymmetry of information and weak credit infrastructure; lack of collateral; limited managerial skills of current and prospective entrepreneurs; and weak collateral enforcement regime. Facing these limitations, banks apply high interest rates and significant collateral requirements (150 percent and above of the value of the loan) on SME loans to better manage the inherent credit risk. As a result, SMEs have limited opportunities to access formal finance in affordable terms. 17. Access to finance is more constrained for women entrepreneurs than for men. The 2017 FINDEX report shows that only 10 percent of adult women have access to a transaction account compared to 15 percent of adult men. Women are also underrepresented on the use of mobile financial services with only 4 percent having made payments with mobile money account (compared to 9 percent of men). Data shows that 14 percent of women have saved to start or expand their businesses but only 5 percent do so through a formal financial institution. Family, friends and other informal networks remain the most viable way of financing a business. Only 3 percent of female entrepreneurs have borrowed from a financial institution to start/operate/expand a business versus 4 percent of men. Challenge 5: Job Specific Skills Training System is not Structured and Remains Informal 18. Businesses most often trained staff in house even when hiring graduates of local technical and vocational education and training (TVET) providers or hired skilled tradespeople from neighboring countries to perform a range of functions: construction, electrical engineering, masonry, carpentry, welding, plumbing, and so on. These skills are in high demand. Businesses also indicated that TVET providers often lacked the assets to properly instruct students in the evolving techniques used across sectors. The speed with which technology changes and the impact of this phenomena on all sectors makes it difficult for poorly resourced TVET to renew equipment, tools, and assets to remain current and relevant. From analytical work, past project performance in CAR and engagement with a wide range of counterparts in CAR, lessons have been learned and integrated in the Project design. This Project is the first large investment lending operation in CAR for private sector development. It follows large investments in more immediate, near- term, and foundational needs to which the World Bank has been responding. These include food security, peace building, public management, infrastructure, and agriculture. The design of the Project has been kept as simple as possible and focused on a few key objectives and needs of the private sector. The need to develop the skills of businesses is addressed through working with organizations and strengthening them as training and support is provided to their constituents: businesses, entrepreneurs, and investors. In this way, the Project will meet its objectives of strengthening institutions needed to provide services to businesses, and intermediate constructively private sector activity. The Project design also incorporates robust implementation support from consultants and experts to assist the government and PIU as necessary. 64. The Project has also taken a narrow focus on economic reforms, choosing to implement a few reform workstreams that are the private sector’s highest priority. Like reforms, SME finance is addressed by focusing on one potentially powerful element, a PCG mechanism. In both cases, the degree of success can dictate the pace at which additional reforms and A2F facilitating elements can be integrated into the Project. 65. Along these lines, the Project design is committed to building the skills of businesses and organizations concerned with private sector development, and fill a specific gap among diverse related Projects, initiatives, and activities. The Project design aims to be complimentary and coordinated with World Bank Project such as Public Sector Digital Governance Project (P174620), the Agriculture Recovery and Agribusiness Development Support Project (P165855), the CAR First Resilient and Inclusive Institutions for State Effectiveness DPO (P175173, PE) and IFC’s CAR IC-II advisory activity. 66. The skills component is based on lessons learnt from other apprenticeship schemes. Consistent with research on what has been successful in other settings, the Project will introduce a dual training approach to include classroom learning to acquire both basic technical skills and theoretical knowledge as well as on-the-job training. 67. PCGs have existed for several decades and the World Bank has supported the establishment of many of these across the world. Based on these successful experiences, the World Bank has developed international principles to support the establishment of successful PCGs that will be applied to this Project. Good examples include Morocco, Burkina Faso, Madagascar or Chile. Key common elements include: independent institutional structure and robust legal framework, robust capitalization, strong corporate governance and internal control systems, portfolio approach towards guarantees (as opposed to individual) predictable and transparent operational procedures. Risks are high given the fragile political and governance situation in the country and could slow or derail reforms over the course of the Project. CAR’s political and governance context remains extremely fragile. Throughout its history this landlocked country located in the heart of Africa has suffered from cycles of violence. While the adoption of the 2015 Constitution began a new process of stabilization, the December 2020 presidential election was marked by violence following the creation of a rebel coalition and the subsequent government counter-offensive. The Government had Page 42 of 77 The World Bank CAR Investment and Business Competitiveness for Employment (P176274) regained control over most of its territory by end 2021. While the country has recently seen important gains in terms of consolidation, fighting continues in some areas of strategic interest. Government troops and their allies are accused of human rights violations. Furthermore, the choice of bilateral partnerships in the security sector is creating tensions with CAR’s traditional political, development and humanitarian partners. The ability of CAR and the international community to establish an effective and sustainable arrangement to address chronic instability will determine the prospects for poverty reduction and shared prosperity and strengthen the resilience against state collapse. 118. Macroeconomic (Substantial). The country continues to face a relatively volatile economic situation as its economy recovers slowly from the crisis. The Project will focus this support on a few areas where there is a labor market demand. The proposed intervention will upgrade the existing apprenticeship trainings to ensure quality improvements in the learning experience of apprentices. Consistent with research on what has been successful in other settings, the training program will introduce a dual training approach to include classroom learning to acquire both basic technical skills and theoretical knowledge as well as on-the-job training. The apprenticeship training will last between one and two years during which apprentices will spend time at (a) the training center34 for foundational and theoretical technical skills, and (b) in local workshops for practical on-the-job training. The training will include courses on literacy and numeracy, life skills and digital skills (when feasible). Theoretical vocational training in the selected trades will be delivered by TVET instructors and trainers from the ACFPE. Practical vocational training will be offered master craft persons in respective local business/workshops in the selected trade who will also receive training from ACFPE. Life skills courses will include training in social emotional skills to facilitate entry in the labor market, such as self-confidence, emotion management, empathy, conflict resolution, communication, and leadership. Life skills training will be delivered by guidance counsellors within the ACFPE. Literacy, numeracy and digital literacy will also be provided to apprentices by TVET instructors in the training centers. The MOE will be responsible for the quality control of the training. 3. The project will finance training and certification costs of apprentices which include operating costs of training centers, training of master craftsmen and TVET instructors, learning materials, toolkits, stipend for transport and meals. The project will focus on select trades for which there is a labor market demand and will not include fields which are already well represented and for which there is potential saturation. Poverty and vulnerability in the Central African Republic take on specific gendered dimensions, such that women are disproportionately affected. With a Gender Inequality Index of 0.68235, it ranked 159 out of 162 countries in 2018. Challenges permeate the entire life cycle of females in the country. Women experience poor maternal health due to high adolescent fertility. The high rates of teen pregnancy and early marriage result in elevated school dropout rates, poor educational outcomes, and low literacy (68 percent of women are illiterate compared to 48 percent of men). As a result, women have low economic empowerment and low participation in the labor force (64.47 percent for women compared to 79.45 percent for men), mainly in agriculture and the informal sector. Discriminatory laws, social norms and poor access to justice, further limit women’s economic empowerment, for example from having to rely on their husbands for access to land. 2. CAR has among the lowest education and gender equality indicators in the world. The poor quality of primary education, low participation rates in secondary school education for girls, and high levels of violence against women and girls, with 11,000 reported incidents each year (2016), 74 percent of which involve children, remain pressing challenges. 3. The project will contribute to pillars of the WBG Gender Strategy (FY16-23). It will contribute to removing constraints for more and better jobs by funding subprojects that benefit business associations and organizations that provide training to businesswomen and women entrepreneurs in management and using digital technologies for business and provide them with business development services (Component 3) and enabling financing of women-owned and women-managed businesses through a partial guarantee mechanism (Component 2). This mechanism will confer specific advantages to these businesses and design its operations to attract them.
