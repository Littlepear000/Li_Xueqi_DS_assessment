Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Pakistan has made significant progress over the last two decades towards reducing poverty. The expansion of off-farm economic opportunities, and the increase in migration and associated remittances allowed over 47 million Pakistanis to escape poverty between 2001 and 2018. Nonetheless, challenges for inclusive growth remain, systematically related to spatial disparities and deficits in human capital endowment, and access to services and opportunities. Human capital outcomes are poor and stagnant, with high levels of stunting at 38 percent and learning poverty at 75 percent. Growth of per capita GDP has also been low, averaging only around 1.8 percent annually. Economic growth in Pakistan has historically been fueled by private and government consumption, with productivity-enhancing investment and exports contributing relatively little. Furthermore, consumption-led growth has been associated with frequent macroeconomic imbalances. Achieving sustained higher economic growth is important for Pakistan to reduce inequality and increase shared prosperity. 2. After rebounding in FY21, growth is expected to moderate in FY22-FY23. Due to low-base effects and recovering domestic demand, real GDP growth at 2015-16 factor prices rebounded to 5.6 percent in FY21 from a contraction of 1.0 percent in FY20. However, with emerging external imbalances and higher domestic inflation, monetary tightening has resumed in FY22. Output growth is therefore projected to moderate to 4.3 percent in FY22 and to 4.0 percent in FY23. Thereafter, economic growth is projected to recover to 4.2 percent in FY24. This recovery is predicated on continued macroeconomic stability and a narrowing of the fiscal and external deficits in the medium-term. Inflation is estimated to rise to an average of 10.7 percent in FY22 but is then expected to decrease over the forecast horizon as world commodity prices ease. Following from the faster import than export growth in H1 FY22, the CAD is expected to widen to 4.4 percent of GDP in FY22. Moderating demand pressures due to monetary tightening, lower commodity prices and the weaker currency are expected to dampen imports in FY23. With the implementation of reforms to reduce import tariffs on relevant intermediates for the export sector and increased allocations for export refinance schemes, the CAD is expected to narrow to 3.0 percent of GDP in FY24. The fiscal deficit (excluding grants) is projected to widen slightly to 6.3 percent of GDP in FY22, and gradually narrow over the medium term as revenue mobilization measures, particularly GST harmonization and Personal Income Tax (PIT) reform take hold. Public debt will remain elevated in the medium term, as will Pakistan’s exposure to debt-related shocks. This outlook assumes that the International Monetary Fund Extended Fund Facility program will remain on track. Pakistan continues to suffer climate-induced crises, with wide-ranging impacts including on the education sector. Pakistan is ranked seventh on the Global Climate Risk Index, and extreme weather events are expected to grow in severity, with high human and economic costs. In 2010, floods in Pakistan damaged or destroyed 10,000 schools, impacting an estimated 1.8 million children across all districts. Schooling in Punjab is also heavily impacted by heat waves, with the school year being shortened every year as students faint or suffer exhaustion from exposure to extreme heat. In 2005 an earthquake in Pakistan led to the death of 853 teachers and 180,956 students, and the destruction of more than 7,500 schools. Education infrastructure and services are at risk of being exposed to physical damage and disruption, compounding Punjab’s current and future development challenges in education and human capital. Sectoral & Institutional Context 4. Punjab’s education sector has made progress over the last two decades, but important challenges remain, particularly for girls and especially after the extensive school closures during the COVID-19 pandemic. School participation rates for ages 6-16 in Punjab, Pakistan have increased over the past two decades from 61% (57% for Pakistan as a whole) in 2000-011 to about 78%2 today (71% for all of Pakistan). The gender gap has narrowed to 3 percentage points in 2019/20, down from 13 percentage points in 2000. For Pakistan overall, the gender gap fell from 17 to 10 percentage points in the same period. In Punjab, the improvement in the participation rate is mostly visible in primary grades (age 6-10), as now 82% of girls and 85% of boys are enrolled in school (PSLM 2019/20). Despite progress in enrolments overall, very few children ages 4-5 enroll in pre-primary education: only 38% of children between the ages of 4-5 attend school in Punjab (PSLM 2019/20). Dropouts in primary school are high for both boys and girls, with about 15% of girls and 14% of boys dropping out before reaching grade 6 (PSLM 2019/20). In absolute terms, an estimated 19 million school-age children (age 6-16) in Pakistan are out of school, out of which approximately 7 million live in Punjab. The COVID-19 pandemic has further aggravated this existing enrollment crisis. Learning levels have remained extremely low for both girls and boys and are likely to have decreased during the pandemic period. According to official estimates (based on NLA 2014, PSLM 2018/19), 65 percent of children aged 10 in Punjab (75% in Pakistan overall) , do not learn to read, and understand a simple paragraph by age 10. This figure has been projected to rise to 79 percent due to COVID-19. Empirical studies seem to confirm substantial learning losses, with the number of Grade 5 children correctly answering Mathematics questions decreasing from 34% in 2018 to 26% in 2021. A recent survey showed that well over 90% of teachers in Punjab are using low-quality instructional practices, whether in public or private sector schools. An estimated 27% of public schools have multi-grade classrooms, and multigrading has been seen to be associated with much lower learning outcomes. This is particularly problematic when it occurs in pre-school where the foundations for school readiness are laid. Classrooms, starting from pre-school, also tend to have a wide range of ages enrolled in them – further undermining effective teaching and learning. 6. Even though the gender gap in enrollment has decreased in the past decade, girls are still more likely than boys to never attend school. About 18% of girls (age 6-16) in Punjab are likely to never attend school as compared to 12% of boys (PSLM 2019/20). Research suggests that the most common factors limiting girls’ access to school include distance between schools and households and a lack of safe transportation as well as lack of safe WASH facilities within schools. Additional barriers include unequal intrahousehold investment in girls’ education and higher risk perceptions for girls. The government of Punjab has been providing secondary school stipends to girls from lagging districts. A recent long term study showed that exposure to the program yielded positive results with increased likelihood of secondary education completion and reduced likelihood of being married before the age of 16 and early childbearing. However, within conservative segments of society, safety and security risk perception are critical. Qualitative studies and focus group discussions reveal common prevalence of sexual harassment, if not abuse, and the impact it has on girls’ potenti al to be in school. Complaint mechanisms remain underdeveloped and underutilized because of the taboo culture and remediation is limited because of weak leadership and school management practices and simply lack of information. 7. Building on almost twenty years of reforms, the province is consolidating its focus on three strategic priorities. To bring more focus to the reform agenda, the province is setting explicit and gender-disaggregated targets for districts and schools and establishing a monitoring and follow-up framework. While there are extensive monitoring and data systems, the usage has remained limited due to low data literacy at the district and sub-district levels and a lack of translation from data points to indicators that can be tracked by policy-makers. Incentives to prioritize the collection and analysis of critical data points particularly learning outcomes have been relatively weak or absent. 8. The first key strategic priority is the sustainable and equitable expansion of school participation. An immediate constraint is that public schools in the province have serious space constraints to enroll students, and learning spaces are not adequately prepared for present and future climate risks. Currently, 1% (475) of public schools do not have a single classroom, 3% (1,443) of public schools have only 1 classroom, and 25% (11,888) of public schools have only 2 classrooms. The GoPunjab estimates that approximately 98,941 classrooms will need to be constructed in public schools to offer primary education with the officially required student-teacher ratio (STR) of 40:1. Girls are particularly affected by the low availability of classrooms because as distance to the closest eligible school increases, the drop in enrollment among girls is three to four times as great as that for boys. In addition, across Punjab, a significant number of schools are at high risk from extreme heatwaves given that projected temperature increases in Pakistan are significantly higher than the global average. 9. The province also plans to further scale-up provision of schooling through Public-Private Partnerships under the Punjab Education Foundation (PEF), which now provides education to approximately 2.5 million children in just over 7,500 schools. While scaling quickly, PEF schools have not explicitly focused on girls’ enrolment, and girls only make up 45% of total enrolment in primary grades. PEF’s budget growth over the last five years has been minimal, and budget fluctuations have impacted some of PEF’s critical functions, including the retention of human resources and improvement in implementation of a quality assurance system. Combined with inflationary pressure, these shortfalls have put the sustainability of the current scale of enrollment and operations at risk. The second strategic priority is to bring more coherence in initiatives to improve learning outcomes for both girls and boys, while also recovering from learning loss from the pandemic. To track learning levels, Punjab has replaced high-stakes student examinations with an overarching assessment policy framework that includes different types of (summative and formative) student assessments in both primary and secondary grades. As these changes are being introduced, there is a need to consistently improve the utilization of data from these assessments and shift away from the culture of examinations which is still pervasive in Pakistan. The province has launched a strategy on Formative Assessment and plans to train teachers on how to use assessments in the classroom to track learning and provide remedial teaching for students that are lagging behind. The Punjab Examination Commission (PEC), which is the central assessment agency administering assessments in public schools, lacks IT resources and exposure to international best practices. 11. Improving school readiness before children enroll in grade 1 is becoming a focus of the entire school system. Many children in Punjab struggle to learn upon entry in school having had little to no exposure to early years learning and stimulation. In 2017, the province adopted an ECE policy with a two-year ECE curriculum, which is now being folded into Pakistan’s Single National Curriculum. The province now has over 13,000 functional ECE classrooms in public schools and over 30,000 trained ECE teachers and Head Teachers. Around 59% (7,067) of these ECE classrooms meet the existing provincial Quality Standards for ECE. Efforts are underway to merge traditional pre-school classrooms (Katchi) with those offering the new ECE curriculum. As this process unfolds, the existing quality standards for ECE will need to be updated to incorporate a focus on teacher-child interactions. 12. The third strategic priority is strengthening the management of schools. First, headteachers or school principals face several difficulties in making meaningful improvements to the school environment. The main tool is the non-salary budget (NSB), which is a small, school-level budget that can be used to improve the school’s infrastructure. However, utilization of the NSB remains low due to operational challenges in spending the budget, including VAT requirements which are difficult to meet in rural areas. Most primary schools do not have a headteacher, and there is no clear policy framework for the role of headteachers in schools. Headteachers are typically selected based on their seniority, rather than their management capacity and do not receive any training or professional development support for a management role. Second, outreach from the school system to parents and involving the community in children’s learning has been somewhat limited, which has restricted avenues for parents to engage or hold schools accountable. Project Description 15. There are three components to this project, each with an investment and results-based sub-component. Component 1: Girls Results Agenda (Performance Based Conditions) 16. This component consists of activities which support Punjab’ education reform agenda on interventions that are known to benefit girls. The component funds eight performance-based conditions (PBCs), under three broad headers (access, learning and governance). 17. PBC 1 supports the overarching goal setting of yearly gender-disaggregated targets for school enrolment and learning levels across the delivery chain (from province down to district and school level) to improve the focus in the overall reform agenda and supporting the collection and use of disability-disaggregated data in schools. This PBC supports improvements in all domains, including access, learning and governance. 18. Second, through PBC 2, the component supports a further scale-up and strengthening of the public- private partnerships model for increasing enrolment of out-of-school children, and equalizing enrolment among girls and boys. This contributes to improving access on the short-run, given that large increases in access through public schools will take more time to materialize. Specifically, the PBC will support expansion of the partnerships under PEF to cover a total of 3 million students, which is 500,000 students above the current baseline of 2.5 million. At the same time, PBC 2 will strengthen the Punjab Education Foundation (PEF) model by developing a long-term strategic plan for PPPs, an updated partnership model that strengthens the management of partner schools with a focus on the position of teachers and student well-being (in particular regarding GBV), the adoption of a fundraising model to attract private sector resources, and an improved school monitoring system. 19. Thirdly, this component will support strengthening teaching and learning processes, particularly to recover Covid-19 related learning losses. It will support three attached departments of the School Education Departments, which all support core learning processes for students. PBC 3 supports a learning recovery curriculum and strengthened learning materials to ensure students recover learning losses. This PBC will be coordinated by SED and the Punjab Curriculum and Textbook Board. PBC 4 will support improvements in the quality of Early Childhood Care and Education in additional 5000 existing classrooms. This will allow for more students to enroll in existing ECCE schools, while also ensuring they are ready for school from Grade 1 onwards. This PBC will be coordinated by SED and the Quaid-e-Azam Academy for Educational Development (QAED). PBC 5 will support capacity building for teachers and headteachers to help teachers respond better to student needs and learning losses. It also includes teacher training based on the code of conduct developed by SED on child protection, including GBV prevention, response, and referrals, and an action plan to strengthen child protection in schools. This PBC will be coordinated by SED and the Quaid e Azam Academy for Educational Development (QAED). PBC 6 support strengthening of the student assessment system, by improving standard setting for large- scale (sample-based) assessments, strengthening the quality of school-based assessments, and introducing monitoring of child development at an earlier age. This PBC will be coordinated by SED and the Punjab Examination Commission (PEC). 20. Lastly, this component will support strengthening the management of schools through PBC 7, which supports improvements to how schools are financed with the Non-Salary Budgets, in particular, to set incentives for schools to improve learning outcomes and to reduce energy expenditures at schools. The interventions under this sub-component will construct approximately 4,500 additional classrooms (Grades ECE to 5). Construction will prioritize girls’ schools as well as schools that can enroll a large share of girls. All classrooms will receive furniture that can be flexibly arranged to have a more engaging learning dynamic in classrooms. All schools selected for project intervention will be provided with accessible wheelchair ramps and handrails. Low-cost climate-smart features will be used which will include climate adaptation efforts (e.g., heat insulation, use of natural ventilation, use of natural lighting, raised plinths, reflective roofs,) as well as climate change mitigation for schools, including, water conservation fittings, rainwater harvesting in high-precipitation areas, and tree planting/afforestation on school grounds. Sites that are prone to flooding will use an elevated school design. All classroom construction will be based on identified need from the school information system, using a mapping of the out-of-school population in target areas from satellite data. Departmental Review Committee. A program review committee will be established at the School Education Department level with heads of each attached department as its members, the committee will be headed by the Secretary SED. The committee will meet on a quarterly basis to review the progress of the program activities against set targets. The committee will comprise Secretary SED, Program Director PMIU, Director-General QAED, CEO PEC, Managing Director PEF, and Managing Director PCTB. 28. Program Steering Committee.
