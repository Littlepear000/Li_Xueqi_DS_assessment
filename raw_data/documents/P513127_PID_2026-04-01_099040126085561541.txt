Introduction and Context Country Context Somalia continues to make notable progress in consolidating its federal governance system, strengthening public institutions, and fostering inclusive, private sector-led development critical for job creation. The attainment of the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Completion Point in December 2023 marked a pivotal step, enabling substantial debt relief and expanding fiscal space for public investment. These reforms, alongside a policy focus on private sector-led growth, are helping to strengthen the enabling environment for enterprise development and livelihoods, particularly for youth. Despite these advances, structural constraints persist—including an unfinished political settlement, fragmented governance, and high levels of insecurity—which continue to weigh on confidence and investment. Private sector activity remains concentrated in a few urban centers where basic infrastructure lowers operating costs; yet urban poverty remains substantial (46 percent of urban residents live below the national poverty line).1 Somalia’s main cities nonetheless represent a key opportunity for resilience and growth: roughly 90 percent of the urban population in cities of 20,000 or more is concentrated along six strategic corridors that account for 93 percent of nighttime light luminosity, a proxy for economic activity.2 1World Bank. 2024. Over half of the population lives below the poverty line, and food insecurity persists, with 6.5 million people facing crisis or above conditions amid erratic rainfall, limited local food production, and constrained humanitarian support. Rapid urbanization is placing mounting pressure on municipal infrastructure and services, contributing to the expansion of informal settlements and heightened exposure to floods and droughts. Somalia is highly exposed to climate change impacts— including recurrent droughts, floods, extreme heat, and rainfall variability—while transport infrastructure is particularly vulnerable to flooding, heat stress, and storm events that can disrupt corridor connectivity and aviation safety and operations.3 Reflecting these pressures, economic growth has moderated since 2024: real GDP growth slowed to an estimated 3.0 percent in 2025 and is projected at 2.8 percent in 2026, as sharp aid cuts, worsening drought conditions, and external spillovers weigh on consumption, rural incomes, and exports. Risks remain firmly tilted to the downside given uncertainty around aid and remittances, climate shocks, security conditions, and global commodity price volatility.4 Sectoral and Institutional Context Foundational infrastructure deficits remain a binding constraint to growth, resilience, and construction-led recovery within and between cities. Construction is a critical entry point for jobs and post conflict urban recovery, yet its economic _For Official Use Only_ impact is constrained by informality, limited scale, and weak firm capabilities, constraints that are exacerbated by poor infrastructure.5 Limited road coverage and poor maintenance restrict mobility and market access. In Mogadishu and the wider Banadir region, recurrent urban flooding regularly disrupts transport, commerce, and livelihoods, reflecting the combined effects of inadequate drainage infrastructure, rapid urbanization, and climate variability. Ongoing flood risk assessments estimate average annual losses at approximately US$120 million, with damage highly concentrated in dense, high-value urban areas. Moreover, 61 percent of households report income losses driven by the inability of workers to reach workplaces with these events triggering work disruptions of three days. At the firm level, flooding along Mogadishu’s primary corridors harms firm inventory and disrupts supply chains.6 Targeted investment in priority trunk drainage infrastructure is essential to reduce flood risk, protect densely populated and economically vital districts, and restore urban functionality. Over the medium term, improved drainage and transport reliability lowers construction costs, enables year-round work, and supports sustained private sector growth and job creation. Economic fragmentation extends beyond urban centers to rural-urban linkages and regional trade, limiting the benefits of agglomeration. Somalia’s 3,300 km coastline and major seaports offer potential as a regional trade hub for the Horn of Africa, linking Africa, Asia, Europe, and the Middle East. Yet fragmented logistics networks, security constraints, and weak infrastructure continue to drive high transport costs even over short distances, contributing to price disparities and limiting market integration. Corridor and urban road investments reduce the cost of moving goods from production centers to markets, while drainage and urban infrastructure improvements ensure that receiving and distribution points in primary cities remain functional year-round. Addressing spatial and economic fragmentation requires a strategic approach to reducing functional economic distances between cities and their hinterlands. The concept of “basins of integration�? is central to this strategy - geographically connected economic regions, often structured around key transport and trade corridors, where multisectoral investments are synchronized to drive agglomeration economies. Developing basins of integration demands coordinated upgrades to road, air transport, energy, and digital connectivity, alongside trade facilitation and institutional strengthening. A recent Horn of Africa infrastructure impact study estimates that such coordinated interventions could yield a 12 percent increase in the employment share of services and a 6.3 percent rise in real incomes in Somalia.7 Interventions in basins of integration can also unlock opportunities for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), microenterprises, and a range of service providers both directly, through transport and logistics, and indirectly, by supporting ancillary activities like supply chain management, veterinary services, and finance. Governance and institutional frameworks for infrastructure governance in Somalia are evolving, with increasing though uneven capacity across sectoral ministries and disaster risk management agencies. Overlapping mandates across several ministries, including the Ministry of Public Works, Reconstruction and Housing (MoPWRH), and the Ministry of Transport and Civil Aviation (MoTCA), among others, pose hurdles to centralizing planning and service delivery. Financial sustainability is an ongoing challenge, as infrastructure development relies heavily on donor support, with limited mechanisms for maintenance and reinvestment. Project Description The Project is Phase 1 of a wider MPA built around three pillars: Enabling Resilient Infrastructure for Access to Jobs and Growth, Institutional Transformation and Capacity Building, and Sustainable and Resilient Operations. Phase 1 consists of three components aligned with these MPA Pillars, as well as a fourth supporting implementation. Activities within the components have been selected based on clear criteria, including criticality to ensure safe, reliable, and climate-resilient connectivity throughout the country, addressing key bottlenecks in the delivery of urban services in regional hubs, and maximizing sustainable job creation, while ensuring feasibility in a volatile FCV context. Together, these components are designed to deliver early, visible results in high impact locations while embedding resilience, preparedness, and institutional strengthening as foundations for subsequent phases. 7 Matias Herrera Dappe and Mathilde Lebrand. “Infrastructure and Structural Change in the Horn of Africa�?. Impacts on biodiversity and ecosystem services may arise from land clearance, habitat disturbance, and changes in drainage or land use, particularly along corridor sections traversing peri-urban and rural areas. Cultural heritage risks are expected to be limited but cannot be ruled out, requiring screening and application of chance-find procedures during construction. Environmental and social risks. Environmental risks are related to construction-induced impacts, including air and noise pollution, dust generation, solid and hazardous waste, wastewater discharge, and increased demand for water, energy, and construction materials. The large footprint of corridor upgrading raises the potential for cumulative and indirect impacts on ecosystems, hydrology, and surrounding communities, while specific risks for the aviation facilities include health and safety risks at the airports' fuel storage facility; life and fire safety of airport users as public access buildings; emergency preparedness and response and potential legacy soil and groundwater contamination. Environmental risks and impacts are further compounded by weak regulatory oversight, limited hazardous-waste management capacity, and climate-related stressors, including flooding and extreme rainfall, which can exacerbate impacts if not adequately managed. On social side, the project faces high risks associated with community health and safety, land acquisition and resettlement, labor influx, and social cohesion. Civil works may lead to temporary or permanent physical and economic displacement, disruption of access and livelihoods, and increased exposure of communities to traffic hazards, accidents, Page 7 Official Use Only The World Bank Somalia Infrastructure Development for Access and Jobs Program, Phase 1 (P513127) and pollution. Somalia’s complex land tenure systems, prevalence of informal land use, and presence of vulnerable groups including internally displaced persons (IDPs), minority clans, women, and youth heighten the risk of exclusion, grievances, and conflict if impacts are not carefully managed. The project also presents elevated risks of gender-based violence (GBV), sexual exploitation and abuse, and sexual harassment (SEA/SH), driven by labor-intensive works, labor influx, power imbalances, and limited access to survivor support services. Security-related risks, including the potential use of security personnel to safeguard project assets, further increase the complexity of managing community safety in certain locations. Institutional capacity constraints, including uneven experience across implementing agencies and limited national and subnational E&S enforcement systems, represent a cross-cutting risk that could affect timely and effective mitigation. Pillar 3 introduces additional risks related to operational sustainability, requiring robust operations and maintenance arrangements, revenue mechanisms, and institutional capacity to ensure compliance with E&S requirements over the life of the assets. These risks will be managed through a comprehensive ESF-based approach, including site-specific Envronmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIAs)/Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMPs), Resettlement Action Plan (RAPs), Labor Management Procedures, Stakeholder Engagement Plans, Security Management measures, and Sexual Exploitation and Abuse / Sexual Harassment (SEA/SH) mitigation actions, supported by enhanced supervision, third-party support, and _For Official Use Only_ capacity building. Continuous stakeholder engagement and accessible grievance redress mechanisms, including GBV-specific channels, will be critical to maintaining community trust and managing risks throughout implementation. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements The project will have three implementing agencies: the MOF, MoTCA, and MoPWRH. Teams from these Ministries, jointly as a National MPA Coordination Unit, will be responsible for the overall implementation of all activities in the project. For urban drainage activities, the Banadir Regional Administration (BRA) Project Implementation Unit established under the SURP-II project will be leveraged. Where airport project sites align (in Mogadishu, Baidoa, Kismayo and Garowe), city level PIUs already in place under the SURP-II project will be leveraged to support implementation. The project will move away from stand-alone project structures and enable a gradual transition to greater reliance on civil servants, supported by upfront transition planning during Phase 1 to strengthen long-term sustainability and capacity.
