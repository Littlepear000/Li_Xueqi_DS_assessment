Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Tajikistan’s steady economic growth and poverty reduction were propelled by favorable external conditions, but GDP per capita remains low, job creation weak, and productivity improvements small. Real GDP growth averaged about 9 percent in 1998–08 before easing to an average of 7 percent in 2010–19. Income per capita grew from $162 in 2000 to above $859 in 2020 while poverty fell from over 80 percent to an estimated 26 percent. Despite that, per capita GDP remains the lowest amongst the former soviet countries. While the working- age population increased by 3 percent per year over 2007-17, net jobs rose only by 1.1 percent annually. Growth was mainly driven by the non-tradeable service and agricultural sectors that operate at low productivity levels. The growth contribution from the industrial sector has been rising but the sector is dominated by SOEs with the lowest productivity. Promoting private sector development, and in particular the development of high value- added sectors, will be critical for both productivity gains and job creation and in Tajikistan. 2. The COVID pandemic harmed Tajikistan’s economy, but the economy rebounded in 2021 thanks to the exports of precious materials and remittance-driven domestic demand. The pandemic revealed how reliance on predominantly unstable, low quality, and low-wage jobs in the informal sector exposes livelihoods to external shocks and raises risks related to fragility, conflict, and violence (FCV). The number of respondents to the World Bank Listening to Tajikistan survey reporting that “no household member had worked during the preceding week�? spiked to nearly 40 percent in May 2020. The growth rate slowed down to 4.5 percent in 2020 due to the COVID- 19 crisis but rebounded to 8.5 percent in the first half of 2021. This can be attributed to increased exports of precious materials and improved domestic demand, partly stemming from increased remittances. Foreign direct investment (FDI) also showed a strong recovery in the first half of 2021, rising by 43.7 percent (y/y) to about $118 million after a 47 percent (y/y) collapse in 2020. The FDI increase was driven mainly by foreign interest in the mining sector, with the country’s dependence on mining leaving the economy vulnerable to metal price Mar 21, 2022 Page 3 of 13 The World Bank Tajikistan Financial and Private Sector Development Project (P177722) fluctuations. 3. The war in Ukraine and Tajikistan’s high dependence on remittances and imports from Russia will hurt the economy. Tajikistan is highly exposed to economic activity in Russia, both directly and indirectly (through its trading partner Kazakhstan), while its direct exposure to Ukraine is low. Worker remittances from Russia constitute around 80 percent of the total inflow of remittances, or about 30 percent of GDP (on average). In parallel, imports from Russia amount to 12 percent of GDP. Exports to Russia and FDI inflows from Russia remain relatively small. Tajikistan is also exposed to an economic fallout in Kazakhstan, mainly through the trade channel. As a result of the crisis Tajikistan’s GDP is expected to contract in real terms in 2022, triggering a significant rise in poverty. Hiking global prices of fuel and food products and a pass-through from the local currency depreciation will trigger higher inflation and erode households’ purchasing power and welfare. The 15-percent devaluation of the somoni versus USD also amplified external debt service payments. Furthermore, the exclusion of several Russian banks from the SWIFT payment system can hurt Tajik banks that rely on Russian banks with correspondent accounts for cross-border payments. 4. Private sector-led diversification in the export market (focusing on regional integration, high-value addition, and knowledge spillovers) can increase growth and economic resilience. Out of Tajikistan’s entire US$1,120 million export portfolio in 2019, 65 percent were commodities, including minerals (24 percent), stone (17 percent), metals (14 percent), and cotton (11 percent). Raw extractive sectors typically do not create well- paid jobs and are at the lower end of global value chains. While 70 percent of the workforce is in agriculture, the sector accounts for only 22 percent of GDP (as a result of low productivity) and pays three times lower wages than the non-agriculture sector. SOEs continue to dominate the economic space and drain resources from the state budget. The preferential market position of SOEs prevents efficient allocation of resources and inhibits further private sector development. In 2018, Tajikistan’s new business registration stood at 0.2 per 1000 people aged 14 to 65 compared with 2 for Kazakhstan and 1.9 for Moldova. 5. Weak financial intermediation remains a key obstacle for efficient capital reallocation, private sector- led growth, and productive SME development. Deposits as a share of GDP were at 9.37 percent in 2020, much lower than in comparator countries such as Kyrgyz Republic (26.9 percent) and Moldova (37.7 percent). Such a low deposit mobilization constraints financial intermediation. The credit to private sector in Tajikistan stood at 13 percent of GDP by the end of 2020, significantly lower than the average of 42 percent for lower middle-income countries and 57 percent for countries in Europe and Central Asia (ECA). Sectoral and Institutional Context Deposit Mobilization 6. Lacking confidence in the banking sector contributes to low deposit mobilization. Only 47 percent of adults have an account with a formal financial institution, and just 11.3 percent have formal savings. Cash held outside the financial system is significant. Tajik retail depositors hold only TJS 930 (US$82) in bank accounts on average, or 10 percent of the country’s per capita income - a low volume, especially given that households lack alternative means of saving other than bank deposits. In general, a quarter of the population doesn’t have accounts at local banks for lack of trust in financial institutions. Notably, recent restrictions in accessing funds at Tojiksodirobonk (TSB) and Agroinvestbonk (AIB) further hurt confidence in the banking system. A credible deposit insurance scheme can support deposit mobilization, but the Tajik scheme has operational and funding weaknesses, and the complementary bank resolution framework suffers from implementation gaps. The proposed project will focus on addressing the identified gaps in IDIF’s capabilities to perform its mandate.1 IDIF reforms include increasing its recovery ratios in future payouts, and incorporating lessons learned from the TSB and AIB payout - namely, the need for further automation to hasten the payout process and for raising public awareness on the deposit insurance scheme and its qualities. Furthermore, the liquidations of TSB and AIB in 2021 enabled IDIF to initiate the payout of insured depositors, but depleted its reserves to around 100 million somoni, far below the target level of seven percent of individual deposits or 350 million somoni. It would take over ten years for IDIF to reach this target based on collecting new premiums from banks (and assuming no further payouts). One key complement of deposit insurance is the bank resolution framework that enables efficiently dealing with failing banks while protecting the bank franchise value. The framework is relatively modern in Tajikistan but suffers from implementation gaps. For example, the National Bank of Tajikistan (NBT) has yet to pass regulations required for the practical adoption of resolution tools. Financial Intermediation 8. Banks allocate capital to the private sector inefficiently, and especially MSMEs face high collateral requirements. Banks dominate the financial sector and have a long history of weak performance due to poor lending practices. Historically, some banks have been subjected to extensive directed lending at subsidized rates for priority projects, politically connected borrowers, and loss-making SOEs. These practices have discouraged adequate risk assessment and made it costly for private firms to access bank credit. In 2019, only 18 percent of private firms had a bank loan or line of credit, compared with an average of 37.6 percent in ECA. Among MSMEs that wanted to apply for credit but did not in 2019, most cite high rates and high collateral as the main reason. The level of collateral needed to obtain a loan stood at 125 percent. Consequently, only 2.5 percent of private firms reported using banks to finance investment, compared with 13.2 percent in ECA. The low credit intermediation has made it difficult for private firms to finance working capital and expand their activities or undertake new investments using more efficient external sources. 9. Female-headed MSMEs’ access to finance and female participation in MSMEs could improve. Fewer than one-fifth of female-headed SMEs have a bank loan or line of credit to finance investments or working capital shortages. Although almost all female-led firms, 95 percent have an account at a financial institution, only 17.7 percent of such MSMEs have a bank loan or line of credit. Around 96 percent of businesses with a female as the top manager used internal resources to finance investments. Overall, female participation in employment, top management, and firm ownership are amongst the lowest in the Caucasus and Central Asia region. Addressing this gap could stimulate productivity and private sector expansion, leading to a decline in unemployment (with the young female unemployment rate at 12 percent in 2019). 10. Partial credit guarantees supporting MSME access to finance could be expanded, including for female- headed and female-owned firms. Such risk-sharing facilities can support MSMEs facing collateral constraints in normal times and higher risk aversion by banks during crises – such as the COVID-19 and the war in Ukraine. There are two partial credit guarantee schemes in Tajikistan - Zamonat and the Credit Guarantee Fund Tajikistan (CGFT). While they have demonstrated high demand, they had limited results due to constrained financial resources and some design issues. SMEs and Value Chain Linkages 11. Tajikistan has several economic sectors with latent comparative advantage but lacks the agency to improve MSME competitiveness, integration into value chains, and international market linkages. There are several sectors with potential comparative advantage for enhanced connectivity into value chains, including inter alia: (i) high-value-added agribusiness value chains such as dried fruits and nuts, due to Tajikistan’s ability to produce off supply peak; (ii) international tourism, leveraging Tajikistan’s strategic location along the ancient silk road and its cultural and natural sites; and, (iii) ready-made garments given Tajikistan’s comparative advantage in raw cotton and cotton yarns, and surplus labor. Nevertheless, private sector activity is overall hampered by a difficult business environment,2 and there is currently no agency with a mandate to increase the market linkages of MSMEs to large local and multinational corporations operating in Tajikistan or the region. While a 2014 Law on state entrepreneurship support sets the framework and envisions budget allocation for the entrepreneurship support programs, there is no robust set of government-backed interventions for MSME capacity building that could unleash Tajikistan’s local comparative advantages. 12. Firm capabilities—notably in the non-agriculture private sector—need to improve for MSMEs to better connect to and move up the local, regional, and global value chains. In 2019, only 2.2 percent of Tajik firms had an internationally recognized quality certification, compared with 22 percent in ECA. Tajik firms are much less likely to develop new products/services (only 19 percent introduced a new product/service in 2019, compared with 29 percent in ECA)3, new process innovations (only 10 percent introduced new process innovations in 2019, compared with 20 percent in ECA), or use digital means to do business (only 28 percent have own website, compared with 50 percent in ECA). 13. Supplier development programs can benefit MSMEs specializing in the lower-value-added industries and suffering from limited market linkages in value chains. Only 1.6 percent of small firms and 4.6 percent of medium-sized firms exported directly in 2019, compared with 14 percent for small firms and 25 percent for medium-sized firms in ECA. Firms that connect to the international market are typically the large ones, with 11.9 percent of them exporting directly in 2019 and mostly specializing in producing and exporting raw materials and commodities. Target government support for MSMEs has proved successful in creating market linkages with large companies and helping MSMEs move up the private sector value chains in other CA and ECA countries, especially in tradable sectors. C. Proposed Development Objective(s) The Project Development Objective is to strengthen deposit insurance, promote bank lending to MSMEs, and boost MSME integration in value chains. Component 1: Deposit Insurance (US$ 12-20 million). Based on the World Bank’s technical assistance on deposit insurance and the broader financial safety net, this component will use a combination of disbursement conditions, a performance-based condition (PBC), investments and technical assistance to improve depositor trust, mitigate the likelihood of bank runs, and contribute to greater deposit mobilization.4 This component will include: a. Subcomponent 1.1: IDIF financing (US$ 10.6-18.7 million, based on a PBC). Depositor payouts in 2021 depleted IDIF‘s reserves. This subcomponent will fund the partial replenishment of IDIF’s reserves that will be complemented by backup funding arrangements from the government,5 and with the remaining gap to be gradually closed by the inflow of industry contributions. Notably, reserves above the IDIF target (due to industry contributions over time) will result in the annual transfer of the equivalent amount to the MoF to be utilized for developmental purposes aligned with the PDO, including under the PRSF or SDP instruments. b. Subcomponent 1.2: Payout automation (US$1 million). Prompt payout is crucial for maintaining confidence in the banking system and preventing bank runs. This subcomponent will provide IDIF with systems to access information on deposits from banks and monitor the banks’ deposit base (Single Customer View software), allowing IDIF to expedite its payouts. It will also cover the execution of one payout simulation exercise to ensure proper coordination between IDIF and NBT on payout issues. c. Subcomponent 1.3: Public awareness campaign (US$ 0.3 million). Trust in banks and deposit mobilization can only be achieved if the population is aware of the deposit guarantee system. This subcomponent will develop a comprehensive public awareness strategy for IDIF and initiate its implementation. The component will finance a PRSF and related capacity building to share through Guarantee Funds (GFs) the credit risk of eligible MSME loans extended by eligible participating financial institutions (PFIs), in order to support MSMEs facing collateral constraints or higher risk aversion by banks—with the use of disbursement conditions. This component will include: a. Subcomponent 2.1: PRSF financing (US$ 16.5 million). This subcomponent will fund an MOF-established PRSF account, with MOF signing agent agreements with eligible GFs to manage designated PRSF windows, including signing agreements with eligible PFIs, registering new eligible guarantees, processing claims payouts, tracking recovery proceeds, and managing the investment of the allocated funds. The PRSF will have windows or key performance indicators (KPIs) for both short-term working capital loans and long-term investment loans.6 The PRSF may include dedicated windows or KPIs to support innovative MSMEs/start-ups, firms seeking to transition to low carbon and climate-resilient technologies, women-led MSMEs, and firms that require financing to integrate into value chains (including beneficiaries of the SDP under Component 3). The exact number and features of the windows or KPIs will be defined in the operations manual to provide flexibility for adjustments based on periodical market assessments. b. Subcomponent 2.2: Capacity building (US$ 3 million). The capacity building will be for the PRSF, PFIs, ultimate beneficiaries, NBT, and other stakeholders. It will also include trainings for vulnerable/ underserved and innovative MSMEs/ start-ups on how to prepare a proposal for a banking credit. 19. This component will also involve gender sensitive activities which will aim to increase the number of loans given to female owned businesses. Specifically, there will be three gender -sensitive activities incorporated into this project component. a) Training for credit officers on gender financing b) Workshops to Participating Financial Institutions (PFIs) highlighting gender programs C. Mini MBA’s for female entrepreneurs to increase their capacity for business management and ability to access loans. 20. Component 3: Supplier Development Program (SDP, US$ 8.5 million). This component will fund capacity building and partial reimbursement grants for MSMEs to strengthen their linkages with large local and multinational corporations. These linkages can boost MSME capabilities and competitiveness, laying the foundation for productivity and export-led growth, and helping attract and retain more FDI. Both environmental and social risks are rated Moderate for the proposed project; therefore the overall ESF risk is also Moderate. The following standards are relevant to the Project: ESS 1, ESS 2, ESS 3, ESS4, ESS 9 and ESS 10. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 23. The Project Management Unit (PMU) will be placed in an existing central Government agency at the MoF. The PMU will ensure procurement and financial management (FM) in accordance with World Bank fiduciary policies and guidelines; will develop an Operations Manual to implement the Project; and will coordinate/engage in agent agreements with the Project Implementation Groups represented by the identified agencies in charge of implementing the instruments. In particular, for each Instrument: a. Deposit Insurance: the MoF will be responsible for implementing this component. Its FM capacity is adequate to meet the World Bank’s requirements (see discussion of Fiduciary isk below). Currently, Zamonat and CGFT/TCSP are operating in Tajikistan and have expressed interest in participation. The WB undertook a confidential assessment based on international best practices and the local context. c. SDP: the Export Development Agency was identified based on a mapping of agencies supporting MSME development and will take responsibility for SDP implementation. A consulting company with recognized international experience in managing and implementing SDP will be engaged to support the Agency.
