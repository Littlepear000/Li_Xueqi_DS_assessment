Mali is a landlocked and fragile country with a rapidly growing population of about 22.6 million and a per capita gross domestic product (GDP) of US$907.6 in 2022. Mali is ranked 186 out of 191 countries in the United Nation’s Human Development Index in 2022. The country is experiencing one of the highest population growths in the world averaging 2.9 percent a year, and its population is projected to double by 2045, increasing the demand for social and infrastructure services, including energy services. Meanwhile, 40 percent of the population continues to live under the national poverty line and 90 percent reside in southern rural areas. There is a large wealth disparity between rural and urban areas with significant associated gender gaps. With a median age of around 16 years, youth unemployment represents a major challenge, as the number of young job seekers is estimated at 300,000 per year, while the formal sector can only provide around 50,000 jobs per year. These issues have been exacerbated recently by security and political crises, with additional socioeconomic vulnerabilities particularly in rural areas of the country.1 2. Mali’s economy experienced limited inclusive growth over the last decade but remained highly vulnerable to external, climatic, and political shocks. Annual GDP growth was relatively limited before the 2020 pandemic, averaging 4.5 percent over 2014-2019 (1.6 percent per capita) driven by private consumption and public investments in roads and social infrastructures. The economy experienced little structural change over the last decade and remains dominated by agriculture and low-productivity services, while manufacturing is small and concentrated in agro-industries and cotton ginning. This combined with the high concentration of exports in gold and cotton, exposes the country to commodity prices and climatic shocks. The low GDP per capita growth also limited progress in poverty reduction while human development indicators show mixed results. These challenges have been further exacerbated since 2020 with the COVID-19 pandemic, external shocks, growing insecurity, and political crises. Annual GDP growth averaged 1.2 percent over 2020–2022 (-1.7 percent per capita) because of the aforementioned shocks. These crises have also significantly affected the most vulnerable households. For instance, the poverty rate increased by 4.8 percentage point due to the pandemic2, pushing 900,000 more people below the national poverty line. 3. Mali has been experiencing instability and conflict since the 2012 security crisis, exacerbated by the military coups of August 2020 and May 2021. Mali has become increasingly fragile over the past 10 years. Since the 2012 rebellion, violent extremist upsurge, and coup d’etat, the country has been experiencing recurrent instability, conflict, and violence despite the 2015 Algiers Peace Accord (Accord pour la Paix et la Reconciliation au Mali). Since 2017, the situation has deteriorated in Mopti and Segou regions in the center, and recently conflict and violence are also gradually moving to the Koulikoro, Kayes, and Sikasso regions in the south of Mali. Aceess to basic services is very limited in many regions. The lack of progress in education and health performance indicators can be attributed to the deterioration of security conditions, particularly in the north and center regions. Political tensions and insecurity have also led to a surge of internally displaced people, generating new demand for services that the country is not able to meet. 1Source: World Bank, 2021. https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/mali/overview. 2Source: World Bank, 2022. “Mali Economic Update - Resilience in Uncertain times: renewing the social contract” https://www.worldbank.org/en/country/mali/publication/mali-economic-update-resilience-in-uncertain-times-renewing-the-social- contract 9 The World Bank Mali Electricity System Reinforcement and Access Expansion Project (Yelen Sira) (P176633) 4. Electricity access and quality of electricity services are among the top priorities for Mali in its efforts to support economic and social recovery following COVID-19 as well as political and security instability. To recover from the multiple crises the country faces, Mali has elaborated its Strategic Framework for Economic Recovery and Sustainable Development (Cadre Stratégique pour la Relance Economique et le Développement Durable) (2019–20233), and the transitional government has demonstrated commitment to implement that strategy. Increased access to reliable and affordable electricity services is a top priority for the Government of Mali (GoM) to power socioeconomic development activities. The GoM sees expanded access to reliable energy as an important input to creating jobs; diversifying the economy; improving human capital outcomes; reducing fragility; and improving the delivery of basic services, such as health and education, in rural areas as well as agricultural production through enabling irrigation and refrigeration. The GoM’s objective, as outlined in the new electrification strategy4 (Programme National d’Amélioration de l’Access à l’Electricité au Mali, PNAAEM)5 that has been recently updated in November 2022 with IDA’s support, is to expand sustainable electricity access for rural transformation, prioritizing potential economic nodes with agro-processing and mining activities. 5. Energy poverty has been identified as one of the key drivers of fragility in Mali, as in the other G5 Sahel countries.6 The provision of energy services is a key part of Mali’s strategy to improve living conditions of the population and of the strategy for mitigating the risk of conflict and violence (2020–2023) that the GoM recently adopted. Increasing access to electricity in conflict areas, in the northern and central regions, will enhance resilience and serve as a prevention measure. In accordance with this strategy, the electrification of high-risk areas can contribute to preventing conflict spillover effects and increasing trust between the state and citizens. Addressing energy poverty will also benefit women and girls as they often have to spend more time fulfilling household chores manually, including, including fetching firewood and water, and as a result, have less time for income-generating and educational activities. Mali Risk and Resilience Assessment The Sahel Regional Risk and Resilience Assessment, completed in February 2020, was complemented in July 2021 by the Mali Risk and Resilience Assessment Update following recent political developments. Both documents highlight how the emergence of violent conflicts in the Sahel is associated with exclusion and perceptions of injustice and marginalization emerging from several drivers, including the lack of public confidence and trust in state institutions; the erosion of the distribution of basic services and infrastructure, including transport and energy, which is plagued by inequity and insufficient quality; and competition for natural resources. These drivers are also highlighted in the recent World Bank Mali Economic Update7 2022 entitled Resilience in Uncertain Times: Renewing the Social Contract . One of the solutions identified is an adjustment to the spatial distribution of investments, through additional investment in neglected or marginalized territories, with potentially multisector projects supporting the deployment of infrastructure, services, and governance systems across territories, focusing on territorial and social inclusion. This has been incorporated in the recently finalized geospatial electrification rollout plan and in the selection of sites to be electrified. Mali has low carbon emissions, but it is highly vulnerable to climate change, 8 which will affect its agricultural sector and pose a direct threat to food security and household income, particularly for rural households. The country’s undiversified economy and high dependance on rain-fed agriculture, in a region where droughts, floods, and locust infestations occur regularly, makes household incomes and overall GDP growth prone to shocks. Mali is already experiencing high natural risks, including flooding (river and urban), water scarcity, extreme heat, and wildfires,9 which are expected to increase further due to climate change. Increased pressure on water and food resources will exacerbate poverty and conflicts between communities. As documented in Annex 8, while already one of the hottest countries in the word, Mali will also suffer from increased temperature (the annual mean temperature is expected to rise by about 2°C by mid-century).10 This will also have a bearing on electricity system design and operation. The increase in heat will affect electricity grid losses, transfer capacity of the transmission lines, efficiency of power plants, and operations and performance of battery energy storage systems (BESS). Moreover, Mali ranks 174 (out of 181) in the vulnerability index on climate change.11 The lack of adequate infrastructure and social safety nets limit the ability of the country and its population to adapt and respond to climate change impacts. In this context, the country is determined to act on climate change and presented its priorities in its updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC). By 2030, Mali aims to reduce its emissions by 31 percent in the energy sector compared to a business-as-usual scenario (notably through the development of its solar resources); to take measures for adaptation, particularly for sustainable land management and to combat desertification, given the increasing competition for natural resources that are poorly regulated and under pressure from demographic growth. In the energy sector, in addition to the reliance of the power sector on fossil fuels, households are still heavily dependent on biomass (primarily firewood) for cooking and heating, which has negative health and environmental impacts, and represents 75 percent of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in the energy sector. B. Strategic and Institutional Context Mali Power System Status 7. Mali's current power system is fragmented and faces three interlinked challenges: (i) high costs of generation, mainly due to heavy reliance on expensive thermal generation; (ii) low levels of access to electricity services; and (iii) a sector characterized by a lack of efficiency and equity in energy services provision. The power system is organized into an interconnected national grid serving 30 urban centers including the capital Bamako, 35 isolated distribution networks in rural areas, and two localities connected to the Côte d’Ivoire distribution network, all operated by the National Electricity Utility (Energie du Mali, EDM). In 2021, the total installed capacity in Mali comprised 781MW and 53 MWp, of which 663 MW and 50 MWp for the interconnected grid; and 118 MW and 3 MWp for the isolated centers. Unsustainably high cost of generation, aging and saturated grid infrastructure, deficient performance of the utility, and tariffs below the costs of service have resulted in the structural financial deficit of the sector, which limits Mali’s ability to deliver reliable and affordable electricity services to all and weighs heavily on its fiscal balance. Hydroelectric and thermal power plants represent the main sources of energy generated at the national level. Thermal power represented 56.86 percent of the generation mix in 2021, and this makes the country vulnerable to fluctuating fuel prices in international markets, especially after the hike in oil products prices on the international markets, partly resulting from the Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. Mali has also suffered from a deficit of imports since December 2020 due to insufficient available generation in Côte d’Ivoire, which has led the country to increase its reliance on costly and polluting emergency diesel rental power, in addition to load shedding.16 Due to the high reliance on thermal power plants and high technical and nontechnical losses, the costs of supply are particularly high (estimated at US$0.25 per kWh in 2021). With a weighted average tariff of US$0.16 per kWh (US$0.17 per kWh for LV customers and US$0.14 per kWh for MV customers), the price of electricity is not sufficient to cover the full cost of service, thereby resulting in a structural deficit of around US$0.10 per unit of electricity billed. 9. These developments have significantly deteriorated the financial situation of EDM-SA, negatively affecting maintenance and investments in grid infrastructure. To respond to these multiple challenges and restore the financial viability of the sector in the medium term, a sector recovery plan was adopted in 2019 and updated in 2020 which sought to eliminate subsidies within five years. This plan aims at reducing the cost of generation from around CFAF 87 per kWh in 2021 to CFAF 63 per kWh in 2025. These objectives are aligned with the electricity master plan adopted in 2015 (covering 2015–2035) and with the electricity Sector Investment Plan (2022–2026) adopted by the EDM-SA’s Board of Directors , which aims at recovering costs while also accelerating electricity access. The main objectives of the national Mali Sustainable Energy and Improved Service delivery for Increased Stability DPF series were to focus on (a) turning around the financial situation of the electricity sector to reduce its financial toll on public finances and improve the reliability of services; (b) improving the efficiency of the agriculture subsidy program to reduce its budgetary burden and enhance land tenure security to support increased productivity; and (c) reinforcing fiscal decentralization for improved services and stability. While the DPF17 Phase-1 was approved in 2020, preparation of the DPF Phase-218 was interrupted due to political and institutional changes in the country. Similarly, Mali participated in the Regional Program to Improve the Performance of Power Utilities DPF Phase 119, and due to political and institutional changes in many participating countries including Mali, preparation of Phase 2 could not be pursued. 10. The LCDP identified solar PV as a major source of generation to phase out thermal generation including expensive rental power, meet capacity deficit, and reduce costs of generation. The LCDP states that from 2026 onward, thermal energy in the generation mix would have been reduced from its current share (~57 percent) to 20 percent – by the development of 600 MWp solar power capacity and 150 MW wind capacity, and storage. Mali is endowed with excellent solar potential, with an average solar radiation estimated at 5 –7 kWh per m2 per day and well distributed, and a daily sunlight duration of 7 to 10 hours. The continuous decline in investment costs of solar electricity generation23 and BESS represents a unique opportunity for the country. Yet, today, solar energy remains unexploited and much-needed transmission and distribution network reinforcements limit the integration of new generation projects. Developing least-cost solar power projects on a competitive basis will contribute to increase domestic generation capacity, diversify sources of electricity supply, accelerate the shift away from expensive thermal production, and significantly lower the cost of electricity generation in Mali. Despite significant investments over the last 12 years, Mali’s access rate to electricity services remains low, especially in rural areas, and overall unreliable. The current access rate of 51 percent is about the average for Sub-Saharan Africa. In the past 12 years, EDM-SA’s customer base grew from 120,000 households to over 700,00030. There are currently 749,025 customers in the interconnected network and 75,202 in the isolated network in regional city centers. However, rural areas suffer from limited access to electricity (24.7 percent) and primarily rely on direct wood fuel and kerosene lamps for lighting. Rural electrification for socioeconomic transformation is a priority in the economic development agenda of the Government. 19. The national electrification strategy and geospatial least-cost access rollout program (PNAAEM)31 finalized in November 2022, which is informed by the Rural Electrification Master Plan (REMP) of 2010 and the Optimal Investment Master Plan (PDIO) of 2015, provides policy guidance for future grid and off-grid investment projects to meet the country’s ambitious access targets. Priority investments defined by the two cited master plans are valid and all new operations are guided by them, as funding is still lacking to cover all the identified investment needs. The master plans prioritize electrification of large communities closer to the existing MV lines and those around substations. The REMP identified about 10,900 localities that are to be electrified (representing 25 percent of the population), of which 69 percent have more than 800 people, 40 percent have population ranging between 800 and 2,000, and 6 percent of unelectrified localities have more than 2,000 people. The survey conducted in 2005 to inform REMP indicated that an average rural household in Mali spends about CFAF 8,500 per month on energy (which is sufficient to buy at least 85 kWh from EDM-SA or 50 kWh from mini grids in rural areas) and is willing to pay CFAF 176.5 per kWh for electricity, compared to the current average tariff of about CFAF 100 per kWh. The survey therefore confirmed that even at the current prices in urban and rural areas, electricity service in Mali is affordable. The GoM has set an ambitious plan to scale up access to electricity from 51 percent to 78 percent by 2025, aiming at universal access by 2030. The geospatial analysis estimated that universal access would require about US$3 billion, including US$1.92 billion for transmission, US$360 million for distribution, US$656 million for mini grids, and US$58 million for standalone systems solutions. Seventy-three percent of the population provided with new access would receive it from the grid while the remaining 27 percent would receive it from mini grids/off-grid solutions. Once it is formally adopted by the Government, the electrification strategy and geospatial rollout plan will guide the investments of the projects to support the country achieve its ambitious access objectives. 20. Institutional Framework for the Energy Sector in Mali 24. Although Mali’s electricity sector was long dominated by public sector institutions, it was liberalized by the Presidential Order No. 00-019/P-RM of March 15, 2000. The Ministry of Mines, Energy, and Water (Ministère des Mines, de l’Energie et de l’Eau, MMEE) is responsible for the implementation of the government’s policy in the energy sector. Within the MMEE, the national directorate of energy (Direction Nationale de l’Energie, DNE) develops the elements of this policy and ensures the technical control of the regional and sub- regional services which contribute to its implementation. (a) The GoM has a concession agreement with the vertically integrated state-owned utility, EDM-SA, which is responsible for electricity service delivery in Mali. EDM-SA owns the interconnected grid and manages the isolated small grids in regional centers. Within the perimeter of its concession, EDM-SA has monopoly over power transmission and distribution, while part of the generation is open to the private sector,36 but EDM-SA is the single buyer. EDM-SA is also responsible for cross-border electricity trade (export and import). CREE’s mandate, which is limited to EDM’s concession perimeter, is to protect customers, promote competition, when possible, arbitrate disputes between the GoM and operators, and approve adjustments to ensure cost reflective tariffs. The capability of the regulator to effectively regulate the sector remains, however, limited today, especially regarding tariff setting. IDA is providing technical assistance to CREE to put in place a transparent system for tariff adjustment. (c) Outside the EDM-SA concession area, AMADER was created in 2003, with the mandate to provide electricity services to the population in remote areas. It supplies electricity through a PPP approach and acts as de facto regulator for rural electrification. Financial Situation of EDM-SA 26. EDM-SA has been facing a structural financial deficit and is dependent on an operational subsidy from the Government, which is however not sufficient to cover the overall tariff revenue shortfall. The sector suffers from a structural financial deficit mainly due to (a) the high electricity costs and tariffs below the costs of electricity supply; (b) the inability to significantly raise tariffs in a fragile environment resulting in a tariff deficit of US$0.10 per kWh of electricity sold in 2021; and (c) operating inefficiencies at EDM-SA with combined technical and commercial losses reaching 23.49 percent in 2021. The amount of subsidy provided to EDM-SA stood at CFAF 63 billion in 2021 (~US$114 million equivalent), which corresponds to about US$0.026 per kWh of electricity sold that year, significantly below the tariff revenue shortfall. This level of operating subsidy is far from sufficient to cover the sector operating costs, let alone cover the investment needs. Rationale for World Bank Involvement and Role of Partners 73. There is a clear rationale for the World Bank’s involvement given the crucial role energy can play in the development of in Mali and the World Bank’s strategy for energy access in the region. Energy access is critical for economic growth, increased productivity and competitiveness, industrialization, and digital transformation, as well as human development and jobs. Many communities in Mali without access to energy are encountering difficulties operating businesses, generating an income, and delivering adequate health and education services. The high costs of electricity generation in the sector due to the high dependance on fossil fuel require investments to shift to least-cost renewable energy. Yet, the financial situation of the sector prevents the country from making these investments, and requires the mobilization of private sector finance, which the World Bank, with the SRMI approach, is uniquely positioned to enable. The weakness of the current network and the critical need for upgrades to integrate VRE is typically not conducive to public-private arrangements, particularly in an environment with high financial risks and limited customer base. The World Bank, however, is uniquely positioned to provide financing for these public investments, noting that these will then enable greater integration of IPPs, and hence greater private sector mobilization. The ambition of the Mali’s access strategy requires a multiyear strategy with a dramatic scale-up of resources, which the World Bank can bring, along with international and regional experience on access models, both for on-grid and off-grid. 74. The World Bank has been engaged in the energy sector in Mali for a long time and is playing a leading role through multiple operations. This project has strong complementarity with the current World Bank energy activities in Mali. It is supporting the Government in many ways to transform its energy sector to eventually deliver affordable, reliable, and sustainable energy to all through sector and fiscal reform, regional integration, national grid strengthening and extension, and private sector mobilization to develop its solar resources. The World Bank is playing an important role in supporting the GoM energy access expansion program. By its convening power, it will continue to support the GoM to mobilize financing for the access program. The World Bank has worked closely with IFC and MIGA in the preparation of this project to identify ways to mobilize private investments in generation, mini-grids development, and off-grid solar deployment, as well as with the AfDB, IsDB and AFD, among others, to ensure that respective donor engagements in Mali are complementary and reinforcing each other. The project will pursue this coordinated approach and close coordination with all donors involved in Mali during implementation (see Annex 3). 75. A central challenge in the energy sector in Mali is strengthening capacities at EDM-SA and at AMADER, the pivotal implementing agencies for access scale-up in the sector. The World Bank’s role is key as it plays a pioneer role in supporting the GOM in designing the power sector recovery reforms to improve the financial viability, governance, and operational performance of EDM-SA through DPFs and IPFs. It also played a pioneer role in supporting the GoM with the creation of AMADER to pilot mini-grids and off-grid solutions with REHSP and HEURA. F. While the mini-grid model in Mali has been built following a ‘bottom-up approach’, new business models are critical to ensure increased and sustainable private capital and ownership, cost effectiveness, enhanced system performance, and improved quality of service. The project will incorporate lessons learned from previously World Bank-funded projects, HEURA and REHSP and the off-grid solar RBF to better serve rural customers. It is necessary to enhance competition through fair, open, and transparent selection. New PPP business models for mini-grid development would encourage local operators, large regional mini-grid developers, and international firms (tender approach) to enter the competition with the objective of increasing private sector participation (financing and ownership) and ensuring long-term sustainability. The tender approach for mini-grids is being tested in countries such as Niger, Nigeria, Chad, Guinea and DRC. Developing mini-grids one at a time is not financially optimal. Therefore, AMADER would bundle several sites into lots of sufficient sizes and bid them out to reach the scale required to attract qualified developers. Setting adequate and cost-reflective tariffs is important to attract private sector operators to develop mini-grids in access-deficit countries. 79. Proper PPP planning requires upstream project development studies. Experience in many Sub Saharan African countries shows that lack of upstream project development studies leads to multiple unsolicited bids with expensive terms that are translated to high consumer prices or government subsidies, if at all the projects are implemented. The utility scale solar PV project design is based on detailed feasibility studies and transaction advisory services supported by the World Bank. The project will follow a ‘plug-and-play’ approach, whereby an auction will be launched to competitively procure the solar park (in the form of IPPs) on pre-selected and pre- authorized sites to reduce development risks for the private sector. 80. Proper planning for new generation is critical to avoid overloading the transmission and distribution networks or stranded capacities. Because of improper planning in the past, HV transmission lines and transformers in key substations in Bamako are congested and overloaded, which limits the ability of the network to absorb additional generation or dispatch available generation. This is the case, for example, for the 92 MW thermal plan of Albatros, which is not being used to its full capacity because of the saturation of the Manantali transmission line. This situation compromises the reliability of service and increases technical losses for the national grid. This project would support the upgrading of the transmission and distribution system to help address these issues. 81. The proposed project design has incorporated lessons from the Burkina Faso Solar Energy and Access Project (P166785) for the development of the solar park. In particular, early experience with the preparation and implementation of this project highlights the critical importance of clear planning and sequencing of generation development and grid updates to ensure smooth integration of VRE and avoid risks of curtailment. C. Sustainability 88. The sustainability of the Mali power sector and the investments financed under this project will depend upon (a) the GoM’s continued commitment to support and implement a power sector reform program, including implementation of a least-cost generation plan to progressively eliminate government subsidies; (b) the financial health of EDM-SA and its ability to generate sufficient revenues to fully cover its expenditures; (c) the ability of new consumers to pay the electricity consumption once connected; and (d) EDM’s ability to maintain and operate the assets sustainably. The project is part of a large sector development program to shift the generation mix from high reliance, expensive thermal power to least-cost imports from neighboring countries and low-cost domestic renewable energy to reduce the supply costs; reform EDM-SA to improve governance and operational performance; moderate increases of tariffs; and expand access. The access expansion’s impact on EDM’s financial deficit is marginal and will not significantly contribute to increasing subsidies. The project will support activities to promote the productive use of electricity in selected areas for mini-grids. This will help end users afford electricity services. Furthermore, the involvement of the private sector in mini-grids development with a focus on renewable energy technologies will contribute to bring down operational costs and ensure sustainability of service delivery. Rationale for Public Sector Provisioning/Financing 98. There is a strong rationale for public sector financing for the project. Transmission and distribution infrastructure will be financed through public resources and conditions will be created for private financing in generation. For the rural electrification component, public financing will be provided to ‘prime the pump’ and lower the price for end users, with private financing deployed wherever suitable to ensure sustainability of electricity service provision. Electrification is often not commercially viable since initially revenue from the sale of additional electricity is low and only accumulates over time as consumption and the ability to pay increase. Furthermore, in Mali, electricity tariffs are considerably below the costs of supply, which hinder incentives to significantly accelerate the rate at which electricity access is provided though the grid, especially in situations where the utility is in dire financial situation and is forced to resort to commercial debt to fully cover its operating expenditures and required maintenance. There is therefore a need for public sector intervention to significantly accelerate the rate at which electricity access is provided in the country. Further to that, the project also aims to provide electricity access in conflict zones, areas that are not attractive for the private sector, albeit where intervention is necessary to ensure socioeconomic development in any community, and thus can be seen as a key priority for sustained development of the country. EDM-SA is heavily reliant on operating subsidies and other forms of government support to remain in operation. The deficit before subsidy reached a peak in 2019 at an estimated US$205 million representing 57 percent of the utility revenue from electricity sales. The deficit decreased slightly in 2020 (48 percent of revenue), a trend which continued in 2021 and is expected to continue until 2025. The key drivers to reduce this deficit would be the improvement in the generation mix and the reduction in the share of costly thermal generation. Significant delays in adding domestic renewable generation or securing additional import sources is a critical risk for the success of the recovery plan. In this respect, the project contributes to the sector financial recovery strategy. 104. The project's overall impact is expected to be insignificant for EDM-SA cash flow assuming electricity tariffs remain constant in real terms. At current tariff levels, connecting additional clients around Bamako and in the rest of the country would mechanically generate financial losses for EDM-SA since tariffs do not cover the incremental costs of supply. This negative impact would however be offset by the replacement of thermal generation with much cheaper PV electricity under an IPP. Overall, the Financial Internal Rate of Return (FIRR) of the project for EDM-SA is estimated at 3.1 percent. The project's financial impact would be greatly improved in a scenario of electricity tariff adjustments since the solar generation savings would remain unchanged and the cost for EDM-SA related to incremental demand would be reduced. In addition, in case the second phase of the solar park were to be implemented, the financial impact of the project on EDM-SA would become significantly positive. Overall, while increasing the share of renewables (domestic and imported) in EDM-SA generation mix is expected to be a key lever to put the sector back on the path of financial viability, adjustments of electricity tariff levels remain necessary. This annex presents the economic and financial analysis of the project, describes EDM’s historical financial performance and main challenges, and justifies the need for public financing as the appropriate vehicle to reach the outcomes of the project. It demonstrates strong economic and financial returns as well as substantial GHG mitigation benefits. Project Rationale 2. Mali’s power sector is facing numerous challenges, including high cost of supply, low level and capacity of investment, and low access rates. In 2020, the weighted average realized tariff for customers served by EDM-SA was US$0.18 per kWh for LV customers and US$0.16 per kWh for MV customers. This tariff is relatively high compared to the Sub-Saharan Africa average (though lower than comparable Sahelian landlocked countries) and yet, it is not cost-reflective, resulting in a deficit of around US$0.10 per kWh. This imbalance contributes to the critical financial situation of EDM-SA and, in turn, to the lack of adequate maintenance and investments. These high costs of service are primarily a result of suboptimal generation mix with a significant reliance on fossil fuels, including emergency rental power plants, and structural geographical factors (landlocked country with high transport/logistical costs and low overall density), and further aggravated by the relatively poor operational and commercial performance (for example, relatively high technical and commercial losses). A key challenge for EDM-SA is to meet the current and increasing demand for electricity (close to 10 percent increase per year), which requires constant reinforcing of transmission and distribution capacity, as well as generation capacity additions. 3. The GoM is committed to shifting the generation mix toward least-cost imports and domestic renewable generation, to meet its increasing demand and deliver affordable and reliable electricity to its population. Currently, about 40 percent of energy is generated from thermal power plants, but the new LCDP states that from 2026 onward, it will be reduced to 10 percent. It is also committed to increase electricity access to 78 percent by 2025 toward universal access by 2030. But, given the tight fiscal space and the need for significant investments to expand access, it is critical to leverage private financing to reach these objectives; however at current pace, 2025 seems a very ambitious target. 4. The proposed project responds to these challenges by leveraging private financing to develop the excellent energy resources in the country and develop new business models for mini grids. The YELEN SIRA project will bring new capacity, help reduce the cost of generation, and provide electricity access to some of the poorest and most vulnerable rural communities through grid densification, extension, mini-grids, and other off-grid solutions. The Component 1 would finance grid rehabilitation, upgrading, and extension to connect areas/localities around the interconnected grid (that is, transmission lines and substations in the regional cities) and enable a sustainable increase of the penetration of VRE into the grid and the deployment of a solar park with battery storage. The Component 2 would finance green mini-grids and off-grid electrification of households, productive uses, and health facilities. The Component 3 would target capacity building, including dedicated support to mobilize private capital and finance the solar park in a competitive manner. 5. The YELEN SIRA project follows the MFD approach, and public investments for the solar park with battery are needed because these projects are considered high risk by IPPs, and the private sector has been struggling to find a business model for mini grids. This resulted in low private sector 92 The World Bank Mali Electricity System Reinforcement and Access Expansion Project (Yelen Sira) (P176633) participation (or delayed in the case of solar projects that take years before being negotiated and lead to high tariffs). Owing to the ability to mobilize and blend climate financing with IDA and its experiences in other countries in developing renewable energy projects, including in West Africa, the World Bank brings a value added to efficiently support the design and implementation of these investments in Mali. The YELEN SIRA project presents a unique opportunity to overcome these challenges and unlock private sector financing. Public financing will be strategic and specifically targeted. For example, for the solar parks, private sector investments would cover capital expenditure (CAPEX) and the installation of the solar PV, and public financing would only be used for the grid upgrades required for VRE integration, dispatching infrastructures, and transaction advisory support. Utility’s Operational and Financial Performance and Project Impact 6. For more than 10 years, EDM-SA has been facing a structural financial deficit and as a result has depended on operational subsidies from the Government, as well as support from investments and debt restructuring. Furthermore, the structural deficit has also been increasing in view of the sustained demand growth, lack of investments, and hence an increasing reliance on thermal generation. Specifically, demand has been increasing in the 8 to 10 percent per year range and driven primarily by new connections, mostly residential. Mali went from one of the lowest rates of electrification to being close to the mean for Sub-Saharan Africa. However, this growth has also resulted in a significant increase in generation costs. In spite of widening tariff revenue shortfall, Malian authorities have been reluctant to adjust electricity tariffs, which remained largely unchanged since 2014. Instead, they have been providing operating subsidies to EDM-SA but not at sufficient levels to fully cover the increasing tariff deficit. This has resulted in EDM-SA accumulating short-term debt (either with local commercial banks or with suppliers of fuel and independent generators). The reliance on short-term financing and dependency on budget support has not been conducive to improved utility management. EDM-SA suffers from significant operating inefficiencies including combined technical and non-technical losses of about 23 percent in 2021. 7. EDM’s tariff deficit has over the years gone up and down depending on petroleum prices and the generation mix but the long-term trend is upwards due to the increase in volume. The tariff deficit reached a maximum in 2019 when it represented more than 60 percent of electricity sales. It decreased slightly in 2020 as a result of lower fuel prices on the international markets and measures implemented as part of the national DPF but increased again in 2021 and reached a record level in 2022 with the rebound in petroleum prices. EDM-SA has received an operating subsidy every year for more than 10 years, the amount has varied between CFA 30 and 50 billion (~US$50 to 80 million). However, during this period, the gap between electricity sales and costs (in particular generation) has increased driven by volumes as well as reduction in the share of hydroelectricity in the generation mix. For 2022, EDM-SA estimates that an additional CFA 90 billion would be necessary in addition 30 billion already received. The recovery plan for the sector foresees a reduction of the tariff deficit to less than 5 percent of sales in the next five years, primarily owing to a reduction of generation costs, provided the generation mix shifts toward lower-cost imports and lower-cost renewable generation sources. While this strategy appears sound, delays in the commissioning of new renewable generation and transmission lines for imports will at the very least delay the timeline by which such a reduction in the tariff deficit can be expected. The proposed project investments are expected to marginally impact EDM’s operating annual cash flow. Economic Analysis Methodology: Cost-Benefit Analysis 8. The economic analysis follows a standard cost-benefit analysis methodology. The economic viability of project investments is assessed by determining the EIRR and NPV over its lifetime, which can be different for the different subcomponents of the project (20 to 40 years). The GHG mitigation benefits and the social cost of carbon are also part of the economic analysis, following the World Bank GHG Accounting Guidelines. It is important to note that only project activities that generate direct, quantifiable benefits have been considered. Indirect benefits stemming from electricity access, such as new income- generating activities, access to basic services (such as education and health), improved air quality, and increased income would be too complex and uncertain to assess and quantify. Similarly, the benefits of Component 3 (Technical Assistance) are excluded from this analysis due to the difficulty of attaching an economic value to its outcomes. The results of this economic analysis are therefore expected to be conservative, particularly for rural electrification, which can have a transformative impact on the beneficiary localities. Economic Benefits of the Project 9. The upgrading and expansion of distribution networks around the capital city of Bamako, the upgrading and expansion of distribution networks around secondary cities, the development of green mini-grids and the off-grid electrification in rural areas, and the public infrastructures for the solar park will bring quantifiable and non-quantifiable economic benefits. The economic analysis of the project 94 The World Bank Mali Electricity System Reinforcement and Access Expansion Project (Yelen Sira) (P176633) investments has been carried out by comparing the economic costs and benefits of ‘with project’ and ‘without project’ scenarios. Under this methodology, the main benefit of the investments in network upgrading and extension as well as mini grids is the expansion of access to electricity services for households, businesses, and social services. Additional benefits come from the replacement of costly sources, such as diesel and HFO, by solar power. On the cost side, the ‘with project’ scenario considers all the incremental investments and operating costs associated with the expansion of electricity services. The methodological approach for analyzing the PV generation park (1.2) is based on avoided costs. It is assumed in the analysis, given the existing generation characterized by a significant share of thermal generation, that incremental solar generation will substitute part of the existing or new thermal generation that would need to be commissioned under the ‘without project’ scenario, assuming a mix of diesel and HFO. Under this approach, the benefits considered are the avoided variable fuel costs of thermal generation (not including potential savings on investments and O&M in thermal generation). The analysis does not quantify other possible benefits from the investments (improved reliability of the power system and reduced technical and commercial losses). In addition, the analysis separately quantifies the economic and GHG emission savings from avoided fossil fuel power production. 10. The economic analysis shows that the project is economically viable even without any consideration of environmental externalities. Using a social discount rate of 6 percent, the baseline NPV of the project is US$209 million, corresponding to an EIRR of 15.7 percent. 11. The project is also expected to bring benefits from reduced GHG emissions and local pollution. Specifically, taking into account avoided GHG emissions, in line with the World Bank’s GHG Accounting Methodology for Energy Access Investment Operations, the NPV of the project increases to between US$340 million and US$471 million, corresponding to an EIRR of between 21.5 percent and 27.4 percent. Enhancing access to electricity by FHHs. Providing households with access to electricity and improved energy services has the potential to promote gender equality, create employment and business opportunities for women, and improve development outcomes. For example, women can get better access to education and the burden of doing household chores manually can decrease. Evidence shows that these benefits are often realized only if approaches specifically target the needs and realities of both men and women and are integrated in the design and implementation of project interventions. Data collected from other projects in Mali show that access to electricity by FHHs is lower than in MHHs. A preliminary assessment suggests that lower income affects both connection and payment for electricity and lower education negatively affects FHH connections and use of electricity. All project studies and interventions will aim to collect data regarding barriers faced by women and men and will be used to design appropriate measures, such as awareness campaigns and pro-poor measures, to alleviate the barriers. Therefore, the project will integrate a specific effort for ensuring connections by FHHs. Right now, the percentage of FHHs in Mali is 17 percent, so this should be reflected in the project as a target for electrification rates by FHHs—the baseline for the specific target zones will be better assessed in the infrastructure detail design studies, but it is estimated at 0. Given that FHHs represent 17 percent, new connections should aim at this to ensure equality between FHHs and MHHs. To do so, the focus will be on mechanisms to ensure that FHHs benefit from electrification and that these mechanisms are embedded in the technical design of activities under grid extension (Component 1), mini-grid installation (Subcomponent 2.1), and SHS deployment (Subcomponent 2.2). Though the mechanisms for this will be further discussed, these might comprise the inclusion of quotas and/or other recommendations from further studies to be developed under the project, namely those focusing on affordability and electricity literacy. Due to the current electricity tariff, it is not expected that households will start using electricity for cooking. The project will include data collection on households’ energy for cooking patterns and preferences to better understand how to integrate measures to support the transition from traditional fuels to more efficient and/or modern energy in future projects. 11. Given the geographic location of this project, consideration of the climate change and natural hazard risk to infrastructure design and overall operation will be important. Disruptions in the power sector from natural events are already costing 0.77 percent of the GDP of Mali annually.107 West Africa is one of the world’s most vulnerable regions to climate variability and change, warming at a greater rate than the global annual mean, with more severe droughts and with a greater number of extremely dry and wet years in the Sahel region throughout this century. Climate impacts in the region will exacerbate current vulnerabilities and will affect livelihoods, food security, and economic stability. 2. As a Sahelian country, Mali sees vulnerabilities to climate change. While already one of the hottest countries in the word, Mali will also suffer from increased temperature (the annual mean temperature is expected to rise by about 2°C by mid-century).108 This does not account for changes in extreme values throughout the year, which may range as high as 4°C or more in the 2040 decade along the Sahel regions.109 Changes in precipitation are harder to estimate, with multi-model ensembles of the Sahel region predicting a drying trend but with enough uncertainty to limit future projections. 3. Mali has a high risk of river flood, urban flood, water scarcity, extreme heat, and wildfire.110 It is worth noting that not all specific siting locations have been decided upon, but the information in this annex may help inform siting and design choices to increase the resilience of the project. This annex briefly summarizes Mali’s experience with mini-grids and off-grid solutions and lessons learned. To date, four generations of mini networks have been implemented in Mali, as discussed in the following paragraphs. 2. Between 2005 and 2010. As part of the implementation of the Domestic Energy and Access to Basic Services in Rural Areas Project (Projet Energie Domestique et Accès aux Services de Base en Milieu Rural, PEDASB), AMADER implemented 255 rural mini grids running exclusively on diesel generators, built and operated by some 60 operators. This was done through PCASER, partially financed by Malian investors who benefited from an initial investment subsidy of between 70 and 80 percent. Today, many of these power plants are at a standstill, mainly due to the high cost of kilowatt-hours (CFAF 250 on average), which is twice as expensive as the price charged by EDM-SA. Other factors also explain this situation, including the failure to strictly apply operating instructions for the equipment, which has led to its deterioration, the random collection of invoices, and the weak technical, financial and managerial capacities of the operators, as well as the multitude of operators with different levels of competence and commitment. Thermal diesel technologies, which largely dominated the achievements of AMADER, is the cause of the multiple operating difficulties experienced by operators, including the price of diesel fuel that kept increasing. The cost of the kilowatt-hour thus increased, and the rates reached a level that was too high for the rural population. This situation has greatly compromised the profitability of the operators, most of whom have stopped operating while waiting for a renewable source of production, such as solar energy. This experience has led Mali and its development partners to reconsider the technology of isolated mini-grids by using renewable energies, particularly solar PV. 3. From 2010 to 2021. The promotion of hybrid diesel-PV systems, through the Russian and Dutch trust funds as well as the KfW118 grant, accelerated their development in some 20 localities, using hybrid PV/diesel technology with convincing results in reducing generation costs for improved and sustainable access to electricity in rural communities. 4. As renewable energy production technologies became more affordable and efficient, solar or hybrid mini-grids represented a viable alternative for access to electricity. Thus, the World Bank, based on lessons learned from PEDASB, has financed the hybridization (diesel/solar) of 50 existing mini-grids through the Hybrid Systems for Rural Electrification Project, under the same PPP as the PEDASB, with a slightly different business model. Operators only invested 5 percent of the investment as a complementary counterpart and the work was carried out following bids by qualified companies. This new option led to a reduction of the price per kilowatt- hour by 30 percent (from CFAF 180 to 150) and to an increase in service time from 6–8 hours to 18 hours or even 20 hours in some localities. However, this second generation of mini-grids faced many challenges. These include: the failure of rural electrification operators to collect operating subsidies granted to EDM-SA, and the difficulty of benefiting from fuel exemption. 5. Finally, a diagnosis of the current model of rural electrification in Mali identified the lack of private capital as a major issue in most of the rural electrification structures. Many of these are owned by concessionaires who mobilize between 5 and 25 percent of their own resources. The trend to concentrate operators around a limited number of larger and financially stronger operators seemed to be a better approach. Under such an approach, the selection of villages and feasibility studies will be carried out by AMADER.
