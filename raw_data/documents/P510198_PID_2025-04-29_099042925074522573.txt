Introduction and Context 1. The Lao PDR Pollution and Waste Management Project (PWMP) is a US$37.85 million Investment Project Financing operation that aims to enhance the country's capacity to manage waste, plastics, and pollution and improve municipal solid waste (MSW) services in Vientiane Capital (VC). The six-year project also looks to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and generate jobs for vulnerable communities as a result of these interventions. The project supports the Government of Lao PDR’s (GoL) commitment to a low-carbon, resilient, and cleaner development pathway, the country’s medium-term Green Growth Strategy to 2030, the 9th National Socioeconomic Development Plan 2021-2025, and its Nationally Determined Contributions (2021), which include a commitment to reduce GHG emissions and enhance climate resilience. The focus on VC, the country’s capital and largest waste generator, ensures that the project's outcomes serve as a model for other cities, thereby amplifying its impact across the country. 2. The PWMP will be the second among the national level projects in the World Bank-financed Southeast Asia Regional Program on Combating Marine Plastics (SEA-MaP) (P175659) Series of Projects (SOP) to support marine plastic solutions Page 2 The World Bank Lao PDR Pollution and Waste Management Project (P510198) at the national level. Marine plastic pollution is a transboundary issue, with shared river systems such as the Mekong River acting as conduits for plastic waste, and shared coastlines receiving plastic waste carried by ocean currents. Plastic pollution from Laos is transported via the Mekong River to the marine environment. Accordingly, single country solutions to manage marine litter carry the risk of not being sufficient; there is a need for regional collaboration to combat marine plastic pollution in critical countries with currently undermanaged plastic waste. The development objective of the SEA- MaP is to reduce plastic consumption, increase recycling, and minimize leakages to prevent land-and sea based marine plastic pollution in Southeast Asia. The program follows a SOP approach under two pillars: Pillar 1 includes a US$20 million regional IDA-financed project that will help strengthen institutional capacity, harmonize standards and guidelines, and promote innovative financing and technological solutions. Pillar 2 focuses on plastic -related investments and policy support at national levels. The regional IDA-financed SEA-MAP project under Pillar 1 was approved in June 2022. In addition to the PWMP, Pillar 2 of the SOP includes the US$67 million Cambodia Solid Waste and Plastic Management Improvement Project (P170976) which was approved in May 2023.1 Implementation of the SEA-MaP is progressing steadily and is on track to achieve its objectives of strengthening plastic policies and promoting innovative solutions. An objective of the Cambodia project is to improve plastic management in selected areas. It is currently procuring key consultancy packages, including for the development of a regulatory framework to better manage waste and plastics, and site selection for waste and plastic recovery facilities and landfills. Laos will draw from the IDA Regional Window to support reduction of plastics, along with ASEAN and Cambodia. The common results indicator for Pillar 2 projects is ‘Number of plastics policies, guidelines, or standards established and aligned with the Regional Action Plan’. This indicator appears in PWMP’s results framework. Country Context 3. The PWMP is being undertaken within a challenging macroeconomic environment. Economic growth in Lao PDR averaged about 7 percent over the two decades leading up to 2019, driven by capital-intensive, resource-based, and debt- financed patterns.2 High public debt levels have contributed to ongoing macroeconomic instability, impacting development prospects; the World Bank assessed under the Low-Income Countries Debt Sustainability Framework that Lao PDR is currently experiencing debt distress with an unsustainable outlook.3 The country faces both solvency and liquidity challenges due to substantial financing needs, limited financing options, low foreign exchange reserves, and notable depreciation pressures. Consumer price inflation accelerated in 2023, primarily due to the weaker Lao Kip (LAK), with average annual consumer price inflation reaching 31 percent, compared to 23 percent in 2022. Higher prices for food, transport, restaurants, and hotels were significant contributors. Despite improved revenue administration and tighter monetary policy in 2024, pressures on the exchange rate and prices are expected to persist. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Between 2000 and 2015, the country’s major cities saw a 40 to 60 percent increase in waste generation, with MSW generation reaching approximately 0.75 kg per capita per day and a projected 54 percent increase in annual waste generation across Laos between 2020 and 2035 if actions are not taken. In VC alone, 1,065 tons of waste are generated per day – making it the highest rate in the country. Plastic comprises about 24 percent of MSW mainly from food and drink packaging and plastic bags.6 Toxic smoke generated from open burning of waste, including plastic, contributes to GHG emissions, air pollution and respiratory issues. Waste in Laos’ rivers and urban drainage systems impedes drainage and exacerbates flooding, affects fishing activity, and spreads plastic waste to marine areas via the Mekong River, damaging local and downstream ecosystems. Popular tourist destinations, face reputational risks and decreased attractiveness, due to widespread plastics pollution with consequent revenue impacts. 5. The GoL has established legal instruments for environmental risk management to address air and water pollution. These include a 2024 updated Environmental Protection Law (EPL) that emphasizes Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) as the main tool for pollution control, and a Prime Minister’s Decree on Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA, revised in 2022). As a result, and with World Bank support,7 the number of ESIAs approved by the ministry in charge, namely the Ministry of Natural Resources and Environment (MONRE), increased significantly between 2012 and 2020. 6. With hydropower, mining, agriculture, and industry being the main pollution contributors, the GoL has been prioritizing its regulation enforcement in these sectors. Agricultural biomass burning and industries such as cement factories have been identified as some of the main sources of PM10 and PM2.5. Water pollution has been linked to mining effluent and agricultural runoff.8 9 Investment projects in these sectors are categorized as Category 2 projects that require an ESIA to be approved. Category 2 project owners are required to prepare and adhere to Environmental Management and Monitoring Plans. They must submit annual compliance reports to MONRE and undergo periodic compliance monitoring by MONRE staff. Air quality monitoring (AQM) and water quality monitoring (WQM) are carried out by the Natural Resources and Environment Research Institute (NRERI), which operates ten AQM monitoring stations and 21 WQM stations across Laos – both with the support of the World Bank.10 7. Yet, there are gaps that must be bridged to ensure effective pollution control for these sectors. Regulation compliance monitoring and enforcement systems lack standard monitoring procedures, staff capacity, and resources, as well as a fines system for noncompliance. Further, data communicated by the AQM systems are insufficiently robust11 and the stations are not integrated to allow effective environmental compliance monitoring, including emission monitoring. The WQM system lacks guidelines and institutional capacities, and data is not consistently reported. 6 World Bank. 2021. Institutional arrangements for waste management include the MONRE with responsibility for national legal and regulatory oversight of waste management policies, and the Ministry of Public Works and Transport (MPWT) with responsibility for constructing waste management infrastructure. Provincial Departments of Public Works and Transport and urban development authorities have responsibility for waste management at city and provincial levels including operating and maintaining waste management infrastructure. 9. VC’s Solid Waste Management Strategy and Action Plan (2021-2030) targets 100 percent solid waste collection, maximizing material recovery, and financial and operational efficiency. The Vientiane City Office for Management and Service (VCOMS) is the urban development authority for VC, and oversees waste management services, with ten private operators contracted to collect and transport waste from households and businesses across the city. About 31 percent of MSW generators have contracts for waste collection.12 Waste is transported by the private companies to a 50-hectare landfill in Xaythany district of VC called the KM32 landfill. VCOMS collects waste from some areas in Vientiane and this waste is transported to the KM16 transfer station in Xaysettha district where it is compacted before it is sent to the KM32 landfill. KM32 landfill receives about 44 percent of MSW generated in VC and is managed by a private operator contracted by VCOMS. The Vientiane government also has in place a concessional agreement with a private operator adjacent to the landfill to provide Refuse Derived Fuel production services with the residual waste disposed of at the landfill for a tipping fee. VCOM’s main revenue streams include fees from waste contracts, tipping fees, and a small annual subsidy that is provided by the Vientiane government, albeit not consistently. Approximately 264 seasonal and permanent waste pickers from nearby villages operate at the KM32 landfill, 47 percent of whom are female, generating income from selling recyclable waste to a waste management community center at the KM32 landfill.13 10. However, the sector and VC specifically face legislative, institutional, and infrastructure gaps that prevent them from meeting demand. Nation-wide, the sector is faced with: (i) absence of comprehensive legislation and clear institutional roles at the centralized and decentralized level for agencies such as MONRE and the MPWT, resulting in their ineffective governance, operational, financial, and technical performance; (ii) lack of a national waste collection data system, which hampers informed policymaking and planning; and (iii) waste infrastructure development that generally lacks integrated planning, recurrent budget allocations for operation and maintenance, and comprehensive training for infrastructure operators. A major gap is the lack of understanding of the total costs of the waste management system. In VC, VCOMS is underperforming because of insufficient budgets and limited revenue generation, inadequate technical and management capacities, and lack of performance incentives. MSW collection is poorly planned and coordinated, resulting in 14-49 percent coverage14 and public grievances. VCOMS struggles to hold the private waste collectors accountable as waste contracts do not have performance indicators. Private operators struggle to collect fees due to households’ unwillingness to pay and lack of enforcement framework, and the fee is too low (LAK50,000 per month, about US$2.25 - less than 1 percent of VC’s disposable income levels) to meet cost recovery needs. The waste treatment facilities are poorly managed: in KM32 landfill, for example, waste compacting and soil covering are not routinely practiced, and there are no leachate collection and treatment protocols in place. The landfill also lacks critical infrastructure, such as a leachate collection and transport system and a suitable leachate treatment facility. Consequently, leachate is discharged into the surrounding area, impacting ground and surface water quality. The landfill also lacks a methane gas capturing system and a passive gas removal mechanism, further causing environmental hazards. Consequently, waste pickers operate in high- risk conditions and face health risks. The GoL, led by MONRE, has in place a National Plastics Action Plan (NPAP) for 2024-2030 focusing on single-use plastic reduction, enhanced recyclables collection, and recycling sector improvement . The GoL has also implemented waste banks in a limited number of schools and government offices in VC for plastic waste collection. Small-scale efforts have been undertaken by the GoL to reduce the use of plastic bags along with plastic reduction campaigns. Domestic recycling is carried out mainly by private companies that process plastics into pellets for export, mostly to Thailand and Viet Nam. 12. However, key challenges facing the government in addressing plastic waste include lack of data and information for plastic waste monitoring, low technical capacity for implementing plastic recycling interventions, and lack of financial resources for implementing programmatic plastic waste reduction activities . Further, domestic recycling lacks compliance regulations and monitoring of plastics collection and recycling by the private sector, raising environmental and health concerns. These issues will become more pressing with impending plastic import bans by Thailand and Viet Nam. 13. The PWMP aims to resolve these challenges by improving the efficiency, effectiveness, and financial sustainability of Laos’ pollution, waste, and plastics management systems, scaling up the infrastructure and capacity of the VC government for MSW service delivery, and improving the working conditions and livelihoods of formal and informal waste workers (IWW). These will generate important regional outcomes as the amount of pollution flowing into shared water bodies will decrease significantly. By doing so, it will support the GoL in implementing the EPL and EIA Decree for air and water pollution control, VC’s Solid Waste Management Strategy and Action Plan, and the NPAP.15 Importantly, the project will also support GoL’s collaboration with other ASEAN Member States on combating marine debris and their adoption of the Bangkok Declaration on Combating Marine Debris in the ASEAN Region (2019). The PDO reflects two sets of challenges faced by Laos that the project will address relating to pollution management, and solid waste and plastics management. 16. To address pollution management challenges, the project will support the development and early implementation of a compliance monitoring system that will be used to help the government better enforce pollution control regulations among waste management facilities and other high-pollution risk enterprises. The project will also upgrade existing air and water quality stations, to increase the accuracy and reliability of air and water quality information that is made available to the public and used in government decision-making. 17. The project will address waste management challenges through several interventions starting with development of a Solid Waste Management (SWM) Decree to strengthen the legal framework for waste management in Laos. These reforms are critical for the sustainability of waste collection and treatment infrastructure and equipment financed by the project. Through PWMP, the government will also invest in low-cost expansion of SW collection equipment, waste transfer and treatment facilities, including upgrading the KM32 landfill, and provide vocational and occupational health and safety (OHS) training, facilities, and protective equipment to waste workers. This will result in an increased amount of waste collected and safely treated, and more persons with access with waste collection services. 18. The longer-term outcomes of the project are expected to be national and regional in nature, namely low-carbon, resilient and cleaner development pathway for Laos, and increased alignment of Laos’ policies, guidelines, and standards with the ASEAN Regional Action Plan. The project will directly benefit around 645,000 VC inhabitants who will have access to enhanced MSW service delivery, as well as 264 waste pickers at the KM32 landfill who will benefit from improved working conditions, training, and employment opportunities at the planned waste management facilities. Among them, 106 female waste pickers will be provided with skills to integrate them into formal waste management systems and alternative livelihoods. Indirect beneficiaries are the estimated 7.5 million inhabitants of Laos who will benefit from improved waste, plastics, and pollution management measures that together will improve their quality of life and environment. Moreover, the project is expected to generate regional and global benefits by reducing plastic waste entering shared water bodies via the Mekong River and decreasing GHG emissions. 19. The project will support the development and deployment of a compliance monitoring system for air and water pollution control enforcement to help the government better monitor and enforce environmental protection measures, especially for Category 2 investment projects.16 The system will comprise procedures for monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and reporting of compliance with environmental regulations, a compliance scoring system to track and report performance of Category 2 projects’ environmental obligations, and a system for monitoring fines and penalties. It is anticipated that the project will bring net positive benefits to the environment and people. However, the project is not without environmental and social risks, with nine of the ten Environmental and Social Standards (ESSs) considered relevant. Five ESF instruments have been prepared for the project and were disclosed on the Recipient’s websites on December 17, 2024: Preliminary Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (Pre-ESIA); Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF); Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP); Social Impact Assessment-Social Management Plan (SIA-SMP); and Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP). 45. The environmental risk of the project is High due to activities such as the rehabilitation of the KM32 landfill, upgrading of the KM16 transfer station, and construction of a new transfer station in Naxaythong District. The key risks involve groundwater pollution from leachate, surface water contamination, air pollution from open burning and landfill fires, and health and safety hazards from waste traffic. These risks are long-term, high in magnitude, and exacerbated by environmental (for example, flooding, extreme heat) and institutional (for example, limited capacity) challenges. Additional risks include cumulative impacts on water resources and agriculture from legacy pollution at KM32 and adjacent private facilities. Construction-related impacts, such as air and noise pollution and health risks, are generally moderate, temporary, and localized. Improper siting of the Naxaythong facility could affect biodiversity and cultural heritage, though exclusion criteria have been developed to avoid such impacts. The KM32 landfill currently operates as an open dump without lining and limited capping, experiencing uncontrolled leachate discharge, waste burning and occasional landfill fires, and GHG (methane) emission. Infectious medical waste from health care facilities is also found at the dumpsite. In addition, there are legacy waste dumps and four wastewater ponds in the landfill. A Pre-ESIA conducted during project preparation identified historic and ongoing infiltration of polluted leachate from the legacy waste on both VCOMS site and the adjacent private concession which, if not mitigated, could potentially continue to pose significant long-term risks to the surface water and groundwater resources, which are used for irrigation and/or domestic consumption and pose a risk to the health of nearby residents, workers, and waste pickers. Environmental risks and impacts from TA and activities under Component 1 may indirectly increase waste volumes due to stricter enforcement. Minor civil works for plastic waste storage could result in minor and temporary impacts, such as air and noise pollution and waste generation. 46. Environmental risk management approach. Prior to appraisal, the government prepared, organized public consultations on, and disclosed an ESMF and Pre-ESIA. The ESMF assesses overall environmental and social risks of the Project and guides site-specific measures and E&S risk management for TA activities. The Pre-ESIA for KM32 identifies preferred design options to mitigate risks such as leachate and air pollution. This will be further developed into a full ESIA, Health Impact Assessment, and corresponding Environmental and Social Management Plan (ESMP)/s for the waste management infrastructure/works to be conducted in Vientiane Capital during implementation. The ESMP/s will be included in bidding document and contracts which will require Contractors to prepare and implement Contractor ESMP/s, and the project will develop a POM. In addition, the project will support establishment of an Environmental and Social Monitoring Coordination Committee (ESMCC) mandated to oversee environmental and social aspects around the KM32 landfill and adjacent Vientiane Waste Management Company concession area and at the KM16 and Naxaythong transfer Page 14 The World Bank Lao PDR Pollution and Waste Management Project (P510198) stations. Terms of reference for the ESMCC are included in the POM. Consultations with waste pickers and local communities have informed the project design, and further consultations will be held for the full ESIA post-effectiveness. 47. The social risk is High but accompanied by positive outcomes, such as improved waste collection and working conditions for waste pickers. Key social risks include potential land acquisition and resettlement for construction of the new transfer station at Naxaythong where local communities includeethnic minorities as well as possible community concerns about new/expanded waste facilities. At the waste management facilities receiving project investments, there are occupational health and safety risks for formal and informal waste workers as well as sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA), sexual harassment (SH) and child protection risks. The project design safeguards waste pickers’ livelihoods by improving working conditions through personal protective equipment, training, and safety measures, while providing opportunities for formal employment and skill training. Instruments such as SIA-SMP, SEP, and ESCP address these risks. 48. The Implementing Agencies have experience with the World Bank safeguard policies but limited familiarity with the ESF, and capacity for managing social aspects related to SWM is low. The client will be challenged in: (i) coordinating among different institutional levels (central, province, and district level) within the same ministries and across ministries; and (ii) managing and mitigating livelihood impacts to informal waste pickers, many of whom are socially marginalized and vulnerable. Specific institutional capacity strengthening/ building measures such as the provision of additional resources and training needs have been identified and presented in the ESF instruments, including the ESCP to ensure ownership and sustainability of the resources. 49.
