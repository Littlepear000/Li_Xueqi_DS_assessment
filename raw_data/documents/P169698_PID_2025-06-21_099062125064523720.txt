Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Georgia’s economy has demonstrated strong performance in recent years, with GDP growing by 9.4% in 2024 and 8.8% in early 2025, driven by ICT, transport, finance, and a tourism rebound. Growth is expected to slow to 5.5% in 2025 and stabilize at 5% due to trade tensions and regional uncertainty. Inflation reached 3.5% in May, slightly above target, but core inflation and the policy rate remain stable. Fiscal management has been strong, with a deficit of 2.5% and public debt falling to pre-pandemic levels. Poverty dropped to 9.4%, though unemployment rose to 14.7%, highlighting labor market challenges. FDI declined for a second year, and while reserves improved slightly, they remain suboptimal. A flexible exchange rate and continued reserve accumulation will be key to strengthening external resilience. Page 2 The World Bank Log-In Georgia (P169698) – Additional Financing Request 1 2. While inequality has declined, geographic and demographic gaps persist. The economy is structurally transforming from low productivity sectors to services and industries. The impacts of such a transformation can be unequally distributed and diminish over time. With a greater concentration of people and businesses in the largest cities, regional disparities have grown in output per capita. Internally displaced people, ethnic minorities, persons with disabilities, and women remain more prone to poverty, compared to other groups. Despite progress toward closing gender gaps in recent years, persistent gender inequality in economic opportunities, agency, and endowments remain. 3. Digitalization is a contributor to resilient and inclusive growth and can support Georgia address economic dualism. Georgia is vulnerable to climate change and natural disasters. Risks include adverse effects of climate change (i.e., increasing frequency and severity of droughts, floods, frosts, and extreme temperature events) on sectors such as agriculture, energy, and transport as well as in access to essential public services in the aftermath of a natural disaster. These effects could be more severe in rural and remote mountainous regions. Investments in digital infrastructure enable people and enterprises to stay connected and operational in the event of natural disasters and other external shocks as well as support relief and recovery efforts, boosting societal resilience. Improved connectivity can also enable remote learning and healthcare, digital public service delivery, and employment opportunities for populations in rural areas, promoting more inclusive growth. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Digital connectivity is the backbone of modern economies, driving both inclusive development and sustained economic growth. As such, Georgia is strategically investing in digitalization efforts, including digitalization of the Middle Corridor, and aims to position itself as a regional connectivity (physical, digital, and energy) hub given its geostrategic role in bridging Europe and Asia1. To realize these ambitions, a robust and dense fiber-optic infrastructure is essential. Recent World Bank initiatives, such as a study on the Middle Corridor2 and the ongoing Enhancing Energy Security through Power Interconnection and Renewable Energy (ESPIRE) Program (P179950)3 in Georgia, underscore digital infrastructure's critical role in enhancing regional trade, energy flows, and digital services. 5. Access to high-quality broadband networks is limited in rural areas, contributing to economic dualism and could worsen other social divides, underpinning the rationale for the proposed Additional Financing (AF or ‘the Project’). Fixed broadband networks using modern technologies like optic fiber cables are less common outside cities. And even though mobile broadband networks cover most of the population, the quality of service varies, with rural areas often not well- served in terms of connection speeds and reliability. A key challenge to delivering broadband services in rural areas to the same level of quality as urban centers is the lack of fiber-optic based ‘middle mile’ infrastructure connecting rural settlements. These gaps imply differences in the ability of people and enterprises in rural areas to access the full range of digital opportunities. 6. These strategies recognize the importance of digital infrastructure and universal access to broadband to support economic development while also advancing digital inclusion and fostering the skills and demand necessary for meaningful internet use, particularly in rural areas. Component 1 (increasing access to high-speed broadband) of the Parent Project finances the State Program for Broadband Infrastructure Development in Georgia (called Open Net)6 as well as implementation of key policy, legal, and regulatory reforms for broadband market development, under the oversight of the Ministry of Economy and Sustainable Development (MOESD) and the sector regulator, Communications Commission (ComCom). Component 2 (promoting the use of broadband enabled services) supports demand stimulation for broadband services through digital skills trainings (called the Digital Adoption and Inclusion Program (DAIP)) in connected rural settlements with a particular focus on the digital inclusion of vulnerable populations. Component 3 (project implementation support) finances routine project administration and implementation activities. The first Component is co-financed with the European Investment Bank, and Counterpart Financing is available to augment all three components of the Parent Project – see Table 1 for details. 7. The Parent Project’s implementation progress demonstrates notable results in connected rural communities. Internet Service Providers (ISPs), serving over 70,000 people, are already connected to the Parent Project financed fiber- optic backbone network in rural areas. The ISP servicing school broadband connectivity is a major user of the network and has already provided fiber optic based broadband connectivity to over 100 schools. Approximately 90 more schools are in the geographical area where network construction is expected to be completed within the calendar year. The Project has also stimulated local economic activity, as seen through the establishment of the first fiber optic-based rural ISP (Racha Net) in one region following completion of the backbone network there. The DAIP has trained over 3,500 people across 200 settlements on digital literacy and online safety, responding to training needs shared by the connected communities through needs assessment surveys conducted early in the Parent Project’s implementation.7 Furthermore, DAIP has adopted a train- the-trainer approach in its implementation, utilizing local talent in the connected municipalities to deliver trainings and contribute to beneficiary mobilization. Other digital literacy projects like the EU-funded Rural Development through Digital Inclusion program continue to use these trainers' experience and community engagement for follow-up activities, ensuring continued support to local populations – a positive outcome of the DAIP. The AF is a proposed US$40 million equivalent IBRD loan, the majority of which is focused on supplementing funding for Component 1 (increasing access to broadband) of the Parent Project, and extension of the Parent Project’s closing date by 24 months to December 31, 2027. The AF component structure remains unchanged from the Parent Project and is designed to allocate its funding to finance a cost overrun in deployment of fiber-optic backbone infrastructure (Subcomponent 1.1), allowing completion of the network as planned under the Parent Project. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 13. Implementation arrangements remain unchanged. As with the Parent Project, the AF will be implemented by Open Net NNLE, as the Project implementing entity (PIE), under MOESD's oversight. Open Net NNLE was established by Government Decree Number 375 to implement the State Program for Broadband Infrastructure Development. Some parts of the Project will be jointly implemented by the PIE, MOESD and the Communications Commission (ComCom). The MOESD's Deputy Minister (responsible for the Information and Communications Technologies (ICT) sector) will play a strategic role in ensuring Project activities are implemented in a timely manner and supporting the overall achievement of Project's outcomes. The Deputy Minister will also continue to chair the Project Coordination Group (PCG) that will coordinate Project implementation across the various implementing agencies involved in this process. 14. Ensuring strong collaboration and transparency across relevant government authorities remains critical for the Project's successful implementation. These arrangements will be governed by guidelines and procedures set out in a Project Operations Manual (POM) and applied through a Subsidiary Agreement between the Ministry of Finance (MOF), MOESD, and Open Net NNLE.8 The POM of the Parent Project will be updated by the PIE and will reflect the Project's financial management, procurement, ESF management, monitoring and evaluation, and reporting arrangements. It will include details on the implementation and action plans, and approaches for Project activities. The Subsidiary Agreement will also detail the relationships, roles, and responsibilities of the various agencies involved in the Project. Importantly, the POM will specify the exact role of Open Net NNLE vis-à-vis activities jointly implemented with ComCom to avoid any confusion with respect to responsibility for each of the activities involved. 8 Since the AF does not include activities led by ComCom, the Subsidiary Agreement for the Project will only include MOF, MOESD, and Open Net NNLE.
