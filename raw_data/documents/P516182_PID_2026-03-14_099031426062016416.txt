Introduction and Context Country Context 1. The Republic of Maldives is a Small Island Developing State (SIDS), with a dispersed geography, that has seen remarkable economic progress powered by tourism, resulting in marked improvements in welfare and poverty reduction, despite persistent vulnerabilities. The country consists of an archipelago of 26 natural atolls scattered over an area covering around 90,000 square kilometers. Total land size is only 300 square kilometers, with 99 percent of territory consisting of ocean, and the population of about 550,000 is spread over 188 inhabited islands with about a third living in the capital island, Male. The spatial distribution of the population raises transport costs and makes the provision of basic infrastructure and public services costly. Poverty is spatially concentrated, with 93 percent of the poor living in atolls, where households rely more heavily on public services and subsidies but face more limited access to infrastructure and markets (Source: Maldives Bureau of Statistics). Although unemployment is relatively low, vulnerable population groups face labor market barriers (Source: Labor Force Survey 2025). For example, the gender gap in employment stood at 27.7 percentage points in 2025Q3. Maldives is almost fully dependent on imported fuel for critical services like electricity generation, water desalination as the country has no freshwater source, and inter‑island transportation. 2. Despite significant economic progress, Maldives faces significant macro-fiscal vulnerabilities. Real GDP per capita more than doubled in the past 30 years, from US$5,539 in 1995 to US$13,016 in 2024, and this remarkable growth has been accompanied by a sustained reduction in poverty. This progress was driven largely by high-end tourism, which accounted for 20.3 percent of GDP and 85 percent of foreign exchange revenues in 2024. Despite impressive growth, the country faces macro-fiscal vulnerabilities. Over time, fiscal and external imbalances have widened, driven by high public spending, an expanded role of state-owned enterprises (SOEs), and increased reliance on non-concessional external borrowing. These imbalances have led to elevated debt distress and mounting FX liquidity pressures. Total public and Page 2 Official Use Only The World Bank Maldives Fuel for Utilities and Essential Logistics Emergency Project (P516182) publicly guaranteed debt reached US$10.1 billion (about 129.2 percent of GDP) in 2025, and external financing pressures are significant with average external debt service obligations of around US$1 billion per year during 2026–27. The macro- fiscal vulnerabilities pose direct threats to poor and vulnerable households, as fiscal pressures could force spending cuts on programs that they disproportionately rely on. 3. In addition to these vulnerabilities, the country has high exposure to external shocks but limited buffers to respond. Typical of SIDS, Maldives faces substantial essential import needs, particularly fuel, raising exposure to external terms of trade shocks. Fuel imports alone averaged US$63.7 million per month in 2024-25 (or 10 percent of annual GDP) and are critical to essential service delivery. Additionally, the country is highly vulnerable to external shocks to international travel given the dependence on tourism. During the COVID-19 crisis, for example, GDP per capita contracted by 33 percent in 2020, and without large-scale government support, poverty could have more than doubled.1 The ability to respond to external shocks is constrained by limited buffers, with foreign exchange reserves estimated at about US$1.3 billion, equivalent to 2.6 months of import coverage as of February 2026. However, usable reserves, which refers to the level of reserves after pre-determined short-term drains are deducted from gross reserves, stand at less than 1 month of import cover (Source: Maldives Monetary Authority and WB staff calculations). Over the medium-term, significant structural and fiscal reforms and action to reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels are needed to address these vulnerabilities and strengthen resilience to shocks. Critical fiscal and structural reforms _For Official Use Only_ include replacing untargeted subsidies with targeted social assistance, State Owned Enterprise (SOE) reform, rationalizing of public capital expenditures and health financing reform. Technical assistance on each of these reform areas continues to be provided by the World Bank in coordination with other development partners under the Multilateral Development Bank (MDB+) platform as well as under the Maldives Competitiveness and Growth Project (MCGP, P179286) under implementation. In addition, it is crucial for Maldives to reduce long-term reliance on imported fossil fuels and increase the share of its energy from renewable sources. A. Project Strategic Context 4. The current conflict in the Middle East has driven up fuel costs and disrupted tourism, causing significant economic stress to the Maldives. Since February 28, 2026, the escalating conflict has contributed to a more than 38 percent increase in global oil prices and widespread travel disruptions resulting in a sharp decline in tourist flows through the Middle East, which historically accounted for 25-30 percent of total arrivals. This poses critical macro-risks and restricts availability of foreign exchange as the country is reliant on tourism for nearly 85 percent of its foreign exchange revenues and enters this crisis with very low foreign exchange reserves. Additionally, these shocks are expected to lead to slower growth, rising inflation, and a slight increase in the simulated poverty rate in 2026. As inflation rises, poorer households, which spend more than one-third of their budgets on food and essential goods2, will be disproportionately affected. 5. By impacting foreign exchange availability, the crisis also poses an immediate risk to the available of essential goods and functioning of essential services in the country. By threatening the ability to import fuel, the crisis indirectly impacts electricity generation across dispersed islands, the operation of desalination plants that supply nearly all potable water, inter-island maritime transport, aviation services, and the functioning of tourism. As Maldives has no domestic fossil fuel production and renewable energy penetration remains limited, the immediate demand for imported fuel cannot be substituted through other means. The government’s fiscal position has deteriorated simultaneously, which limits the ability to intervene. Tourism- linked revenues (Green Tax, Tourism Goods and Services Tax, resort lease income) represent more than 50 percent of total government revenue and the revenue shortfall from reduced arrivals has come at the same time as higher expenditures related to elevated costs for fuel and social protection commitments. This is likely to contribute to a widening of the fiscal deficit, already elevated at 9.9 percent and 3.2 percent of GDP in 2024 and 2025 respectively, with financing constrained by already elevated debt levels. Sectoral and Institutional Context 7. Though the country has increased the share of its energy from renewable sources in recent years, particularly, solar, electricity supplied through approximately 186 island powerhouses rely almost entirely on imported diesel fuel. Two state‑owned utilities serve most inhabited islands. The dispersed geography of the archipelago creates significant logistical complexity and cost, making the continuity of fuel supply critical for electricity generation, water desalination, health services, and inter‑island transport. The cost of electricity production varies significantly across islands, with _For Official Use Only_ generation costs for the most efficient generators estimated at between US¢19 and US¢33 per kWh, rising to as high as US¢69 per kWh for more remote outer islands — reflecting the logistical burden of fuel supply across a geographically dispersed archipelago. The World Bank has been supporting efforts to increase the share of renewable energy, through projects such as Accelerating Sustainable Private Investment in Renewable Energy (ASPIRE, P145482) and Accelerating Renewable Energy Integration and Sustainable Energy Project (ARISE, P172788), and is working towards scaling up support in this area in order to reduce the risks associated with fossil fuel dependence. 8. The State Trading Organization (STO), an entity primarily owned by the Maldives’ government, holds exclusive or quasi-exclusive mandates for the import and distribution of petroleum products.3 This is through its subsidiary for power generation fuel, and direct retail operations. STO also operates distribution networks across all 20 administrative atolls, serving as the critical supply chain backbone for an archipelago where private sector import logistics are limited by small island scale and high per-unit distribution costs. 9. Current financing constraints are beginning to directly threaten the Government of Maldives ’ ability to import fuel through STO. Fuel imports are financed through a combination of: (i) revolving commercial credit lines provided by international commodity traders and petroleum suppliers; and (ii) a revolving trade finance facility from the International Islamic Trade Finance Corporation (ITFC). Financed fuel invoices from suppliers and ITFC are settled through the Bank of Maldives (BML) and other domestic banks, supported by foreign exchange allocations from the Maldives Monetary Authority (MMA). Under the current supply agreement, STO is granted a supplier credit period of 30 days within an agreed credit limit and tenor. However, during periods of fuel price surges, getting the foreign exchange component required for Letter of Credit (LC) issuance can become constraint for the Government of Maldives. This constraint may also create challenges in settling outstanding supplier invoices within the agreed credit period. Slowdown in fuel imports is likely to lead to shortages and immediately impact critical service delivery. Given the Maldives’ near-total dependence on imported fuel, any disruption in the financing of fuel imports would rapidly translate into shortages across the system. This creates an acute risk that utilities and logistics operators will be unable to secure adequate fuel supplies, with potential cascading impacts on electricity provision, water supply, transport connectivity, health services, and tourism operations. 11. In this context, ensuring the continued availability of fuel for essential utilities and logistics represents the most urgent priority for maintaining basic service delivery and economic functioning during the current crisis. The proposed operation therefore focuses on financing critical fuel imports. While project financing will be disbursed against fuel imports, the development impact extends beyond the commodity itself, as maintaining energy availability is fundamental to sustaining essential services and economic activity. 12. The current emergency thus creates an urgent need for World Bank financing to support continued provision of public services. World Bank financing is critical to provide urgent counter-cyclical funds to help maintain essential fuel imports during a period of acute balance-of-payments pressure. By providing foreign exchange liquidity against verified fuel import payments, the proposed operation will help prevent disruptions to electricity generation, water desalination, inter-island transport, and other essential services that underpin the functioning of the Maldivian economy. 13. This proposed Project is designed as a time-bound emergency liquidity operation responding to a specific and _For Official Use Only_ acute foreign exchange shock that is constraining the country’s ability to finance critical fuel imports (Marine Gas Oil (MGO: 500ppm and/or 10ppm) and Mogas 95 Ron ULG (Unleaded Gasoline). It does not constitute a long-term investment in the energy sector, nor is it intended to cover Maldives’ full fuel import requirements on a sustained basis. Rather, by front-loading liquidity during a period of acute balance-of-payments stress, including through retroactive financing, the operation aims to rapidly unblock stalled import transactions and prevent immediate disruptions to essential services and logistics. 14. While this operation focuses on the near-term foreign exchange shock, the World Bank has a broader engagement in Maldives to address macro-fiscal vulnerabilities. The country’s vulnerability reflects a combination of rising public spending, mainly driven by high capital expenditure, subsidies, health spending and rising SOE‑related fiscal risks, and high-cost borrowing, which have eroded fiscal and external buffers over time. Addressing this underlying vulnerability requires implementation of structural and fiscal reforms, notably replacing untargeted subsidies with targeted social assistance, rationalizing public capital expenditures, SOE reform, and health financing reform. Technical assistance in these areas continues to be provided by the World Bank in coordination with other development partners under the Multilateral Development Bank (MDB+) platform as well as under the Maldives Competitiveness and Growth Project (MCGP, P179286) under implementation. 15. Finally, the financing structure of the operation reflects the constrained fiscal and debt environment currently facing Maldives. Approximately 80 percent of project financing (US$40 million) will be mobilized through the restructuring and recommitment of unwithdrawn balances from three existing IDA-financed projects [Sustainable and Integrated Labor Services (SAILS, P177240) US$20.7 million, Maldives Competitiveness and Growth Project (MCGP, P179286) US$13.2 million and Maldives Urban Development and Resilience Project (MUDRP, P163957) US$1.76 million]. The operation provides financing to GoM for ensuring the continued availability of petroleum products required for electricity generation, water desalination, and inter-island transport, which are critical for the functioning of the Maldivian economy and basic service delivery across the archipelago. 18. The Project comprises one component: • Component: Critical Goods Imports (Fuel) - US$50,000,000: This component finances the importation of petroleum products required for the continued operation of essential utilities and logistics services in Maldives. It provides time-bound emergency financing to reimburse eligible fuel import payments. Disbursements will be made against verified documentation of fuel import payments, ensuring rapid implementation while maintaining appropriate fiduciary control. While this component addresses immediate liquidity constraints affecting fuel imports, the World Bank will continue to engage with the Government of Maldives on a broader energy security and mitigation agenda through its ongoing renewable energy program, which serves as the country’s medium- to long term anchor for reducing dependence on imported fuels and easing fiscal pressures. Existing operations supporting solar generation, battery storage, and grid integration are already helping lower fuel import needs and associated subsidy costs, thereby complementing short term fuel support. If the external shock persists, the Bank will also explore targeted Technical Assistance (TA) through Bank administered Advisory Services to help the Government assess policy options that strengthen energy security during the transition, including fuel price hedging mechanisms, demand side management and energy efficiency measures, and the role of strategic reserves. Together, these efforts link short term fuel stabilization with a clear pathway toward a more resilient, lower cost, and fiscally sustainable energy system anchored in renewable energy. The Project carries a Moderate overall E&S risk rating, reflecting its emergency nature as a fuel import support operation with no civil works, land acquisition, or physical/economic displacement. 20. Environmental and social risks are rated Moderate, as the Project finances fuel procurement rather than civil works potential risks are indirect and related to fuel transportation, handling, and storage. These risks are well understood and manageable through existing regulatory frameworks and operational practices of STO, supported by the ESCP. _For Official Use Only_ 21. A proportionate, risk-based approach under ESS1 has been applied, consistent with the emergency context, limited scope, and reliance on existing institutional arrangements. The MoFP, as the implementing agency, has prior experience implementing World Bank–financed projects under both the former Safeguard Policies and the ESF. Environmental and social oversight will be carried out through the existing PMU under the MCGP, which includes designated environmental and social staff. 22. Environmental risks relate mainly to fuel transportation, offloading, storage, and distribution, including potential localized fuel spills, marine and near shore pollution, fire and explosion hazards, hazardous waste generation, and occupational health and safety risks. These risks are well understood and routinely managed in the fuel supply sector through existing national regulatory frameworks and the STO’s established operational procedures, which include spill prevention and response arrangements, emergency preparedness plans, OHS protocols, and incident reporting systems consistent with international maritime and fuel handling standards. The Project relies on existing infrastructure and does not introduce new fuel handling activities or facilities. 23. The Project relies on STO’s existing workforce and supply arrangements. Existing labor management systems, grievance mechanisms, and stakeholder engagement practices of MoFP and STO provide a solid foundation for managing labor related and community health and safety risks, supplemented by commitments under the ESCP. 24. As part of preparation, the E&S team conducted a high level review of the corporate level ESG/HSSE frameworks of STO and the fuel supplier, drawing on available policies, governance arrangements, organizational structures, and publicly disclosed information. This review confirmed that both entities have established systems and institutional capacity to manage the Project’s moderate, indirect, and operational risks. The remaining E&S consideration relates to the explicit application of these existing systems to Project-financed fuel and associated supply-chain activities, rather than to the adequacy of the corporate systems themselves. The Project will utilize the existing PMU under the MCGP within the MoFP which will serve as the Implementing Agency (IA) for the Project and will have overall responsibility for project coordination, fiduciary oversight, and compliance with World Bank requirements. The MCGP PMU will oversee project implementation, including financial management, procurement aspects, monitoring and reporting, and adherence to environmental and social commitments. The MCGP PMU will be responsible for liaising with the World Bank, consolidating project documentation, and ensuring timely submission of reports and withdrawal applications. The MCGP PMU is well placed to handle this function given its track record of satisfactory performance in the MCGP project, familiarity with World Bank requirements and adequate staffing (with no additional hiring anticipated). 26. The State Trading Organization (STO), a majority government-owned enterprise, will support MoFP in Project implementation, serving as the operational entity responsible for the procurement and importation of eligible fuel under the Project. STO will leverage its existing long-term contractual arrangements with fuel suppliers and its nationwide fuel distribution network to procure petroleum products required for electricity generation, water desalination, and inter- _For Official Use Only_ island transport. STO will execute fuel purchase transactions, make payments to suppliers, and maintain complete and verifiable documentation related to the defined positive list of eligible petroleum expenditures financed under the supply contracts. This documentation will be submitted to the IA to support reimbursement under the Project. Fuel purchases financed under the operation will be limited to eligible petroleum expenditures under existing and any potential future supply contracts and will be subject to applicable World Bank requirements, including documentation verification and relevant fiduciary and integrity provisions. All relevant contracts will be reviewed and approved by the World Bank to confirm their eligibility for Bank financing and compliance with applicable fiduciary and integrity requirements. MoFP, through STO, will ensure that each supplier of fuel has executed a letter of acceptance (LoA) of the World Bank’s Anticorruption Guidelines and sanctions framework, in form and substance satisfactory to the Association. This will be an effectiveness condition for the operation. 27. MoFP and STO has entered into a subsidiary agreement to ensure that Project implementation obligations under the Financing Agreement, undertaken by the Recipient (represented by MoFP), are appropriately passed on to STO.
