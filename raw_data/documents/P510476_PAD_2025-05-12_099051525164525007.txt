STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. The Government of Uganda’s (GoU’s) progressive refugee response model has generated a development dividend for hosting districts, but they still face major development challenges and refugees are well short of achieving self-reliance. Increasing refugee inflows continue to place a strain on host communities at a time of declining humanitarian aid. Since Phase I of the Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project (DRDIP) in the Horn of Africa was approved in May 2016, the refugee population in Uganda has increased markedly from 220,000 to over 1.8 million.1 More inflows are expected given the ongoing instability in the region. Most refugees are hosted in rural communities that are struggling with poverty and unemployment, human capital and service delivery deficits, limited basic infrastructure, and a degraded natural resource base. Despite the opportunities and rights afforded them, refugees in Uganda lack employment opportunities and suffer from poverty rates almost three times higher than Ugandan nationals. Given the huge population, self-reliance of refugees and host communities is an important development priority for Uganda. As a regional and global example of the value of an open-door policy and the development approach to forced displacement, it is crucial to support Uganda with long-term financing for refugees and host communities. 2. Seeing the protracted refugee presence as both a development challenge and an opportunity, DRDIP Phase II (DRDIP II) in Uganda will continue to address the negative impacts of the refugee presence while maximizing the positive. Negative impacts relate to overcrowded basic services (education, health, and water) and damage to the environment in refugee-hosting districts (RHDs) and transit districts. On the other hand, the refugee presence in Uganda is associated with substantial improvements in local development, demonstrating that a combination of progressive policies and long-term development responses has the potential to turn a refugee “burden” into an “opportunity.”2 In line with the objectives of the Global Compact on Refugees (GCR) and findings from the 2023 World Development Report on Migrants, Refugees, and Societies, DRDIP II will support the achievement of self-reliance of refugees and local residents and the integration of refugees into national service delivery systems, which lower costs and promote social cohesion. Accordingly, building on the success of Phase I, DRDIP II will continue to: (a) invest in basic service infrastructure such as schools, health facilities, water systems, roads, bridges, and markets; (b) respond to the environmental impacts of the refugee presence and Uganda’s land degradation crisis; and (c) support livelihood and income generation for refugees and host communities.3 B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Uganda is the largest refugee host in Africa, and among the five largest worldwide. There are over 1.8 million refugees and asylum seekers in the country at present, most of whom originate from South Sudan (54 percent) and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (32 percent). Uganda continues to experience significant inflows, with over 170,000 refugees having arrived during 2024. The refugees are formally settled in 12 of Uganda’s 146 districts, plus Kampala. 4. Uganda is globally recognized for its refugee policy framework, which is closely aligned with the priorities of the GCR. During the 2023 Global Refugee Forum (GRF), for which Uganda was a co-convener, the GoU made five pledges to support the national refugee response by: (a) increasing resilience and self-reliance; (b) taking action against climate change; (c) localizing the refugee response; (d) building durable solutions; and (e) ensuring effective transition management. Consistent with international good practice, the government’s ambitious transition roadmap seeks to convert some education, health, and water facilities run by humanitarian partners in refugee settlements to government management. Uganda DRDIP II will support this sustainable operating model by helping strengthen the capacity of local authorities and addressing infrastructure gaps so that facilities to be transitioned to government management would meet national standards. 5. Despite the strong policy enabling environment, both host communities and refugees in RHDs continue to face significant development deficits. Refugees are predominately hosted in rural communities that are struggling with major development challenges. While the refugee presence – primarily by attracting humanitarian and development assistance – has generally supported development progress in RHDs, poverty rates and basic service provision have either plateaued or worsened in rural areas of Uganda over the last decade. Rural access to clean water, for instance, has declined from 67 to 57 percent since 2013.4 Almost 60 percent of refugees are located in the Northern Region, which suffers from particularly high rates of poverty and limited basic infrastructure.5 Growing number of refugees are opting to settle in urban areas in search of better opportunities.6 6. Degradation of the natural environment is placing refugees and host communities at higher risk from climate change effects. Uganda’s economy is highly dependent on ‘climate-sensitive’ sectors such as agriculture, fisheries, tourism, and forestry. Ranked 163rd out of 187 on the Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative index, urgent action is required to address climate vulnerability in Uganda. Key trends over the last 60 years include temperature rises (1.3- degree Celsius average temperature increase, expected to reach 1.8 degrees by 2050) and a statistically significant reduction in rainfall. Droughts have become more frequent and longer-lasting, especially in the western, northern, and north-eastern regions, which cover most of the DRDIP II target areas.10 This is causing more frequent crop failure, threatening livelihoods, increasing food insecurity, and aggravating water scarcity. In the future, rainfall is expected to decrease further in the north and north-eastern regions and increase in the west. As the climate crisis intensifies, the country faces rising incidents of extreme weather, leading to disasters such as floods, droughts, and landslides.11 Between 2018 and 2023, over 455,000 people were displaced due to weather-related disasters, mainly floods.12 With rising rural- urban migration and unplanned urbanization, cities are especially prone to floods, the risk of which is exacerbated by climate change. To respond to climate vulnerability, DRDIP II will integrate adaptation and mitigation measures in activity design and implementation. It will also fund activities to improve climate-smart agricultural productivity, restore degraded land, and protect and expand tree cover. See Annex 2 for more details on climate adaptation and mitigation. 8. Humanitarian funding cuts are exacerbating the development challenges. Funding for refugee response has declined since 2018, while the refugee population continues to grow. Since July 2023, food rations have been cut, new arrivals have had limited access to water, and some humanitarian health workers are not being paid. This is causing pressure on social services, livelihoods, and infrastructure, triggering tensions within settlements and with host communities, negative coping behaviors, and mental health problems.13 This trend increases the urgency to expand integrated development responses to reduce the cost of refugee management and support sustainable responses. 9. Refugees are well integrated into national policies and planning structures, but more support is required on coordination at the national and district levels. Refugees are integrated into national and district planning systems and statistical systems under the National Development Plan (NDP). Ministers chair quarterly Comprehensive Refugee Response Framework (CRRF) Steering Group meetings, which bring together Ministries, Departments, and Agencies (MDAs), humanitarian and development partners, refugees, and RHD leadership. A district engagement forum and refugee engagement forum are also established under the CRRF, which is served by a small secretariat under the Office of the Prime Minister (OPM). While the coordination structures are fully functional, more support is needed for operational coordination on the ground considering the growing refugee population and the increasing number of development projects supporting host-community-refugee inclusion. For instance, in 2016, DRDIP was the only World Bank-financed project on forced displacement in Uganda. By 2025, however, there are 11 projects funded by the IDA Window for Host Communities and Refugees (WHR), with a total budget of US$666 million. Continued strong government leadership from OPM will be required to facilitate effective coordination across these and related projects. 10. II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Development Objective 12. To improve access to basic social and economic services, expand economic opportunities and enhance environmental management for host communities and refugees in the target areas. B. Theory of Change and PDO Indicators 13. The Project Development Objective (PDO) indicators are: • Displaced people and people in host communities provided with improved access to services (number, of which refugees, host community members, and women (percentage)) (corporate scorecard indicator); • Households benefiting from activities and actions that expand and enable economic activity (number, of which refugees, host community members, and women (percentage)); and • People benefitting from enhanced resilience of terrestrial and aquatic systems (number, of which refugees, host community members, women, and youth (number)) (corporate scorecard indicator). Theory of Change 14. The Theory of Change is centered on enhancing the socioeconomic inclusion of both refugees and host communities in Uganda. The project provides a practical manifestation of the development response to forced displacement, demonstrating the value of Uganda’s progressive refugee policies. Project activities will support self- reliance of refugees and host communities to reduce dependence on humanitarian aid. They will also support the government’s transition roadmap (see paragraph 4 above). Activities include improving access to basic services and economic opportunities and strengthening environmental management. A World Bank strategic review of the Uganda WHR-financed portfolio highlighted the substantial impact of the project, with notable achievements in strengthening the inclusiveness of district level development planning, strong community engagement, and efficient implementation through the community-driven development (CDD) approach and community procurement. The Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) for Phase I highlighted that other DRDIP countries drew inspiration from Uganda’s legal and policy framework and operational approaches on issues such as CDD, livelihood, and refugee inclusion in local planning and decision-making.18 20. Phase I introduced innovations for forced displacement preparedness and social cohesion. The project featured the world’s first displacement risk financing mechanism, the Displacement Crisis Response Mechanism (DCRM). The DCRM provided rapid contingency financing to build resilience in districts experiencing large refugee inflows. A pilot to reduce gender-based violence (GBV) and violence against children (VAC) also contributed to social cohesion. A 2022 World Bank study on forced displacement reinforced the value of projects such as DRDIP, concluding that ‘multi-sectoral development investments directed to refugees and host communities can improve welfare, mitigate the negative effects of displacement, generate positive externalities for host communities, and promote social cohesion.’19 21. DRDIP II will be informed by lessons learned from Phase I and from other development responses in Uganda and the region over the last seven years. Based on implementation experience and stakeholder consultations, key lessons include: (a) the CDD approach – particularly community-led procurement – is effective in the Uganda context for planning, community engagement, targeting, monitoring, and implementation; (b) the Labor-Intensive Public Works (LIPW) approach used for integrated natural resource management (INRM) subprojects based on the watershed approach has demonstrated encouraging results for environmental improvement, though with sustainability challenges for commercial assets such as tree plantations; (c) infrastructure investments have been of high quality and have improved basic services, including reported reductions in pupil-classroom, pupil-desk, and pupil-sanitation facility ratios. However, operations and maintenance (O&M) needs to improve; (d) implementation arrangements were effective, particularly the role of the district and the presence of the Inspectorate of Government (IG) to promote transparency and accountability; and (e) there were strong impacts from the VRF in host communities, however, it was less successful in refugee communities, where mindset change on accessing credit is required. 22. DRDIP II will place a strong emphasis on helping the government lead a coordinated approach to development responses to forced displacement. The large WHR-financed portfolio in Uganda has two major implications: (a) DRDIP II can do less, dropping some sectoral investments, such as renewable energy, that are now covered by other projects; and (b) government needs support to lead coordination of the development response across multiple operations. All infrastructure will be built to climate-resilient standards to reduce climate risk and strengthen adaptive capacity. Climate-risk assessments will be conducted as part of subproject design to maximize climate adaptation and mitigation. The assessments will reflect climate vulnerabilities in each location, such as: (a) RHDs and settlements in the northern region are increasingly prone to rainfall deficits and drought; and (b) project sites in the western parts of the country experience heavy rainfall and are vulnerable to flooding and landslides. Subprojects will address these climate risks through: (a) investments in water in northern regions; (b) flood mitigation measures on steeper slopes, bridges and climate-resilient road investments in the west; (c) use of energy-efficient light emitting diode (LED) lighting; and (d) water harvesting as standard for public institutions. Adaptive capacity will be enhanced through: (a) investments in water supply in drought-affected areas; (b) upgrading of schools and multi-purpose centers to be used as emergency shelters in case of flooding or other natural disasters; and (c) upgrading of health facilities to support response to increased water-borne diseases in areas prone to increased rainfall and flooding. Activities will be implemented through three sub-components. 25. Sub-component 1.1: Community and Strategic Investment Funds (US$174.5 million). The sub-component will improve communities’ access to basic social services and economic infrastructure designed to help expand and improve service delivery and build infrastructure for local development in education, water supply, human health, public administration rural roads, market structures, and storage facilities. School and health facility construction included staff housing to attract and retain personnel. These priorities will continue in Phase II. Sub-component 1.3: Displacement Crisis Response Mechanism (DCRM) (US$10 million). The DCRM is a contingency financing mechanism for RHDs that experience large refugee inflows. Funds from this sub-component will be rapidly disbursed to districts that reach pre-agreed population triggers in terms of children per school classroom, patients per health facility, and people per water point. The DCRM financing will be used for infrastructure construction, upgrading or rehabilitation to expand education, health, and water service capacity in districts experiencing public service pressure caused by large refugee inflows. The DCRM triggers, eligible expenditures, and other details will be defined in an updated DCRM Manual for Phase II, approval of which by the World Bank is a disbursement condition. The sub-component will: (a) fund data collection to measure strains on schools, health facilities, and water points in the RHDs; and (b) finance approximately 41 infrastructure subprojects. Through ongoing analytical work, the World Bank has developed an Artificial Intelligence (AI)-based model that predicts refugee inflows before they occur.21 If the World Bank is able to raise funds to run the AI model live, the GoU will undertake an assessment to consider using it for Phase II to facilitate the disbursement of DCRM funding to districts before large refugee inflows occur, supporting preparedness and minimizing stress on host populations and refugees in the RHDs. Component 2: Integrated Natural Resource Management (US$30 million equivalent) 30. Component 2 will support investments in environmental restoration and protection to respond to ecosystem degradation in the RHDs and additional impacts caused by climate change and refugee presence. The expansion of farming by refugees and host communities is driving rapid deforestation in the RHDs, reducing the extent of natural forests and woodlands. Refugees and hosts also harvest wood for cooking, shelter, and fencing in excess of sustainable yields, causing additional degradation of tree cover and affecting fragile sites such as wetlands, hillsides, and riverine areas. In addition to the environmental impacts, this can be a source of tension between refugees and host community members. Climate change is exacerbating these challenges, as previously explained, with lower rainfall, longer and more frequent droughts, and more frequent extreme weather events. These factors are causing more crop failure, water scarcity and floods, droughts and landslides. As in Phase I, INRM activities will be identified and planned based on the watershed approach. The component will support a planning process in each RHD to identify priority watersheds and determine the most appropriate environmental remediation measures. Subproject planning will be based on multi-year planning principles using the GoU’s national community-based LIPW guidelines. The watershed approach will be aligned with the Parish Development Model (PDM) to comply with the GoU’s development framework. The project will prepare a participatory multi-year watershed plan in each target area, which will be translated into annual investment plans. Subprojects will be implemented through group-based approaches, with a participation target of 50 percent women. Implementing partners (IPs) will be contracted to provide technical support for planning and implementation. Component 2 will also finance the equipment and materials required to implement the selected subprojects, together with capacity-building measures. 32. The Phase II design responds to lessons learned from Phase I as follows: (a) some activities, such as drip irrigation systems and fish ponds, are too technically complex for communities to manage, so they will be dropped; (b) due to sustainability concerns about community-managed woodlots, tree planting activities will only support pre-existing groups with proven management capacity; (c) IP contract payments will be restructured to provide incentives for quality and to ensure they facilitate community-led implementation rather than implement activities themselves; (d) the subproject budget ceiling will be increased to reflect inflation and ensure sufficient scale to generate environmental impacts; and (e) due to the major investments in electricity provision and clean cooking under the World Bank-financed Electricity Access Scale-up Project (P166685), including in RHDs, DRDIP II will discontinue support for access to renewable energy, except for the provision of renewable energy and/or improved cooking systems for public institutions, which will be incorporated into Component 1. Component 2 will be organized around two sub-components, as described below. 33. Sub-component 2.1: Restoration of Degraded Ecosystems (US$20 million). The DRDIP II Environmental and Social Assessment (ESA) carried out in 2024 identified various types of sensitive and degraded ecosystems within the project area, including riverbanks, wetlands, bare and steep sloping hillsides, forest reserves, gully erosion sites, and other sites at risk due to climate change. Such areas serve as water catchments, biodiversity hotspots, and sources of ecosystem services to the communities and will inform the selection of watersheds for restoration activities. Subproject selection within those watersheds will be guided by local needs and community priorities that will respond to climate change impacts. Measures to be supported will include: (a) construction of soil and water conservation structures, such as terraces, bunds, check dams and trenches to build community resilience to climate change-induced water scarcity; and (b) planting of ground cover and indigenous trees to reduce run-off, tackle erosion and landslides, enrich the ecosystem, re-stabilize the environment, and strengthen resilience to drought. These time-limited interventions are expected to directly respond to climate change impacts on land in the target areas. Activities will be implemented through the LIPW model, providing a commensurate amount of cash to poor and vulnerable community members to implement measures that will improve local environmental conditions. LIPW beneficiaries will also have compulsory savings targets to build financial resilience and help prepare them to access financing under Component 3 on economic opportunity. 34. Sub-component 2.2: Expansion of Tree Cover (US$10 million). The raising and planting of trees will be integrated under this sub-component to ensure better coordination and appropriate sequencing. Recognized community groups with demonstrated experience in forestry activities will be supported to establish or expand their operations. This will include improving the scale and quality of community tree nurseries, and the establishment of tree plantations with market orientation, based on locally appropriate and climate-resilient species with commercial demand and also adapted to future climate conditions. The use of indigenous tree species will be promoted where technically viable. Inter-cropping within plantations during the early years of growth will provide co-benefits to group members from food and cash crops. Groups will also be supported to establish wood fuel plantations in public institutions with kitchens (e.g., schools and health facilities) to provide a sustainable supply of fuel for mass catering. Sub-component 3.1: Resilient Value Chains and Enterprise Investments (US$77.5 million). Value chain investments to be supported have been identified in each district based on comparative advantage, profitability, and marketing potential. Existing value chain diagnostics indicate that crop-based investments in the Western Region will consider coffee, beans, maize, sweet bananas, vegetables, and aquaculture, contributing to industrial potential and food security. On the livestock side, in the same region dairy has been identified as a key priority investment. The West Nile and Northern regions will prioritize on-farm value chains such as cassava, maize, soybeans, sunflowers, groundnuts, sorghum, sesame, as well as aquaculture. Off-farm livestock activities could consider trading, baked goods, agro-technology (milling), apiary, piggery, and cattle-rearing for milk and beef value chains. In refugee settings, where land access is relatively limited, non-farm enterprises and small livestock (e.g., poultry) will be encouraged. Utilizing existing diagnostics, a list of high potential value chain options by order of priority for the targeted regions has been developed. Each district will target two-three of these key value chains to ensure scale, impact, and synergy with ongoing investments. Priority value chains will inform group-based investments through the CDD model, where a positive list will be presented to beneficiaries for informed choice. 38. For selected value chains, the sub-component will strengthen coordination efforts at the national and district levels to avoid duplication. The project will support the development of a District Economic Opportunities Plan (DEOP) that will outline existing efforts in the chosen value chains, identify private sector players and off-takers, and compile a catalogue of assets and infrastructure requiring construction or maintenance. The DEOP will serve as a framework for DRDIP II investments to collaborate effectively with activities under the Uganda Climate Smart Agricultural Transformation Project (UCSATP – P173296), the Generating Growth Opportunities and Productivity for Women Enterprises project (GROW – P176747), the Investment for Industrial Transformation and Employment (INVITE – P171607), and the Northern Uganda Social Action Fund Phase II (NUSAF IV – P179904). 39. Primary producers actively engaged in priority value chains will have access to VRF grants of up to US$3,000 per SHG, to be managed by a Village Revolving Fund Management Committee (VRFMC). This will serve as a sustainable capital base for well-performing groups, allowing members to access credit for climate-smart inputs, technology upgrades, value addition, and diversified income-generating activities. Value – and challenges – of the CDD approach in Uganda. The CDD approach empowers communities to assess and identify their development needs, ensuring that project investments align with local priorities. Procurement of goods, works, and services for infrastructure subjects by communities through the CDD approach was perceived by stakeholders as a key element of the success of DRDIP in Uganda. It proved to be quicker and more cost effective than regular public procurement methods and is now being adopted by other World Bank-financed operations in Uganda. The project will fund a study to explore the incorporation of community procurement into national procurement guidelines. The CDD approach also brings refugees and host communities together for joint planning and implementation, underpinning social cohesion outcomes. While effective, however, it can be difficult to supervise and ensure quality control over hundreds Page 14 The World Bank Uganda Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project Phase II PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT (P510476) of sub-project sites. Monitoring will be enhanced in Phase II, including regular beneficiary phone surveys and quarterly technical supervision missions, targeting infrastructure subprojects that are more complex and challenging. 52. Importance of collaboration and partnerships across the humanitarian-development nexus. The scale of the refugee presence and the scope of interventions required to bring about inclusion and self-reliance necessitates collaboration between various stakeholders, including government, non-governmental organizations, and international partners. Specific activities under DRDIP II to strengthen government-led coordination efforts are intended to build strong partnerships to create a holistic and integrated response framework. 53. Refugee situations tend to last for years and managing them exclusively through emergency and humanitarian programs is ineffective. The main conclusion of the World Development Report 2023 on Migrants, Refugees, and Societies is that policies should be geared toward financial and social sustainability by means of internal mobility, self- reliance, and inclusion in national services. The design of DRDIP II fully aligns with these lessons through its focus on self- reliance and integration of refugees into national service delivery systems. 54. Multi-sectoral development investments directed to forcibly displaced populations and host communities can improve welfare, mitigate the negative effects of displacement, generate positive externalities, and promote social cohesion. The 2022 World Bank report, Social Cohesion and Forced Displacement, concludes that to pre-empt social tensions that can emerge in situations of forced displacement, governments and humanitarian and development agencies need to direct short- and long-term assistance to both the displaced and host communities. This can help reduce real and perceived inequalities and prevent host communities from forming negative attitudes about newly arriving displaced persons. DRDIP II will continue the area-based approach of Phase I, providing multi-sectoral support to respond to the impacts of forced displacement, covering refugees and host communities. III. The project aligns with Paris Agreement objectives for mitigation and adaptation. DRDIP II will help the GoU to meet its commitments on climate adaptation and mitigation. It is aligned with the GoU’s National Climate Change Policy (2015) and Updated Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) 2022, in which it commits, among other things, to increase the resilience of communities, infrastructure, and ecosystems. Uganda’s Updated NDC includes a conditional commitment to reduce GHG emissions by 24.7 percent by 2030 relative to a business-as-usual scenario. The project does not hinder this mitigation goal by ensuring that: (a) all infrastructure subprojects will be subject to climate risk assessments and will comply with climate-resilient standards; (b) the economic opportunity component will only finance climate-sensitive activities; and (c) INRM activities will directly respond to ecosystem degradation and climate change impacts on the environment. In addition, the project will explore opportunities to monetize emission reduction resulting from project activities and to generate revenue through carbon credits; pairing carbon credits with other development activities can improve buy-in and sustainability of these projects. More information on adaptation and mitigation measures is provided below and further elaborated in Annex 2. 66. Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks. Project activities include construction, rehabilitation, and upgrading of basic service infrastructure such as roads, schools, water systems, markets, and health facilities (Component 1); INRM activities, focusing on restoration and protection to address ecosystem degradation (Component 2); and economic opportunity (Component 3), which may be moderately impacted by the main climate risks identified for the project location (floods, droughts, landslides, storms, and earthquakes). To keep material impacts to an acceptable (low) level, the project design includes several risk reduction measures, as follows. All infrastructure (Component 1) will be designed to climate-resilient standards based on rigorous climate risk assessments, with capacity building on how to conduct these assessments; subprojects will be located considering flood and landslide risks; and water investments will be prioritized in the northern region, where decreasing rainfall and drought are high risks. INRM activities (Component2) will target degraded, eroded, and fragile sites and will integrate climate remedial measures within the prioritized watersheds to restore and protect ecosystems, to reduce the risk of climate impacts worsening these areas. This will be particularly important in the western regions, where climate-induced flood risk is highest. Grants and VRF issued under sub-component 3.1 will only support economic opportunity activities informed by climate-sensitive market assessments and value chain analyses. Subprojects supporting the multiplication of climate-resilient seeds will be encouraged. Sub- component 3.2 will provide capacity building on climate-resilient business planning and adaptive systems strengthening. 67. Assessment and reduction of mitigation of risks. The project is expected to have low impact on GHG emissions and to align with Uganda’s transition to low GHG emission pathways. Project activities will likely generate GHG emissions, but given their small scale, these are low, and significantly less than without the project (i.e., no other means of achieving the PDO with lower GHG emissions). Infrastructure upgrading and rehabilitation (Component 1) will integrate low-carbon features such as water harvesting, renewable energy, and improved cooking systems for public institutions. Road subprojects will not expand or promote expansion of roads into areas of high carbon stocks or high biodiversity areas. INRM activities in Component 2 will contribute to a reduction of GHG emissions, for example, through the construction of soil and water conservation structures, planting of grasses, ground cover, indigenous trees and shrubs, and expansion of tree cover. Component 3 will provide capacity building on climate-resilient business planning. Overall, the operation Page 19 The World Bank Uganda Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project Phase II PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT (P510476) does not prevent the transition to lower-carbon alternatives that may become viable in the future and remains viable after accounting for transition risks. 68. Through the CDD approach, communities will be facilitated to assess development problems, prepare a local development plan, and implement priority subprojects themselves. Kyangwali, Rwamwanja, and Kiryandongo refugee settlements are situated near Bugoma Central Forest Reserve, Katonga Wildlife Reserve, and Kibeka Central Forest Reserve, which have already been encroached upon. Likewise, the south-western region has Lake Nakivale and Mburo-Nakivale wetland system, which serves as a habitat for unique species of birds, animals, plants, and fish. Grievance Redress Services 91. Grievance Redress. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a project supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms or the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly reviewed in order to address project-related concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non-compliance with its policies and procedures, and the Dispute Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the attention of Bank Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond. For information on how to submit complaints to the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS), visit http://www.worldbank.org/GRS. For information on how to submit complaints to the Bank’s Accountability Mechanism, visit https://accountability.worldbank.org. V. KEY RISKS 92. The overall risk rating is Substantial. Key risks and challenges include upcoming elections, potential miscommunication of project interventions and benefits, and political economy challenges, which may put at risk the effective use of project resources. Institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability (Substantial). OPM implemented DRDIP I to a high standard, but the long gap between the closure of Phase I and commencement of Phase II means many project staff have moved on to new positions. The main project delivery mechanism is CDD, which means communities lead on planning, subproject identification, procurement, and FM. While this approach has proven effective, it requires extensive technical support to succeed. The success of the project also depends on intensive coordination across line agencies, districts, and humanitarian agencies, adding to complexity. The World Bank will provide hand-on support to the government teams on core project management functions, such as FM, procurement, and E&S management. At the community level, the project will contract partner organizations to build local implementation capacity. 95. Fiduciary (Substantial). The FM risk is Substantial and the procurement risk is Moderate. The major risks are delays in disbursing to communities and making payments as well as accounting for project funds by local governments. The government’s payment system that local governments will use, known as the TSA, also faces cyberattack risks. These will be mitigated through training of local governments and communities to improve the efficiency of disbursements, making payments, and accounting of project funds. In addition, MoFPED and BoU are working on strengthening internal control mechanisms around TSA payments to ensure the correct beneficiaries are paid. For procurement, community-level procurement proved an effective mechanism in Phase I and will be continued in Phase II. However, Phase I demonstrated procurement risks, including delays, absence of thresholds for community procurement, and weak contract management. These risks will be mitigated by developing business standards for procurement processing, updating the community procurement module, and developing a tracking mechanism for contract performance. For both procurement and FM issues, the strong engagement of the IG, backed by the network of community monitors that it will establish, will support both corruption prevention and response in the project. 96. Environment and Social (Substantial). DRDIP II will transition from the World Bank safeguards policies to the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) in Phase II. DRDIP personnel were trained on the ESF, however, several PIST staff left when the project closed, and new staff will need technical support. The main E&S risks are associated with civil works subprojects under component 1 and capacity to screen and ensure compliance with ESSs across hundreds of subproject sites. Contextual social risks may also be significant, as declining humanitarian aid could lead to tensions within refugee populations, with potential to spill over to host communities. The social risk is also substantial given the potential for exclusion and discrimination of vulnerable or marginalized individuals or groups. To mitigate these risks, the project includes capacity-building initiatives for E&S staff at the national and sub-national levels, such as targeted training, hands-on mentoring, and coaching from the World Bank. Robust provisions for E&S compliance will be incorporated into contracts with construction companies and suppliers. Regular E&S audits will verify adherence to standards. 97. Other (Substantial). As required for all projects which include financing from the WHR, the “other” category refers to the risk that the country will not maintain an adequate protection framework for refugees. Uganda has long had progressive laws and policies and an open-door response to forced displacement. However, large new inflows over the last two years, ongoing instability in the region and major cuts to humanitarian aid pose risks to the protection framework. Accordingly, the risk is rated Substantial for this category. This demonstrates the urgency of increasing the provision of long-term development resources to refugee-hosting areas, but balancing support for refugees and local residents.
