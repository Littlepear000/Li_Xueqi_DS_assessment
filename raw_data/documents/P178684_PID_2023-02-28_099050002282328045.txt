Introduction and Context Country Context 1. The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is the largest country in Sub-Saharan Africa – with an estimated population of 92.4 million.1 The DRC is a fragile country, due to its long history of conflict, political upheaval and instability, and authoritarian rule. With its abundant and diverse natural resources, including critical minerals needed for the green energy transition, a strategic location at the center of Africa, and a large and young population, the DRC has considerable economic potential. However, weak governance and institutions, mismanagement of natural resources, and protracted conflict and violence have all hindered its progress in developing human capital and infrastructure, leading to economic underperformance and high levels of poverty. 2. The third-largest population of people living in poverty in the world lives in DRC. Although the two existing household budget surveys (2005 and 2012) have revealed a decline in extreme poverty from 94.3 to 77.2 percent between 2005 and 2012, the number of poor people is increasing by about 1.5 million every year due to the high rate of population growth (3 percent). The poverty rate is 69.7 percent. 3. DRC ranked 164th out of 174 countries on the 2020 Human Capital Index (HCI), reflecting decades of conflict and fragility, and limiting its development. The country has an HCI score of 0.37, which is below the 0.40 average for Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and means that a Congolese child born today can expect to achieve only 37 percent of his/her potential, compared to what would have been possible if provided with a full, quality schooling experience and optimal health conditions. The main contributors to low HCI are the low child survival rate of children under age 5 (0.91), stunted growth of children (the share of non-stunted children is only 0.57) and quality of education (while a 4-year-old child is expected to complete 9.1 years of schooling before his/her 18th birthday, factoring in what children actually learn, the number of expected years of schooling is only 4.5). While girls fare better in terms of the health components of HCI, they lag behind boys both in terms of expected years of schooling and learning outcomes. 4. DRC ranked 151st out of 170 countries in the 2021 Gender Inequality Index, which assesses national gender gaps using reproductive health, empowerment, and the labor market as its key benchmarks. Similarly, the Gender Development Index is currently 0.885, indicating that women’s human development is about 89 percent of men’s (UNDP 2022). Across DRC, girls and women face profound and interconnected disadvantages compared to men. National-level indicators from the World Bank’s Africa Human Capital Strategy show that girls and women in DRC are highly disadvantaged in terms of empowerment and agency over life choices; access to enhanced health services to enable family planning; education, particularly at the secondary level and beyond; and employment, in terms of labor market participation and earnings. Gender-Based Violence (GBV) is pervasive in the country: 52 percent of women aged 15–49 reported experiencing physical violence1 and 27 percent have experienced sexual violence1, most commonly at the hands of a current or former husband or intimate partner.1 This level of violence, higher than global and regional averages for Sub-Saharan Africa, represents a critical barrier to women’s and girls’ full participation in the social and economic space. 5. The COVID pandemic has contributed to widening existing gaps, especially for women and girls who were most affected by COVID-19 restrictions and school closures. First, according to interviews with school principals, attendance dropped after schools reopened, following a 7-month closure period due to COVID-19 restrictions. Second, tensions in the food supply emerged in the areas most affected by COVID-19, resulting in a rise in malnutrition among young children, as evidenced by the surveys undertaken by the Social Sciences Analytics Cell (SSAC). Furthermore, there has been a decrease in the use of contraception, an increase in pregnancies, and a decrease in the use of prenatal care. The increase in early pregnancies is pushing young girls out of school. Finally, the incidence of sexual- and gender-based violence increased dramatically, with rates more than doubling since the beginning of the COVID-19 crisis.2 6. Addressing gender disparities, by investing in girls’ empowerment, learning, and health, and by expanding their economic opportunities will be critical for the Congolese Government to revitalize the economy and put the country on a path towards sustainable development. Achieving lasting results in improving girls’ education will be particularly important as it can be a powerful transformative force for the girls, their communities, and the economy as a whole. Girls’ education, particularly at the secondary level, is a factor that consistently positively influences not only the girls’ lives, but also improves many other development outcomes, such as a reduction in child and maternal mortality, improvements in educational outcomes of offspring, a reduction in poverty and the achievement of equitable growth (World Development Report (WDR, 2012). 7. Climate change poses a serious risk to the DRC’s sustainable development. Due to a combination of political, geographic, and social factors, DRC ranks 177th out of 181 on the 2018 Notre Dame Global Gain Index,3 illustrating its high vulnerability to climate change. Globally, the DRC is therefore one of the most vulnerable countries, but also the 5th least prepared4 – highlighting its high vulnerability to the effects of climate change, but also its complete lack of preparedness in making investments to address it.5 8. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 9. Primary and secondary education in DRC includes public and private schools, with public schools managed under two regimes: écoles conventionnées and écoles non-conventionnées. In 2020, public schools constituted the majority of schools:87 percent in primary education and 84 percent in secondary education. Écoles conventionnées account for 80 percent of public elementary schools and 78 percent of public secondary schools. They are run by faith-based organizations (FBOs) under a 1977 agreement. This agreement establishes that the State “organizes�? education and churches “manage�? the schools. The State defines the curriculum, oversees quality, and pays staff and operating costs. The FBOs hire teachers and mobilize additional resources as needed, including for new infrastructure. They have their own administration and inspection services. Écoles non-conventionnées are managed directly by the Government. Both types of schools are supervised by administrative structures called management offices (bureaux gestionnaires; BGs). 10. The Government of DRC has made efforts to improve access to education, and as a result, enrollment rates have improved significantly across the whole education system for both boys and girls between 2000 and 2020. However, participation and completion remain low, particularly at the pre-primary and secondary levels. In 2020, an estimated 602,526 children were enrolled in 8,250 preschools staffed by 24,747 teachers, 18.8 million in 59,987 primary schools staffed by 446 205 teachers, and 6.8 million in 32,787 secondary school staffed by 474,224 teachers6. The pre- primary gross enrollment rate for 3-5-year-olds was 5.7 percent in 2020. At the primary level, completion is not yet universal, with the gross intake rate for the last grade of primary education estimated at 71 percent in 2020. The secondary school network is limited. Ten provinces (including Kinshasa) have only one to two secondary schools per 1,000 secondary school-age children and youth (Figure 2). Seventeen percent of children enrolled in secondary school have to walk six kilometers or more to school. This is particularly problematic for girls. Widespread harassment of students by teachers, including sexual abuse, exploitation and sexual harassment (SEA/SH), has been linked to dropouts in secondary schools in DRC. Physical and sexual violence against girls is higher in the DRC than in neighboring countries. Teenage girls and younger women are particularly vulnerable, and local community engagement in school management practices is not sufficient to reduce such pervasiveness. Thirty eight percent of girls aged 15 to 19 have experienced physical violence and 16 percent have experienced sexual violence.20 The scarcity of female teachers is seen as contributing to this situation. Female teachers represent only 16 percent of the workforce in public secondary education. Various factors contribute to this low rate, including the relative difficulty women (compared to men) face to relocate, financial insecurity if the teaching position is not permanent and salaried, a work environment that is not always welcoming to women, and traditional norms pertaining to professional roles at the secondary level. However, given that at the primary level the proportion of female teachers is approximately one-third (and rising), these constraints are not binding. With the right incentives in place, there is room to hire substantially more female teachers, of which there is a large pool. On the government's side, financial incentives from another Bank- financed operation (Emergency Equity and System Strengthening in Education, P172341) have proven effective in getting teachers added to the public payroll. On the teachers’ side, the relatively generous, stable and secure salary encourages applications and promotes retention. Each year, approximately 5,000 women complete a teaching degree program that qualifies them to teach in secondary schools. Currently there is a backlog of female college graduates who completed teacher training but have not found a teaching position for several years. This backlog of trained teachers would be adequate to fill the approximately 6,000 teaching posts that become vacant each year because of turnover for some time. Global research has shown that the gender of teachers can have a positive impact on girls’ educational performance, especially in socially conservative cultures, where girls and women are isolated or segregated into sex-specific spaces and have less mobility, or where there are strong concerns about physical or sexual violence against girls by teachers or students.22 Research also suggests that hiring more female teachers is necessary in contexts where there is a large disparity in the ratio of male to female teachers and where social norms prevent girls from attending school with male teachers.23 22. The teacher training system is fragmented and inadequately linked to practice. The pre-service training of preschool and primary school teachers is handled by the Ministry of Primary, Secondary Education and Vocational Training (MEPST), while the Ministry of Higher Education is responsible for the pre-service training of secondary school teachers. There is a disconnect between pre-service and in-service training, as well as between training in general and the needs of teachers in the classrooms. Continuous professional development to strengthen the quality of teaching is pursued locally through schools and networks of community schools (Réseaux d’Écoles de Proximité, or REPs).26 Within the school, teaching quality is monitored and reinforced primarily through the director of studies and the heads of instructional units (Chefs des Unités Pédagogiques; CUPs).27 Schools have few pedagogical support resources, often lacking such basics as the latest academic program in sufficient quantities for each teacher, as well as reference materials on how to strengthen various areas of teaching-learning practice. An inspector periodically visits each school to check for compliance to standards and may review teaching-learning practices; but neither the inspector, the director of studies nor the CUPs systematically mentor teachers based on the findings of classroom observations. There is a little or no use of digital technologies as means to support teachers’ professional development and classroom practices. 24. Quality assurance systems are lacking at the secondary level. An institution for quality assurance (CIEAS) has been created at the MEPST, but as yet it has not developed instruments and procedures to assess learning outcomes or teaching-learning practices. Such assessments have never been conducted at the secondary level. Demand-side constraints 25. Household poverty is one of the main obstacles to secondary school participation, particularly for girls. The proportion of secondary-school age girls who are not in school ranges from 12 percent among the wealthiest quintile to 45 percent among the bottom quintiles. Gender disparity also increases with poverty. While the proportion of out-of-school children in the top quintile is roughly equal for girls and boys, the gender disparity steadily increases as the household becomes poorer, rising to 13 percentage points for the poorest quintile (45/32 percent for girls/boys). The main constraints are the direct and opportunity costs associated with schooling. At the secondary level, households spend approximately US$80 per year per child on school fees and other expenses (e.g., transportation, uniforms), which puts education out of reach for many of the poorest households. The project will be implemented over a five-year period using an investment project financing (IPF) modality with performance-based conditions (PBCs). In order to empower girls through education where the challenges are particularly extensive, to benefit from synergies from complementary Bank-financed operations, and to facilitate the transition to secondary education in provinces where the Bank has been supporting the Government’s free primary education policy, the project will operate in the ten CPF focus provinces, with a core focus on five provinces where girls’ net secondary enrollment rates are the lowest. The project will put in place the basic elements (currently missing in secondary education) to create an effective learning environment and promote knowledge, skills and behavior changes needed to make teaching more effective and schools safe, inclusive and free of SEA/SH, and to provide sexual reproductive health (SRH) and menstrual health management (MHM) education in secondary school classrooms and through extracurricular activities. Component 1: Improved Access to Quality and Girl-Friendly Schools (US$ 210 million) 33. The objectives of this component are to improve access to school by creating quality learning environments, alleviating financial barriers to female participation, and increasing the proportion of female teachers. 34. Sub-component 1.1: Improving Learning Environments (US$ 140 million). The objective of this sub-component is to increase access to quality, girl-friendly and inclusive learning environments, including for children who are refugees, internally displaced or with disabilities. 35. Sub-component 1.2: Increasing Female Participation in Education (US$ 70 million). The objective of this sub- component is to increase the number of girls attending secondary schools and the proportion of female teachers teaching at this level. The objective of this component is to improve the quality of education, particularly for girls, through an improved availability and effectiveness of teaching-learning resources and practices, citizens’ engagement and the creation of safe and inclusive school environments. 37. Sub-component 2.1: Strengthening the Curriculum, Textbooks and Teaching-Learning Materials (US$ 90 million). The objectives of the sub-component are to increase the availability and use of quality, affordable and gender-sensitive teaching-learning materials including in digital format. 38. Sub-component 2.2: Strengthening the Quality of Teaching (US$ 60 million). The objectives of this sub-component are to strengthen beneficiaries’ voice and participation throughout project implementation, and to promote safe and inclusive education environments for girls through the establishment of an accountability and response framework and of measures to prevent violence against children in schools. The main social risks arise from concerns about access and social inclusion/exclusion of girls from poor and vulnerable communities and indigenous peoples (IP) communities, as well as the potential need for squatter removal and additional land requirements for expansion of existing infrastructure or development of new schools. The project will to the extent possible avoid new acquisition of land, and construct on existing Ministry of Education/school owned property, and while land scarcity is not a challenge in rural settings, a Resettlement Policy Framework (RPF) has been prepared as part of the project to ensure that any land acquisition by the Government and ensuing resettlement is managed in accordance with ESS 5. According to the RPF, no construction activities for sub-projects will be undertaken until all resettlement is completed and PAPs are resettled and/or fully compensated. According to the LMP, the project’s E&S measures will include weekly monitoring of workers by labor inspectors to ensure compliance with standards, and frequent verification that no children are employed below the legal age for the work they are performing. Other risks identified in the LMP pertain to potential labor influx because of construction works and labor issues (poor labor practices, insufficient wages), discriminatory employment (recruitment, retention, and promotion of women as teachers and principals, including women from IP communities and other marginalized groups). To enhance inclusion of vulnerable and excluded communities (including under component 1.2: hiring of female teachers), the project will encourage training and participation of IP women through capacity building as proposed in the IPPF, and large-scale awareness campaigns will be undertaken in vulnerable and IP communities to enroll girls in the scholarship program. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 44. Project and sector strategic oversight. The Steering Committee (SC) established in the context of another, ongoing Bank-financed operation (EESSE, P172341) will be charged with providing overall direction during project implementation and ensuring that the different ministries coordinate as needed to achieve the PDO. The SC will continue to be chaired by the Minister of MEPST and to have as members the Secretary Generals (SGs) of MEPST, MOB and MOF, a delegate of the President’s Office, a delegate of the Prime Minister’s Office, and the Coordinator of SPACE. The SC will continue to meet twice per year. A government decree amending the SC membership and referencing its added responsibilities in overseeing the project will be published within three months of its coming into effect. 45. Project coordination and implementation. The MEPST is the government entity responsible for implementing the project with the technical, administrative, fiduciary and safeguards support of a Project Coordination Team (PCT – see below). The MEPST is ultimately accountable for meeting the project objectives and for providing oversight, monitoring and evaluation of project activities with the support of the PCT. The MEPST is also responsible for overall management of the project and providing guidance to the PCT, as well as to the MEPST directorates and technical units that will implement project activities within their functional missions. With respect to sub-component 2.2 (a) only, the MESU will similarly be responsible for overall management and for providing guidance to the PCT regarding ISP-related activities, as well as for ensuring that the ISPs implement the activities involving them. The directorates and technical units of the MEPST with implementation responsibilities, as well as the ISPs, will work closely with the PCT. 46. The Secretary General (SG) of MEPST will be responsible for the regular, overall coordination of the project.
