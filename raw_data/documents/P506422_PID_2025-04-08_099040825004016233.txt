Introduction and Context Country Context 1. The Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) and the Federated States of Micronesia (FSM) are defined by their extreme remoteness, small size and geographic dispersion. RMI consists of 29 coral atolls and five isolated islands in the Western and Central Pacific Ocean (WCPO), with a total land area of 181 square kilometers (km²) and an Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) of just under 2 million km², making it the 19th largest EEZ in the world. FSM is an even more widely dispersed, and overall larger, archipelagic nation in the WCPO, comprising over 600 islands stretching 2,700 km² from West to East, and organized into four semi-autonomous states: Pohnpei, Chuuk, Yap, and Kosrae. FSM's total land area is 702 km² and its EEZ is the 14th largest in the world, covering approximately 3 million km², including some of the most productive tuna fishing grounds in the WCPO. The geographic dispersion and remoteness contribute to high costs of public administration and an uneven availability of quality public services. 2. Both countries are classified as fragile due to limited state capacity, and exposure to natural disasters and climate change. Fragility in RMI and FSM arises from their extreme geographic dispersion and limited economic viability, thin institutional capacity, unplanned urbanization, youth unemployment, poor land governance, and gender-based violence. There is also high exposure to frequent and damaging natural hazards, with both countries having low capacity to manage the ensuing risks. Vulnerability is exacerbated by the lack of technical capacity for adequate disaster preparedness and mitigation, and by climate change, which is predicted to amplify the magnitude of cyclones, droughts, and flooding. Addressing these challenges is essential for building resilience and supporting sustainable development in both countries. 3. Negative net migration has driven a decline in the population of both countries in the past decade, resulting in acute shortages of skilled labor. Between 2012 and 2022, FSM’s population (currently 115,224) declined by about 10 Page 2 The World Bank FSM and RMI: Implementation Capacity for Transformative Results (IMPACT) (P506422) percent, with outbound migration playing a central role1. RMI's population of 41,996 has declined from 51,352 in 2013 and is concentrated mainly in the capital, Majuro, and the island of Ebeye. Citizens of FSM and RMI have visa-free access to live, work and study in the United States under the Compacts of Free Association (COFA), easing emigration. The resulting shortage of skilled labor creates dependencies on foreign experts and undermines domestic revenue generation and economic growth. 4. The countries’ labor markets are characterized by gender disparities, high levels of informality and reliance on public sector employment. Women are deeply under-represented in paid employment, more likely to be employed below their education levels and earn less than men. The youth of FSM (aged 15-24) have high NEET (Not in Education, Employment, or Training) and youth unemployment rates, while gender disparities are even stronger within this age group than the national averages2. In RMI, public sector jobs have grown steadily with the increase in sovereign rents since 2014 and now account for approximately 60 percent of jobs, whilst private sector job growth has been anemic. 5. RMI and FSM face significant economic challenges characterized by low growth rates, external vulnerabilities, and dependence on foreign aid. FSM is a lower middle-income country with a Gross National Income (GNI) of US$4,250 per capita in 2023 and RMI is an upper middle-income country with a GNI of US$7,860 per capita in 2023. Over the past 15 years, RMI's real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) has grown at an average rate of 1.8 percent per year, a modest pace by global standards and marked by significant volatility. FSM, however, faces even more pronounced economic challenges, with an average annual growth rate of just 0.6 percent over the same period. FSM’s economy grew 0.8 percent in 2023 and is estimated to have strengthened to 1.1 percent in 2024, driven by an increase in public wages and the resumption of infrastructure projects partly benefitting from new COFA with the United States. RMI’s economy fell modestly by 0.7 percent in 2023 but is estimated to have grown by 3.4 percent in 2024 due to strong fisheries activity and COFA funded construction activity. In 2025, both FSM and RMI are projected to grow more quickly at 1.7 and 4.0 percent respectively. Both countries remain highly vulnerable to external shocks, such as global commodity price fluctuations, labor shortages due to outmigration, and climate-related disasters. 6. RMI and FSM are focused on building human capital and strengthening governance. The National Strategic Plan 2020 – 2030 for RMI and FSM’s Strategic Development Plan 2025 – 2045 recognize that human capital is essential for ensuring sustainability of economic growth. RMI’s key policy objectives include: (i) enhanced skills and attributes necessary for a dynamic workforce and resilient economy; and (ii) enhanced capacity of public servants and employees of public agencies, including those in the outer islands. FSM’s desired outcomes include: (i) build a strong and diverse private sector workforce; and (ii) develop an efficient and effective public service workforce. These Plans emphasize resilience, infrastructure, and governance (public administration) and institutional strengthening to achieve sustainable economic growth, which are aligned to this project. Both countries are committed to strengthening transparency, efficiency and institutional performance as means to improve overall governance. 7. The activities under this project are consistent with the Strengthening of the Operating Model component of the World Bank Group (WBG) Evolution Roadmap, which aims to strengthen client support and capacity. The Project is providing support to RMI and FSM to build institutional and human capacity to achieve improved project portfolio quality, stronger results and greater impact. The activities will seek to: (i) strengthen and improve the efficiency and capacity of key ministries and agencies to prepare and implement projects; and (ii) support the enhancement of the technical skills of public sector workers and consultants responsible for World Bank funded operations and to attract, train and retain local talent. 1InternationalMonetary Fund Article IV, 2023; FSM FY22 Economic Statistics FSM FY22 Economic Statistics published by the US Graduate School in March 2024 is available at: https://pitiviti.org/fsm Page 3 The World Bank FSM and RMI: Implementation Capacity for Transformative Results (IMPACT) (P506422) Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. Capacity is a major constraint to effectively managing newly available resources. COFA agreements approved by the United States on March 9, 2024, will deliver a total of US$5.6 billion in assistance to the two countries over a 20- year period starting in FY24. FSM will receive US$3.3 billion and RMI US$2.3 billion. The WB portfolio has grown to eleven projects in RMI with a total commitment of US$298 million and ten projects in FSM with a total commitment of US$344.45 million. In addition, other development partners are also providing technical and financial resources. This increases the governments’ responsibility to use these resources efficiently and effectively through improved preparation and implementation of development projects. To achieve results, RMI and FSM must develop local human capacity to manage projects. In addition, strengthening institutional capacity and monitoring systems will lay a strong foundation for managing projects and support broader efforts to enhance governance. 9. There is a shortage of skilled workers to support development projects. There is a small pool of skilled workers available in the labor market, given the high migration and low level of education outcomes. Limited technical expertise and a high turnover of employees in public institutions are among the most frequent problems encountered across both countries. Capacity-building efforts are often undermined as staff with newly acquired skills and qualifications move to new roles in or outside the public service, including with other development partners. The response is to hire international consultants, but this leads to high transaction and overhead costs (given the remoteness and fragility of the countries), and challenges in supervising consultants’ performance. Remote islands have more challenges hiring international consultants, and many of whom do not complete their full contract term. Experience has shown that the reliance on international expertise has not built national capacity which is required for sustainable implementation of development projects. 10. The Governments have begun to address capacity constraints. The governments of RMI and FSM are acutely aware that the lack of capacity is limiting RMI’s and FSM’s growth. They have established scholarship programs for local and overseas tertiary education. The Marshall Islands Scholarship Grant and Loan Board Program and FSM National Scholarship and Financial Assistance Program raise and administer financial assistance for qualified applicants. There are also scholarships offered by bilateral partners including: Australia, China, Japan and New Zealand. Most scholarship recipients are required to return to their country of citizenship, after completion of their studies, to contribute to national development. There is no systematic linkage of scholarships and training programs with each country’s strategic workforce needs and permanent jobs in the recipients’ areas of studies are very limited.3 Therefore, most participants migrate to the United States for better opportunities after they complete their required work obligations. 11. The WB has a track record of capacity development activities to improve operational efficiency. The IDA18 scale- up and the compressed IDA19 period led to a faster than expected expansion of the WB-financed portfolio, especially in RMI, both in the number of projects and value of commitments. Given the unique context and capacity challenges faced by RMI and FSM, in 2019 the WB financed Central Implementation Units (CIUs) in the Ministry of Finance, Banking, and Postal Services (MOFBPS) of RMI, and Department of Finance and Administration (DOFA) in FSM.4 CIUs were also implemented in other capacity challenged countries in the Pacific (the Republic of Kiribati, Independent State of Samoa, Kingdom of Tonga, and Tuvalu) and in the Caribbean (Dominica and Sint Maarten). The CIUs respond to the need for a differentiated approach for faster and transformative impact in small island states. Shared services units offer a meaningful way to improve government capacity.5 The CIUs provide support and capacity building for pipeline delivery and the implementation of projects in the RMI and FSM portfolios. The objectives are to: (i) to strengthen institutional capacity, build systems and develop policies and processes; (ii) develop a cadre of local staff to operate these systems over the longer term; and (iii) enhance quality and timeliness in the preparation and implementation of development financed operations, especially WB-financed projects. CIUs comprise of local and international staff, under individual consultant contracts. These staff provide support in common function areas including procurement, financial management (FM), environmental and social (E&S) aspects, and monitoring and evaluation (M&E) - coordinated by a Project/Program Manager. PIUs typically have a technical Project Manager, a Project Officer, administrative staff and technical consultants. 13. The recent Joint Country Portfolio Performance Reviews (CPPRs) for RMI (December 5, 2024) and FSM (December 12, 2024) highlighted the impacts of the CIUs – and the challenges they face. Between 2022 and 2024 the RMI CIU, with majority local staff, doubled the disbursement rate (from 7 to 14 percent). The FSM CIU, with a majority of international staff, had a marginal increase in the disbursement rate over the same period (6 percent to 7 percent). The governments are committed to key actions to improve portfolio quality and results. However, there are challenges which undermine the CIUs’ effectiveness, including: (i) lack of clarity about the mandate of the CIU, which encompasses both implementation support and capacity building; (ii) inefficient policies, procedures and systems (e.g., the absence of standard operating procedures); (iii) a lack of clear performance metrics; and (iv) lack of incentives for capacity building (e.g., performance and compensation are not linked to capacity building outcomes). During the CPPR exercise the governments of RMI and FSM requested support from the WB to address these challenges. 14. The relationship between CIUs and PIUs requires streamlining to improve operational efficiency. Currently PIUs are responsible for project implementation including the preparation of FM and procurement documentation and work plans and budgets etc. CIUs provide final approvals for FM functions, oversight and advice to PIUs in common function areas and have a mandate to provide capacity building, though not formally implemented. PIUs typically do not have specialist consultants in common function areas such as E&S Officers, therefore relying on the CIU to support these functions. However, there exists a lack of clarity in roles and responsibilities of PIUs, CIUs and Implementing Agencies. For example, the overlapping division of responsibilities (Project Officer and CIU doing the same function). At times the division of responsibility between the finance and implementing agencies (ministries) with respect to the CIUs has been unclear, as well as the line between the strategic role required of the government and the hands-on implementation required of the CIUs. 15. The project will focus on transitioning the CIUs to more effective successor arrangements with sustainable local capacity to drive results. The CIU successor arrangements (CSAs) will resolve the existing ambiguities in institutional mandates and formalize increased capacity building support to WB portfolio PIUs. The institutional review to be conducted under the project will guide the structure and arrangements of the CSA and their role in project implementation. This will underpin a more focused approach to portfolio management and improve overall portfolio quality, delivering stronger results. The consolidation of CIU/CSA operational support and capacity building activities under this stand-alone IPF, with a designated results framework, is expected to increase accountability for the CIU/CSA performance. The project will strengthen the institutional and human resource capacity of the implementing agencies within the Governments of the RMI (GoRMI) and the FSM (GoFSM) to enhance operational efficiency and portfolio quality. This will be achieved by implementing innovative solutions to improve fiduciary, E&S, contract management, and M&E capacity and develop sustainable human resources. The project consists of three components: (i) strengthening institutional capacity; (ii) enhancing human capacity; and (iii) project management. Component 1. Strengthening Institutional Capacity (Approximately US$12 million) 22. This component will strengthen and improve the efficiency and institutional capacity of key ministries and agencies to prepare and implement projects. The activities include: (i) an institutional review to establish a CIU successor arrangement (CSA) and strengthen the governance framework; (ii) implementation of the recommendations from the institutional review including the development of human resource (HR) and labor frameworks for the PIUs and CSAs; (iii) the development of streamlined processes to increase efficiency (e.g., SOPs); (iv) investments in emerging technologies for innovative solutions to improve operational efficiency and monitoring through Geo-Enabling Initiative for Monitoring and Supervision (GEMS) and other approaches; and (vi) financing the operations of the CIUs and CSAs including technical surge support through the transition period. The institutional review will include: (i) mapping the roles, responsibilities, and relationships of ministries, agencies, and units involved in the preparation and implementation of World Bank projects; (ii) assessing the demand for WB operations and project management support for development partners and government-financed projects; (iii) identifying the alternative institutional arrangements to improve portfolio delivery; (iv) developing a competency framework to benchmark the minimum personnel and skill sets required for CSAs and PIUs; (v) developing a harmonized contract framework for the CSA consultants (national and international) including rates and other benefits; (vi) developing a performance management framework for CSA consultants; and (vii) preparing a change management and communication strategy for the recommended changes. Gender disaggregated data will be captured in the review, and recommendations will be designed to ensure female personnel are not disadvantaged. The institutional review will be conducted in Year one of the project. 24. The recommendations arising from the review will be implemented along with the other activities. This includes the development of (i) a labor framework including job descriptions, (ii) compensation structure, and (iii) performance assessment tools to attract and retain talent. Human resource (HR) policies will be designed to promote gender parity. Processes and procedures will be streamlined to increase operational efficiency and reduce the administrative burden of the CSAs and PIUs. SOPs will guide the governance and authorizing environment of the respective CSAs. Toolkits, guidance notes, and templates for Project Operations Manuals, procurement plans, and project progress reports will be developed to improve project management. Innovative ICT solutions for monitoring and evaluation (M&E), real-time risk management, and portfolio mapping including investments to facilitate climate budget tagging and monitoring,6 such as GEMS, will be identified and implemented. Implementing the review findings and the governments’ longer-term vision to build capacity of national staff will require flexibility and adaptability and will be supported with specialist consultants in the common function areas to provide additional technical surge support. The specialists will provide specific and targeted support on an identified challenge or issue that is not able to be resolved with existing CSA or PIU capacity (for example, high-risk and complicated E&S issues, challenging procurement packages). Component 2: Enhancing Human Capacity (Approximately US$2 million) 25. This component will support the enhancement of the technical and professional skills of personnel responsible for World Bank operations and undertake measures to attract, train, and retain local talent. The focus is to identify and build a pool of skilled local talent to support the implementation of development projects and to implement incentive schemes to retain local workers. The key activities to be financed are to: (i) undertake a capacity and skills assessment of CSA and PIU consultants, informed by the review findings in Component 1; (ii) develop and implement training plans for CSA and PIU consultants (that is, attendance at courses, conferences, and relevant trainings, and hiring of specialist trainers if required); (iii) develop and implement a formal knowledge transfer program among CSAs, PIUs, and implementing agency staff; (v) undertake a labor market study and develop recommendations for labor contracts and incentive schemes for attracting and retaining talent; (vi) support courses focusing on E&S, M&E, and project management in partnership with educational institutions; (vii) support internship programs and mentorship consultancies; and (viii) develop materials and conduct awareness campaigns in high schools and colleges to support and promote career pathways within RMI and FSM. 26. Internships will be offered to recent graduates, particularly those that attain relevant certification through the planned college partnerships. To complement the internship program, consultants will be hired to provide on-the-job training in-country for a minimum number of weeks per year, while providing remote mentoring support when not on- island. The dual modality of the consultancy is intended to attract high-calibre international candidates. A stipend manual will be developed to detail the implementation arrangements for the provision of stipends to interns under the internship program, including the selection and enrolment of interns, responsibilities and expectations and payment arrangements. The preparation and adoption of the stipend manual will be reflected as a condition of disbursement in the legal agreements. The capacity building activities, particularly the internship program, are expected to transition participants to national employment. High school and college awareness programs will be led by the Division of International Development Assistance (DIDA) in the MoFBPS/DOFA, supported by a consultancy, to raise awareness on career pathways on-island and to attract graduates to the countries. It is envisioned that while this will have a public sector career focus, the private sector will also be invited to showcase opportunities for graduating students. 30. Creating business opportunities for local firms. In addition, budget permitting, the CSA will be strengthened to conduct outreach to promote greater participation of local firms in WB procurement activities. The objective of the project is to strengthen institutional capacity and enhance technical skills in project management of development finance operations in RMI and FSM. This will be achieved by amplifying innovative solutions to improve fiduciary, E&S, contract management and M&E capacity and develop sustainable human resources. The project activities are all institutional and capacity strengthening activities and do not support any civil works, nor any feasibility studies/technical design for future physical infrastructure. Therefore, the project is not expected to have any direct impact on natural habitats or cultural heritage sites, nor support any activity that leads to involuntary resettlement. Key risks relate to labor management of project workers including government staff and workers engaged as interns, as well as stakeholder engagement which will be managed via the inclusion of requirements in the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP). This will include the requirement to operate an accessible grievance redress mechanism for project-related grievances. The ESCP will require that environmental and social considerations are integrated into terms of reference for all project activities. To implement the project, DOFA and DIDA will each house a project PIU staffed by existing CIU consultants for cost efficiency and to facilitate the early initiation of project activities. The current CIU consultants have the required skills and experience to effectively implement the project and will serve as the IMPACT PIU for the interim period. The project will benefit from a simplified design and the strengthening of institutional capacity. The IMPACT PIUs will be under the direction of the Assistant Secretary DOFA and Assistant Secretary DIDA respectively, and include a Program Manager, a Procurement Officer, FM Officer(s) and an Environmental or Social Officer to support project implementation, including coordination with relevant national and state government institutions where applicable. The IMPACT PIUs will oversee the day-to-day implementation, monitoring, and reporting of project activities, carrying out financial management and procurement of goods and consultancy activities under the project with support from the other consultants of the CIUs, and monitoring and reporting on results achieved by activities financed under the project. The CIUs and additional technical support under this project, using remote-based consultants, will perform surge implementation support related to procurement, financial management, communications, and M&E on the project during the transition period. DOFA and DIDA will prepare Project Operations Manuals (POM) which will set out the institutional and implementation arrangements for day-to-day project execution. 25. A Project Steering Committee (PSC) will be maintained to enhance project coordination, provide strategic leadership and governance oversight to deliver results. The PSC will comprise the Secretary of Finance of MoFBPS, Secretary of DOFA, The Project Manager/Program Manager of the CIU or CSA and the Assistant Secretaries of the WB project implementing agencies or their designate. The PSC is expected to meet quarterly and discuss WB financed project portfolio progress, approve the CIU/CSA annual work plan and resolve portfolio issues. 26. To facilitate efficiencies in economics, scale and lessons sharing, a virtual knowledge exchange forum will be established between RMI and FSM CIUs/CSAs.
