Page vi of vi The World Bank Governance Modernization to Enable Efficient Service Delivery Project (P178808) VIII. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Macroeconomic and financing pressures have increased significantly in recent years although growth remains high. Ethiopia’s state-led and public-investment-intensive development model supported growth rates of nearly 10 percent between 2004 and 2018, among the world’s highest, and drove significant gains in poverty reduction. Despite these achievements, there were negligible improvements in structural transformation even as macroeconomic pressures mounted as reflected in growing macroeconomic imbalances, debt vulnerabilities, fiscal constraints, depleted liquidity buffers, and foreign-exchange shortages, further exacerbated by internal conflict, the COVID-19 pandemic, and drought. Growing fiscal pressures, coupled with a fragmented approach to public institutional reforms are root causes that require urgent attention. 2. Despite remarkable progress in reducing poverty and improving human development, challenges remain. The share of the population living in extreme poverty decreased between 2005 and 2016 from 37 percent to 23.5 percent.1 Human development outcomes have improved significantly. Between 2011 and 2021, the gross primary school enrollment rate increased from 94 percent to 106 percent2; under-five mortality decreased from 88 per 1,000 live births to 59; and average life expectancy has increased by about one year annually since 2000 and is now higher than the averages for both Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and low-income countries worldwide.3 These gains notwithstanding, Ethiopia ranked in the lowest decile of the 174 countries included in the Human Capital Index globally in 2020, with an annual per capita income of US$890 and Human Capital Index score of 0.38. Progress towards reducing gender inequality, similarly, has been slow, with Ethiopia ranking 129 out of 170 assessed countries in the UN’s Gender Inequality Index. 3. Climate change impacts are significant, will increase over time, and may turn Ethiopia’s demographic dividend into a demographic liability. The recently published Country Climate and Development Report for Ethiopia indicates that under current policies, average annual losses to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and household consumption will range between 1-1.5 percent annually between 2024-30 and could reach as high as 5 percent in the 2040s. With about 115 million people, 44 percent of which are under the age of 15, Ethiopia is well placed to reap a high demographic dividend. Its economic dependence on sectors directly impacted by climate change – agriculture, water, tourism, and forestry, coupled with an increase in the magnitude, frequency, and intensity of droughts and floods since the 1970s,4 however, create precarious conditions for Ethiopia’s youth. 4. The Government of Ethiopia (GoE) recognizes these challenges, and has laid out an ambitious reform agenda. Civil Service Performance 6. The growth in the size of the civil service has not been accompanied by commensurate investments in managing performance and in developing modern capabilities. The civil service has grown from 150,000 in 1991 to close to 2.5 million today, with large and necessary increases in teachers and medical workers, who account for nearly half of the public sector workforce. The public sector wage bill as a share of GDP has increased between 2000 and 2020 from 5.3 percent to 6.6 percent of GDP, and doubled as a share of public expenditure from 21 to 46 percent. 5 The draft Civil Service Policy and accompanying action plan identifies key structural constraints and pathways for reform. 7. Transitioning to an evidence-based and data-informed approach to Human Resource Management (HRM) in Ethiopia is constrained by the lack of effective investment in Human Resource systems. Decisions on recruitment, performance management, training, and capacity building require up-to-date information on workforce composition, location, and skills deficits. Human Resource (HR) systems are fragmented and currently capture data on only a limited number of civil servants, lack interoperability, and have a limited number of data fields. This system fragmentation is driven by poor digital connectivity of service delivery facilities, the lack of clear governance arrangements, and the inability of institutions to drive compliance. With World Bank support through the Digital Foundations Project (P171034), the GoE is addressing issues regarding digital connectivity and infrastructure, but critical governance and capability issues remain unaddressed. 8. The absence of data on civil servants at federal and sub-national levels impacts the ability of the state to promote critical issues such as gender equity . The need for gender disaggregated data in the civil service is crucial for informed decision-making and the development of effective policies that address gender disparities. The GoE does not currently have data that helps identify levels at which gender imbalances exist, to enable targeted interventions to promote equity. Developing gender disaggregated data will support in monitoring progress over time, allowing institutions to track the impact of initiatives aimed at promoting gender equality. Without such data, it becomes challenging to accurately assess the extent of gender disparities, hindering efforts to implement evidence-based policies and practices that foster an inclusive and equitable work environment within the civil service. Therefore, the systematic collection and analysis of gender disaggregated data are essential tools for achieving genuine gender equality in the public sector. 9. A 2022 World Bank survey of civil servants found that only half of federal civil servants were recruited through examination and interview. Federal civil servants also self-reported significant concerns regarding the efficacy of Balanced Scorecard reforms introduced a decade prior, given perceptions of implementation bias and minimal repercussions for poor performance. World Bank surveys pre-COVID in 2019 and post-outbreak in 2022 consistently show low levels of civil servant motivation, driven by low salary satisfaction, inadequate resources, the lack of professional development opportunities, and low prestige of the civil service. 10. The current approach to civil servant training is fragmented and inadequate. Ethiopia has several public sector training institutions that provide often overlapping training of varied quality. These include the Civil Service University that provides degree (bachelors and masters) training to civil servants, the Ethiopia Management Institute (EMI) that provides short-term training and consultancy services, and the Africa Leadership Excellence Academy that provides short-term training to senior civil servants and political appointees. As competency frameworks are not in place, training offered is not clearly linked with organizational needs and are often compliance and procedure-oriented and do not respond to emerging organizational requirements. New competencies are needed to meet new challenges. For example, mainstreaming global priority issues such as climate change or gender in the way business processes are conducted is critical across government. However, there is very limited training and exposure provided on such issues. Domestic Revenue Mobilization 11. Ethiopia’s tax effort has not kept pace with its economic growth . While the average real GDP growth rate between 2011-19 was an impressive 9.5 percent, with over 6 percent growth each year6 in the last three years, the tax-to-GDP ratio dropped from 12 percent over the decade ending in 2016/17 7 to 6.8 percent in 2023.8 The government has an ambitious plan to increase revenue by an average of 26.1 percent per year for each year till 2030, to achieve a tax-GDP ratio of 18.2 percent. 9 Strong reform measures will need to be taken to meet this goal. Building on the HERA and the TYDP, the Tax Transformation Roadmap and the National Medium Term Revenue Strategy list a comprehensive reform agenda that includes expanding and rationalizing the tax base and prioritizing the overhaul of Value-Added-Tax (VAT) and excise laws; implementing tax policy reforms that support economic development and fairness; and improving and modernizing the tax administration through improvements in taxpayer services and education, voluntary compliance, and building strong data and analytics capabilities that support risk management. Tax administration reforms are founded on deploying digital technology, and developing a workforce focused on customer service and performance. The transformation is needed at both the federal and regional levels. 12. Citizens face major challenges in complying with the tax system. Enhancements to the Information and Communications Technology (ICT) platforms are urgently needed to improve returns from past investments and improve tax collections. While a Standard Integrated Government Tax Administration System (SIGTAS) exists, an ICT diagnostic10 in the Ministry of Revenue (MoR) in 2018 identified a number of challenges, such as incomplete transition to a full self-assessment system and an inadequate national payments system and software and network issues. More fundamentally, it identified the inability of MoR to implement significant and domestic reforms through its current structures and recommended a large-scale evolution. The report finds wide misuse of SIGTAS, potential tampering of taxpayer data by ICT staff, inadequate infrastructure, and serious security concerns. The report points to two active risks that might cause catastrophic operational and reputational damage to MoR, namely physical security of infrastructure and the lack of a disaster recovery center. The MoR has established a Tax Transformation Office, which has been tasked with identifying and implementing solutions to address these challenges. 14. Policy and compliance gaps significantly impact tax collection. Closing the tax-GDP ratio will require revising tax expenditures and exemptions, reforming policy and compliance gaps in VAT, Excise, Customs, and personal income tax. Reforms in customs, VAT, excise and Personal Income Tax administration that focuses on improving compliance could potentially achieve an increase of 6-7 percentage points in the tax-GDP ratio in the short- to medium-term. With a policy gap of about 2.4 percent and a compliance gap estimated at 4.1 percent of GDP11, there is enormous potential to increase VAT collections. VAT collections are only two percent of GDP compared to an average of over four percent of GDP in SSA, and about five percent of GDP in Low Income Countries (LICs). Excise revenues represented about only 0.73 percent of GDP which is much lower than the average of 1.4 percent of GDP in the SSA region12. There are several policy issues that have a negative bearing on the tax collections which are being addressed through a proposed Ethiopia First Sustainable and Inclusive Growth Development Policy Operation (P181591).13 15. Public Financial Management 17. Constraints in Public Financial Management (PFM) threaten aggregate fiscal discipline and contribute to the misallocation of resources and ineffective service delivery. Over 29 of the 44 dimensions assessed in the 2019 Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) were scored as either ‘basic’ or ‘below basic’. The GoE’s newly adopted PFM Reform Strategy proposes an array of reform entry-points across the PFM-cycle, and importantly improves on the prior strategy by recognizing the importance of extending PFM reforms beyond the Ministry of Finance (MoF), both horizontally within the Federal tier of government and vertically in recognition of the highly decentralized approach to service delivery. 18. The adoption of core PFM systems is contributing to improved data availability, but system utilization and adoption for decision-making remains low. Through the recently concluded Ethiopia PFM Project (P150922), the newly adopted Integrated Financial Management Information System (IFMIS) is currently used to manage 78 percent of the federal budget. A migration plan is in place for remaining federal entities, as well as to roll-out the system to regional levels as envisioned in the GoE’s IFMIS Reform Strateg ic Plan (2021-2025). As identified in the IFMIS Effectiveness Review (2019), however, the prior focus on technological aspects of the roll-out has meant that analog complements have not been sufficiently addressed. The World Bank has also supported the development and piloting of an e-Government procurement system (e-GP) to address some of these issues. However, as identified during consultations with the Federal Public Procurement and Property Agency (FPPPA), significant investment is required to support the full roll-out and utilization of the system. 19. Strengthening PFM processes and integrating climate change considerations into the PFM cycle in line with the GoE’s Nationally Determined Contributions are core challenges identified in the PFM Strategy. The 2021 PEFA Climate Assessment concluded that the country’s overall performance in integrating climate change considerations in PFM is still weak and not aligned with international standards. Despite the significant increase in own-source financed climate-related spending, total expenditure on climate change adaptation and mitigation-related items accounted for less than 0.04 percent of GDP in 2020, compared to spending on fossil fuel subsidies which accounted for nearly 1.5 percent of GDP in the same year. In addition, tracking of climate-related expenditure in Ethiopia is problematic given the absence of appropriate classification and tagging in both the budget preparation and outturn reporting processes. This makes it challenging to ascertain the levels and appropriateness of the government’s fiscal strategy with regards to addressing climate adaptation and mitigation considerations in GoE’s PFM Strategy including in areas such as budgeting, public expenditure tracking, public investment management, procurement, asset management, debt management and tax policy and administration. Given the numerous PFM capabilities rated as basic or below basic in Page 6 of 41 The World Bank Governance Modernization to Enable Efficient Service Delivery Project (P178808) the 2019 PEFA assessment, efforts to integrate climate change considerations into the PFM cycle are unlikely to be successful without continued support to the GoE to holistically improve its systems, processes, and approach to capacity building. This holistic, transversal approach along the PFM cycle will also be critical in helping to address the gender gap in PFM systems in Ethiopia, which have not gone beyond the promulgation of gender-responsive budgeting and reporting manuals. 20. The procurement function does not support the GoE to achieve value for money in its investments. A recent assessment - the Methodology for Assessing Procurement Systems (MAPS) identified significant weaknesses. The regulatory framework needs to better address issues of green procurement, embed transparency, incorporate sustainability factors, and establish mechanisms for market assessments to inform procurement. A procurement law has been approved by Parliament, which seeks to fill some of these gaps. Regulatory functions in procurement are fragmented, resulting in conflicts of interest and diffusion of responsibility. While public bodies have identified procurement positions, the job requirements for these are still generically defined and not based on competencies. Procurement records are often incomplete and inaccessible, undermining transparency. While the Electronic Government Procurement (e-GP) system is contributing towards improved data transparency in public procurement, investments in professionalization and capability development will be critical for addressing MAPS deficiencies. 21. Developing citizen trust and investor confidence in government systems will require significant improvements in public participation and fiscal transparency mechanisms. Citizens’ participation is one of the key rights provided by the Ethiopian Constitution and although policy frameworks exist to support key interventions, the GoE significantly underperforms against regional peers and the SSA average for transparency in PFM operations. In the 2021 Open Budget Survey (OBS), Ethiopia ranked 110 out of 120 participating countries with a score of eight; less than a quarter of the average score of all SSA countries that participated. Improving Ethiopia’s performance along the transparency dimension of the OBS will require articulating institutional arrangements to deliver key budgetary and fiscal outcome documentation and developing staff capacity to produce these. Addressing shortcomings in public participation will require the MoF and the Office of the Federal Auditor General (OFAG) to consider piloting mechanisms to engage the public, including women, vulnerable and underrepresented communities, to engage actively across the budget cycle. 22. The GoE’s ability to effectively monitor and report on public operations is further undermined by a weak accountancy profession and the limited adoption of international standards. Ethiopia has established a legislative framework for financial reporting, which mandates the use of International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) and International Public Sector Accounting Standards (IPSAS) and has established the Accounting and Auditing Board of Ethiopia (AABE) as a regulatory body. Challenges to operationalizing these standards include: the inability to recruit and retain qualified staff due to salary structures; the limited number of qualified professional chartered accountants in the country; the lack of a Professional Accountancy Organization (PAO) in-country; and the lack of understanding and awareness of the roles and responsibilities of AABE. Near universal delays ranging in the submission of annual financial reports by SoEs undermines the GoE’s ability to undertake fiscal risk reporting, implement effective liability management planning, and fulfill its regulatory function. These challenges and priorities are laid out in Ethiopia’s 2024 Home Grown Economic Reform 2.0 (HGER 2.0) program and form the basis of World Bank support for governance reforms. The GoE recognizes that urgent investment is needed in people, processes, and systems, both at the center-of-government and at regional levels, to achieve its ambitious inclusive growth and service delivery agenda. The GoE has sought World Bank support through a financing operation to address critical gaps in three core areas: public service reform, domestic revenue mobilization, and public financial management and fiscal governance. The cross-cutting and foundational investments suggested in the draft HGER2 and supported by the Project aims to build individual and institutional capabilities and positively impact both the central and sub-national level. Ethiopia’s Constitution provides significant autonomy to regions. Civil Service Management and Public Financial Management are delegated responsibilities with differentiated management and reporting systems in some cases. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 24. The project is aligned with the World Bank Group Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Ethiopia for 2018- 2022 (Report No. 119576-ET). The CPF emphasized the importance of investing in the country’s institutional capacity at all levels of government, and on developing citizen-state trust through greater accountability and transparency in government institutions. The project directly supports Focus Area 3 Objective 3.1 – ‘increased capacity and improved governance in service delivery’ – and Objective 3.2 – ‘strengthened citizen engagement and holding government entities accountable’. The project is aligned with the forthcoming World Bank Country Partnership Framework for Ethiopia (FY25-30). The project also supports the World Bank’s regional priority of making institutions more efficient and accountable by focusing support to build capabilities for public management of service delivery. It is also strongly aligned with Fragility, Conflict and Violence (FCV) Strategy commitments to remain engaged during conflicts and crisis situations and help countries transition out of fragility. Similarly, the project is fully aligned with the IDA20 focus on governance and institutions, given its emphasis on building more resilient and accountable institutions. 25. In terms of Paris Alignment, the project is aligned with Ethiopia’s 2021 Updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC),15 which outlines the need for support on capacity building and technology transfer in critical areas related to climate change. The project contributes to the NDC and will support the Government’s climate mitigation objectives through its cross-cutting theme of transitioning from paper-based to digital processes across core government functions and citizen-facing service domains. It also indirectly supports mitigation and adaptation priorities through the integration of climate change priorities identified in the PFM Strategy into Component 3, and through the potential initial focus on the Ministries of Agriculture and Health under Component 1. The project also aligns with capacity building objectives in the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change process at systemic, institutional, and individual levels.
