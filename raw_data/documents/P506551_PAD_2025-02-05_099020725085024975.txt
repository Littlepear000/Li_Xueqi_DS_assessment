STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. While Tunisia’s economy has remained resilient, the country is navigating a delicate economic situation with persistently high levels of unemployment. Tunisia is one of the most diversified economies in Africa, with a historically large services sector that has grown over the past decade.1 In 2023, significant export gains came from hotels and restaurants (+12.8 percent) thanks to the recovery of tourism, and from the mechanical and electric sectors (+5.8 percent).2 Export growth, driven by complexity products like electrical machinery and medical apparatuses, has outpaced overall economic growth in recent years, becoming crucial to the economy.3 However, severe drought, tight financing conditions, uncertain external financing conditions, low domestic and external demand, and pace of reform implementation slowed economic growth in 2023 and the first half of 2024, leaving the economy below its pre-Covid level compared to other countries in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. Compounding these challenges, since 2011, Tunisia has increasingly relied on recurrent public expenditures to address citizens' immediate needs, resulting in widening fiscal and current account deficits4. While the current account has recently improved, authorities have relied more on domestic borrowing to finance Budget in recent years. This has also contributed to a rising debt burden, which reached 79.8 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 2023. As of Spring 2024, the budget remains under pressure with limited growth affecting tax revenues.5 2. Despite significant investments in human capital, the country has struggled with high unemployment rates in recent years6 (16 percent in Q3 2024), particularly among youth (about 40.5 percent) and women (over 22.1 percent)7. The persistently high youth unemployment disproportionately affects those with advanced levels of education. On the supply side, structural issues include graduates’ preference for public sector positions (which offer benefits and about 40 percent higher wages8), skills mismatches (including shortcomings in terms of soft skills), and aversion to informality.9 On the demand side, the public sector is saturated, and the private sector suffers from low productivity and competitiveness, which coupled with restrictive product market regulations and state dominance in certain industries, hinder the creation of high-quality jobs. Institutional barriers, such as labor market regulations and limited access to finance for businesses, further constrain private-sector growth.10 3. In the coming decades, Tunisia, like other countries in the MENA region, will increasingly face the impact of climate change, digitalization and population aging. While these megatrends are not new, they will accelerate significantly within the century. To withstand their potential threats and capitalize on associated opportunities, the Tunisian economy requires a structural transformation, including through changes in its academic and scientific research sectors. (a) Climate change is causing more frequent and severe weather events, rising sea levels, and ecological disruptions, posing grave threats to ecosystems, communities, and economies. Agriculture and tourism, key contributors to Tunisia's economy, are highly susceptible to climate impacts. By 2050, yields for olives, soft wheat, and barley are expected to drop by 69 percent, 35 percent , and 41 percent , respectively, due to water shortages and temperature changes. The tourism sector, heavily dependent on coastal assets, faces threats from rising sea levels. Without urgent measures, Tunisia's economy could shrink by 3.4 percent of GDP by 2030, equivalent to TND 5.6 billion or US$ 1.8 billion annually. This heightened exposure exacerbates the country’s economic challenges, making climate resilience and the green transition critical to its future economic development. Proactive environmental strategies and decarbonizing the electricity sector could increase GDP by 9 percent , reduce poverty by 12 percent , and cut energy sector carbon emissions by 80 percent by 2030. Tunisia needs to build capacity through tertiary education to diversify livelihoods, especially for the rural population. Extreme weather also threatens education by creating non-conducive learning environments, increasing school closures, and causing economic shocks and food insecurity, leading to student dropouts and lower learning outcomes (b) The continuous emergence of digital technologies is transforming lifestyles, jobs, and social interactions. Tunisia aims to drive economic growth and job creation by embracing technologies like Artificial Intelligence (AI). The government’s strategies, including Tunisia Digital 2020, SmartGov 2020, Digital Strategy 2025, and the National AI Strategy, place technology at the core of sustainable development. Based on the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) World Telecommunication ICT indicators database, as of 2021, Tunisia leverages its 80 percent internet penetration by investing in AI to build a tech-savvy future. Initiatives include the Tunis Higher Institute of Digital Engineering for advanced training and Elghazala Technopark for fostering collaboration between academia, industry, and startups. Digital platforms enhance education, identify at-risk students, and improve learning. AI is also used in agriculture and tourism to optimize resources and services (c) Simultaneously, longer life expectancies and declining birth rates are leading to significant demographic shifts and challenging healthcare, pension systems and workforce dynamics.11 Tunisia has the highest share of individuals aged 65 and older in MENA– underscoring the need for an adequate healthcare workforce. From 1950 to 2020, Tunisia's population grew continuously, from less than 4 million to nearly 12 million. Based on the United Nations median scenario, the population is expected to continue growing until it peaks at 14 million between 2050 and 2065, before declining to nearly 13 million by 2100. The share of the population above 65 years old will see the fastest increase in the region in the coming decades12 – rising from just over 1 million to nearly 3 million by 2050 (22 percent of the population) and almost 4 million by 2100 (31 percent).13 A clear strategic plan is needed to improve elderly services, including adequate training of multidisciplinary teams. 4. Economic challenges and the new megatrends are affecting Tunisians unequally. Inequality, as measured by the Gini Index, increased from 32.8 to 33.7 between 2015 and 2021, mainly driven by disparities within urban and rural areas and between regions. Large disparities persist between the interior regions, where official poverty ranges between 22.5 percent (North-West region) and 37 percent (Center-West region) and coastal regions of Grand Tunis (4.7 percent), North- East (15.2 percent) and Center-East (13.2 percent).14 Furthermore, low-income households are particularly exposed to natural hazards and climate change due to limited access to resources and capacity to cope with weather shocks, as well as increased physical vulnerability due to densely populated and substandard quality housing. Water scarcity is also likely to impact the rural poor disproportionately, as they are more likely to rely on agriculture, which is highly vulnerable to climate-related shocks like droughts. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. The strategic vision for higher education and scientific research for 2035 aims to cultivate responsible and competent students and direct scientific research to support the future economy. To this end, the strategy of the Ministry of Higher Education and Scientific Research (MOHESR) revolves around the following three axes: (i) Strengthening qualified and responsible human capital to achieve comprehensive development; (ii) Research and innovation aligned with the needs of the economy and society; and (iii) A student life that fosters students' personal development and creativity. The strategy also highlights the following levers: (a) Global digital transformation; (b) Flexible and effective governance that meets system development requirements; (c) Integrated systematic reform of the legal framework; and (d) Efficient and effective use of financial and human resources. 6. The Tunisian higher education system uses the LMD Licence (undergraduate degree), Master, and Doctorate (LMD) structure. All programs adhere to this structure, except for the bachelor, engineering, medicine, and architecture programs. As of 2023 according to MOHESR data, most students are enrolled in Licence programs (64 percent), followed by Master’s programs (16 percent), engineering schools (5 percent), preparatory cycles (5 percent), doctorate programs (4 percent), medical schools (4 percent), architecture programs (1 percent), and other degrees (2 percent). Tunisia’s Higher Education system comprises a total of 13 public universities, including the Virtual University of Tunis; 80 private establishments; and 206 public tertiary institutions, including 25 high institutes of technological studies (Instituts Supérieurs des Etudes Technologiques (ISETs)). 7. Tunisia’s tertiary enrolment is on par with the MENA regional average and is expected to increase in the coming decade. Data from MOHESR shows that as of 2022, the tertiary gross enrolment ratio in Tunisia is close to 40 percent, closely mirroring the 41 percent ratio in MENA (World Bank World Development Indicators). The number of students enrolled in tertiary education more than tripled between 1995 and 2005, increasing from 102,000 to 330,000 students, due to the massive expansion of secondary education and an increase in school-aged population. However, the following decade witnessed a decline, with enrollments decreasing from 370,058 students in 2010 to 270,430 in 2020/2021, largely due to declining demographic patterns. Latest data from MOHESR shows that recent years have seen a modest rebound with enrollments reaching 315,184 in 2023/2024. Projections from the National Institute of Statistics (INS in French) suggest that this pattern will continue for the next five years, with student numbers expected to experience exponential growth and reach an unprecedented peak in the second half of the next decade.15 Latest data from MOHESR shows that while the majority of students (266,837 in 2023/2024) attend public institutions, the share of students enrolled in private tertiary education has rapidly increased in recent years, climbing from 1 percent in 2006, to 11.5 percent in 2017/18 and 15.3 percent in 2023/24. Several factors contribute to the increase in private sector enrolment, including an increased number of high school graduates; the higher selectivity of public programs; the expansion, diversification and quality improvement of private universities, with some obtaining internationally accredited courses in engineering; and their partnerships and international cooperation with some private universities offering attractive mobility programs. 8. Women make up the majority of students and faculty members. As of 2023, as per MOHESR data, women represent two-thirds (66 percent) of higher education students, with a gross enrolment ratio of 48 percent, surpassing the MENA region’s average of 43 percent. Women are also over-represented among graduates, particularly in Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM) fields where they account for 58 percent of graduates. The country made considerable strides in enhancing higher education quality in recent years, as evidenced by improved international accreditations, ISO-certifications and international rankings. 27 establishments (16 of them public) and 88 programs (54 of them public) have been accredited. In particular, 55 engineering programs are internationally accredited16 (a third of engineering programs eligible for accreditation), alongside 4 faculties of medicine. Further, 40 institutions (19 percent of eligible institutions) received an ISO certification.17 Eight of the thirteen public Tunisian universities were listed in the 2025 Times Higher Education ranking, and several are ranked in a range of subjects – including seven in economics and business, eight in IT and four in medicine and engineering. As an example, the University of Tunis El Manar ranked among the top 1000 universities in the world in the Times Higher Education 2025 ranking, and rose from 976th place in the Quacquarelli Symonds (QS) World University Ranking in 2024 to 776th place in 2025. University of Tunis El Manar is also ranked in the 2024 Shanghai ranking. The World Bank financed Tertiary Education for Employability Project (TEEP) (P151059, closed in 2024), also known as PromESsE (Projet de Modernisation de l’Enseignement Supérieur en Soutien à l’Employabilité), significantly contributed to the international accreditations, ISO certifications and improved performance of Tunisian universities enabling higher international ranking. Since 2022, the Tunisian Agency for Accreditation and Evaluation (Agence Nationale de l‘Evaluation et de l‘Accréditation - ATEA) has been responsible for implementing a national accreditation process for both public and private programs and institutions. 10. With the support of TEEP, the country also made significant progress in conducting graduate tracer surveys for better employment information. These surveys were led by university observatories in coordination with the Office of Studies, Planning, and Programming (BEPP, Bureau des études, de la planification et de la programmation) at the MOHESR. The last survey took place in 2022/2023 and was implemented by 11 universities and the General Directorate of Technological Studies (DGET). These surveys serve as a decision-making tool and as a reliable data source on important aspects such young graduates’ employability, voluntary and involuntary unemployment, causes of non-integration, and the attractiveness and saturation of the various economic sectors. However, consolidated analysis at both the university and the system level is limited given that the survey is not implemented by all higher education institutions (HEIs) and the instrument is not harmonized. Furthermore, there is no central database on graduates, and capacity for processing, analyzing and publishing the results is limited at the BEPP and in some university observatories. 11. Tunisia has shown a strong commitment to fostering equity in higher education, addressing disparities at the regional level and among students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds. The country made a concerted effort towards regional equity by opening new universities and HEIs in interior regions, and towards socioeconomic equity through scholarships and subsidized university housing and restaurants. Remarkably, approximately 40 percent of students consistently received scholarships and 25 percent consistently received housing from 2017 to 2023. The MOHESR also invested in the quality of services provided, with the Offices of Students Affairs receiving TND 14.97 million (approximately US$4.8 million) under TEEP to finance sports fields and living spaces in university residences, amenities for student life, and improved quality of catering services as confirmed by food safety management certifications of several university restaurants in accordance with ISO guidelines. 12. Nonetheless, significant disparities persist across students from different regions, income levels, gender and abilities. Based on the World Bank World Development Indicators database, parity indices18 still show that rural students are 40 percent less likely to be enrolled than urban students (parity index of 0.4 in terms of gross enrolment). The inequality is even more stark along income levels (parity index of 0.16). Most Tunisians who start higher education complete it, but a high number of them repeat at least one grade. As per MOHESR data, 2.4 percent of students (5,000 students) dropped out of higher education in 2022/2023 – a rate consistent with the trend observed since 2010 and considerably lower than the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) average of 31 percent. However, almost one in four (22 percent) students repeat at least one grade. The share of repeaters at the undergraduate level has remained roughly constant over the past decade at approximately 25 percent, but rose above 30 percent in 2012-2014. On average, only 57 percent of students pass the first year of the license, which could partly reflect the low learning outcomes of students coming from secondary school. The UNICEF MICS results show that over a third (36 percent) of children aged 7-14 years old lack basic reading skills and over two thirds (68 percent) lack fundamental mathematical skills. Deteriorating education quality in primary and secondary grades could exacerbate student difficulties in higher education. 14. While the quality and relevance of higher education in Tunisia has improved, aligning higher education with highly productive sectors, and thereby improving the employability of graduates, is still a challenge. INS indicators show that as of the third trimester of 2024, youth unemployment remains particularly high among university graduates, reaching 17 percent among men and 31.6 percent among women.22 Transition from school to first employment takes a median of 29 months, and up to 42 months among women.23 This is partly due to graduates not being adequately equipped with the technical and soft skills needed to successfully transition to the labor market, and for women, to the difficulty of finding a job aligned with their personal preferences in terms of work-life balance and working hours, among others. About 35 percent of Tunisian firms cited an ‘inadequately educated workforce’ as a significant constraint, the highest in MENA, where the average is 20.4 percent.24 The perception of inadequacy among employers is primarily due to a deficit in essential cross-cutting skills such as work ethics, social skills and problem-solving abilities.25 On the other hand, graduates are affected by insufficient labor demand and limited absorption capacity, which stems from a small formal economy and weak private sector growth. This results in graduates mainly seeking employment in the public sector or in limited private sector positions. 15. Tunisia spends a considerable amount of its budget on higher education and scientific research, but as in similar countries, most of it covers salary expenditures. Based on MOHESR and World Bank data, the government spends 4.5 percent of its budget on higher education and scientific research (corresponding to 1.4 percent of GDP), which is the highest among neighboring countries and higher than the OECD average (1 percent of budget). MOHESR data also highlight that salary expenditures take up 71.6 percent of the budget, while investment represents 9.2 percent of expenditures. In comparison, staff compensation represents 61 percent of total expenditures in tertiary public institution in OECD countries32 and 67 percent of recurrent expenditures in Morocco.33 As of 2023, the MOHESR allocates close to 70 percent of the budget to higher education programs, 21 percent to scholarships, housing and feeding programs, 8 percent to scientific research and 2 percent to the general administration of the system. In this context, there have been several attempts to decentralize university governance related to academia, administration and financial management since 2000. The 2008 Higher Education Law introduced the Public establishment of scientific and technological nature status (Etablissement Public à Caractère Scientifique et Technologique (EPST)) which increased institutions’ autonomy in managing their financial resources. The status of EPST entails ex-post control, commercial accounting, and flexible procurement procedures. In addition to the scientific council, institutions that gain EPST status are endowed with a governing board (similar to a board of directors). However, to effectively assume greater autonomy with accountability under a new state-institution contractual framework, the transition to EPST necessitates significant changes in organization, management, and capabilities. Given the transition of 15 institutions into EPST in 2023 and the envisioned expansion of this status within other universities and HEIs, the government would need to support institutions to prepare for this transition, and provide ongoing support to those that recently transitioned. STEEIR will use PAQs, building on the extensive experience and lessons learned from its predecessor project, TEEP, which was itself designed taking into account the teachings of PARES II. PAQs are competitive funds, mainly to universities and HEIs, that aim to meet the growing demand for higher education, improve the quality of programs, and enhance the managerial and financial viability of the public university system. Page 17 The World Bank Strengthening Tertiary Education for Employability Innovation and Resilience (P506551) 54. The proposed design of STEEIR builds on the key strengths of TEEP, specifically: (a) The competitive, bottom-up approach of the PAQs which ensured strong institutional leadership and ownership; (b) Principles of quality, evaluations and accountability which were introduced in universities through the PAQs; (c) The enhanced outreach to the private sector through co-constructed programs, internships, applied learning with private sector and PhD student mobility with industry, among others; and (d) The importance of governance and quality support through the PAQs to universities and HEIs’ management including the development of universities and HEIs strategic plans for the first time. 55. STEEIR also seeks to improve on the main challenges encountered during the implementation of TEEP. In particular: (a) The large number of PAQ sub-projects (over 400) had the important benefit of impacting the entire ecosystem. However, it made monitoring and support a challenge and spread investments thinly across multiple projects. Thus, aiming to balance equity with transformational potential, this project proposes 100-150 sub-projects and 6 PAQ windows (instead of 400 sub-projects and 14 PAQ windows); (b) Execution was slowed down due to a heavy reliance on project teams’ intrinsic motivation to lead PAQ implementation and a lack of project management support to these teams. To counter this, this project includes the creation of Project Management Offices (PMO) within universities and HEIs implementing large PAQs ; (c) Heavy administrative and procurement processes hindered efficiency. In this project, the TORs for the calls for proposals for competitive funds and their associated templates will be part of the POM. Furthermore, there will be consolidated procurement contracts for activities beyond the PAQs; (d) Some projects had insufficient institutionalization, remaining at a small-scale, thus suggesting a need for stronger integration and coordination at the university-level to maximize their research and longer-term impact. 56. Building on these lessons, the STEEIR Project will seek to have a more system-wide impact. Specifically, through a more targeted and impactful competitive fund that will support an institutional approach, shifting from small individual research grants to consolidated, institutional support both at the HEIs and universities; and through enhanced system- focused PAQs that build on TEEP’s project support of strategic plans for a larger systemic impact on the institutions. Tunisia’s geographic location makes it one of the most vulnerable countries in the Mediterranean region to climate change67 - facing adverse impacts from rising temperatures, increased aridity, reduced precipitation, and rising sea levels. While Tunisia has observed a mean average temperature increase of 1.4°C since 1901, the country is projected to experience mean annual temperature increases up to 5.3°C by the 2080s.68 Precipitation, which has dropped by approximately 3 percent over the past 30 years, is expected to continue decreasing through the end of the century. 69 Furthermore, the rise of sea levels is projected to result in the loss of a sizable proportion of the northern and eastern coastlines due to a combination of inundation and erosion, with consequential loss of agricultural land, infrastructure, and urban areas.70 If no urgent measure is taken to address climate change-related risks, particularly water shortages, the Tunisian economy could shrink by 3.4 percent of GDP by 2030 (close to TND5.6 billion a year or US$1.8 billion in net present value).71 On the other hand, adequately addressing climate change and decarbonizing the electricity sector could increase the GDP by 9 percent, reduce poverty by 12 percent and cut energy emissions by 80 percent by 2030.72 2. In response, Tunisia’s Vision 2035 and national sectoral strategies elevate sustainability as a key priority, focusing on developing a climate resilient economy and a knowledge economy. The Government of Tunisia has developed high-level strategies to address climate change and foster a green transition. The 2023-2025 Development Plan underscores the need for a climate-resilient economic transition and estimates the investment needed to achieve the transition at TND6.7 billion (or US$ 2.2 billion). The National Strategy for Ecological Transition launched in 2023 aims to implement a resilient, sustainable, socially fair and inclusive development model. The Water strategy for 2050 aims to ensure the availability and access to water resources efficiently and sustainably, including via water resource mobilization programs, demand management policies, and increasing economic efficiency of water usage. Similarly, Tunisia’s energy strategy 2035 aims to increase the share of renewable energy in the primary energy mix to 18 percent, and the share in the electricity production mix to 50 percent by 2035. 3. The Government of Tunisia prioritizes higher education and scientific research as critical pillars for driving climate mitigation and adaptation and ensuring a successful green transition. In its 2021 Updated NDC submission to the United Nations,73 the Government of Tunisia highlighted that its commitment to developing renewable energies and reducing emissions can only be achieved through investments in education and training, among others. Revamping the higher education curriculum is pivotal in driving the country’s green transition, equipping the emerging workforce with the relevant skills and foundational knowledge required for green jobs and the greening of brown sectors. In fact, modernizing the academic offer is a strategic imperative to fulfill the forecasted demand of 70,000 additional jobs in the energy sector between 2021 and 2035, including 65,000 jobs in green hydrogen by 2035, and an additional 400,000 by 2050.74 Similarly, the Tunisian government aims to enhance applied research as a key driver of the climate resilient economy, leveraging innovation to advance climate adaptation and mitigation. To this end, the MOHESR launched a call for proposal in 2024 to develop research consortia in key sectors, including energy and water security, environment and ecological transformation and sustainable development of marine areas and support for the blue economy. In line with the Government of Tunisia’s priorities, the STEEIR Project aims to empower higher education and scientific research as pivotal sectors in addressing climate vulnerability, building resilience and promoting sustainability. The project builds on the premise that meeting the increasing demands to address climate vulnerability will require a whole-of-university approach, which puts sustainability at the center of the mission of higher education and scientific research institutions, and strive for a greater integration of education, research and management of university operations.75 This approach aims to create coherence across curriculum, research, outreach and infrastructure, so that students can engage with climate change at different levels of depth and complexity, integrating what they learn in different classes with what they experience in classrooms and in the campus.59 It seeks to reduce climate vulnerability by considering both mitigation and adaptation within each component: whether by fostering green skills among students, promoting applied research in renewable energies or greening campus or ensuring all supported infrastructures are climate-resilient and energy-efficient.76 Importantly, STEEIR will strive to embed sustainability within university strategic plans, thus providing strong university ownership over their sustainability agenda and ensuring it extends beyond the STEEIR Project’s scope and duration.
