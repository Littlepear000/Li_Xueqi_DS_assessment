Introduction and Context Country Context 1. The Government of Uganda’s (GoU’s) progressive refugee response model has generated a development dividend for hosting districts, but they still face major development challenges and refugees are well short of achieving self-reliance. Ever-increasing refugee inflows continue to place a strain on host communities at a time of declining humanitarian aid. Since Phase I of the Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project (DRDIP) in the Horn of Africa (HoA) was approved in May 2016, the refugee population in Uganda has increased markedly from 220,000 to over 1.8 million. More inflows are expected given the ongoing instability in the region. Most refugees are hosted in rural communities that are struggling with poverty and unemployment, human capital and service delivery deficits, limited basic infrastructure and a degraded natural resource base. And, despite the opportunities and rights afforded them, refugees in Uganda lack employment opportunities and suffer from poverty rates almost three times higher than Ugandan nationals. Given the huge population, self-reliance for refugees and host communities is an important development priority for Uganda. As a regional and global exemplar of the value of an open-door policy and the Page 2 The World Bank Uganda Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project in the Horn of Africa Phase II (P510476) development approach to forced displacement, it is crucial to support Uganda with long-term financing for refugees and host communities. 2. Seeing the protracted refugee presence as both a development challenge and an opportunity, DRDIP Phase II (DRDIP II) in Uganda will continue to address the negative impacts of the refugee presence while maximizing the positive. Negative impacts relate to overcrowded basic services (education, health, water) and damage to the environment in refugee-hosting districts (RHDs) and transit districts. On the other hand, the refugee presence in Uganda is associated with substantial improvements in local development, demonstrating that a combination of progressive policies and long-term development responses has the potential to turn a refugee “burden�? into an “opportunity.�? In line with the objectives of the Global Compact on Refugees (GCR) and findings from the 2023 World Development Report on Migrants, Refugees and Societies, the proposed project will also support the achievement of self-reliance for refugees and local residents and the integration of refugees into national service delivery systems, which lowers costs and promotes social cohesion. Accordingly, building on the success of Phase I, DRDIP II will continue to: (a) invest in basic service infrastructure such as schools, health facilities, water systems, roads, markets, etc.; (b) respond to the environmental impacts of the refugee presence and Uganda’s land degradation crisis; and (c) support economic opportunity and income-generation for refugees and host communities. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Uganda is the largest refugee host in Africa, and among the five largest worldwide. There are over 1.8 million refugees and asylum-seekers in the country at present, most of whom originate from South Sudan (54 percent) and the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) (31 percent). Uganda continues to experience significant inflows, with over 170,000 refugees arriving during 2024. The refugees are formally settled in 12 of Uganda’s 146 districts, plus Kampala. 4. Uganda is globally recognized for its refugee policy framework, which is closely aligned with the priorities of the GCR. Uganda’s refugee policies deliver the shared international vision under the 2018 GCR, that allows refugees to work, access land, move around freely and access social services alongside Ugandan nationals. 5. Despite the strong policy enabling environment, both host communities and refugees in RHDs continue to face significant development deficits. Refugees are predominately hosted in rural communities that are struggling with major development challenges. While the refugee presence – primarily by attracting humanitarian and development assistance – has generally supported development progress in RHDs, poverty rates and basic service provision have either plateaued or worsened in rural areas of Uganda over the last decade. Growing number of refugees are opting to settle in urban areas in search of better opportunities. 6. The large refugee inflows are also contributing to Uganda’s land degradation crisis. While environmental degradation and deforestation are not new phenomena in Uganda, the large-scale and long-term presence of refugees has added to the existing pressure on the environment. From 2001 to 2023, Uganda lost 1.1 million hectares of tree cover, equivalent to a 14 percent decrease since 2000. Wetlands decreased from 16 percent of the country’s land area in 1994 to an estimated 10 percent in 2021, according to the Ministry of Water and Environment. In a country where 89 percent of primary energy needs are met by biomass, land degradation is a multi-faceted crisis that has been deepened by the large refugee population. 7. Degradation of the natural environment is placing refugees and host communities at higher risk from climate change effects. Uganda’s economy is highly dependent on ‘climate-sensitive’ sectors such as agriculture, fisheries, tourism and forestry. Ranked of 163 out of 187 on the Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative index, urgent action is Page 3 The World Bank Uganda Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project in the Horn of Africa Phase II (P510476) required to address climate vulnerability in Uganda. Droughts have become more frequent and longer-lasting, especially in the western, northern and north-eastern regions, which cover most of the proposed DRDIP II target areas. This is causing more frequent crop failure, threatening livelihoods, increasing food insecurity and worsening water scarcity. In the future, rainfall is expected to decrease further in the north and north-eastern regions and increase in the west. As the climate crisis intensifies, the country faces rising incidents of extreme weather, leading to disasters such as floods, droughts, and landslides. To respond to climate vulnerability, DRDIP II will integrate adaptation and mitigation measures in activity design and implementation. It will also fund activities to improve climate-smart agricultural productivity, restore degraded land, and protect and expand tree cover. 8. Humanitarian funding cuts are exacerbating the development challenges. Funding for the Uganda Country Refugee Response Plan (UCRRP) has declined since 2018, while the refugee population continues to grow. Since July 2023, food rations have been cut, new arrivals have had limited access to water and some health workers are not being paid. This is causing pressure on social services, livelihoods and infrastructure, triggering tensions within settlements and with host communities, negative coping behaviors, and mental health problems. C. Proposed Development Objective(s) Development Objective(s) (From PAD) 9. To improve access to basic social and economic services, expand economic opportunities and enhance environmental management for host communities and refugees in the target areas. Key Results 10. The proposed PDO indicators are: • Displaced people and people in host communities provided with services and livelihoods (number, disaggregated by type of service and target group, including refugee/host community member & gender) (corporate scorecard); • People benefiting from activities and actions that expand and enable economic activity (number, disaggregated by target group, including refugee/host community member & gender); and • People benefitting from enhanced resilience of terrestrial and aquatic systems (number, disaggregated by target group, including refugee/host community member & gender) (corporate scorecard). D. Project Description 11. Beneficiaries and geographic coverage area. The project activities are expected to benefit three million people (two million host community members/one million refugees). It will cover the 12 refugee-hosting districts, five urban centers and six transit districts, which temporarily host asylum seekers when they cross the border and before they are settled in one of the hosting districts. The project will be organized around five components, as described below: 12. Component 1: Social and Economic Services and Infrastructure (US$143 million). This component will support the construction, rehabilitation and upgrading of priority basic service infrastructure. Subprojects will be identified by communities to address ongoing infrastructure and service delivery deficits. The investments are expected to include schools, health facilities, water systems, rural roads, bridges, markets and storage facilities. Infrastructure subprojects will benefit both refugees and host community members. The project will fund 480 subprojects. The component includes Page 4 The World Bank Uganda Development Response to Displacement Impacts Project in the Horn of Africa Phase II (P510476) a US$15 million contingency financing mechanism that can rapidly disburse funds to districts that experience large refugee inflows. 13. Component 2: Integrated Natural Resource Management (INRM) (US$30 million) will support environmental restoration and protection to respond to land degradation in the RHDs and the additional impacts resulting from the refugee presence and climate change. The component is expected to support 760,000 people (50 percent female/30 percent refugees) with enhanced resilience of terrestrial and aquatic systems, covering 14,800 hectares of land. The component will support a combination of: (a) land restoration and protection activities such as hillside terraces, bunds, check dams and trenches to build the ecosystem and resilience to water scarcity, and planting of grasses, ground cover and indigenous trees and shrubs to reduce run-off, tackle erosion and landslides, re-stabilize the environment, and build adaptation to droughts; and (b) expansion of tree cover by supporting registered community groups with demonstrated experience in forestry to improve the scale and quality of community tree nurseries, and the establishment of tree plantations with market orientation. Groups will also be supported to establish wood fuel plantations in public institutions with kitchens (e.g., schools/health facilities) to provide a sustainable supply of fuel for mass catering. 14. Component 3: Economic Opportunity (US$78 million) will enhance economic opportunities and investment capacity of host communities and refugees across agriculture, livestock, and non-farm enterprises. It aligns with the Government of Uganda’s strategic emphasis on agri-business-based industrialization, value addition, entrepreneurship mindset, and economic transformation. The component will support accelerated investments in priority value chains, advance the integration of science, technology, and innovation, and expand access to finance. The component will support at least 200,000 households (35 percent refugees) with a package of services (capacity-building, technical assistance, asset transfer and financing) to support climate-sensitive business plans. 15. Component 4: Project Management, Accountability Systems and Coordination (US$27 million) will support project management, coordination, communication, monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and learning. This will include specific transparency, accountability, and anticorruption measures, working with the Inspectorate of Government. 16. Component 5: Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC) (US$0). The project covers a wide geographical scope, with potential cumulative risks and impacts of multiple activities implemented under DRDIP I, activities by other development partners operating in refugee hosting areas and proposed activities to be implemented under DRDIP II. Some of the activities to be supported through the project under component 2, such as integrated NRM will have positive environmental impacts. The potential environmental and social risks that may arise from component 1 may include: (a) noise, dust, soil, water pollution; (b) construction debris; (c) sedimentation and deposition of fine materials in downstream watercourses during construction; (d) soil and water pollution due to construction; (e) occupational hazards from the construction of new buildings; (f) construction works for health facilities, primary schools, and secondary schools, and upgrading, and expansion of TVET centers, rural feeder roads, small bridges, water systems and sanitation facilities, marketplaces, storage facilities, water harvesting; and (g) small-scale processing facilities linked to Component 3. 19. Risks for the operational phase will come from fugitive dust and other emissions (e.g., from vehicles). There are risks to sensitive ecosystems and biodiversity hotspots in the western and south-western refugee hosting districts. The activities under component 2, which covers land restoration, protection of fragile ecosystems, afforestation, agroforestry and woodlot establishments will have positive environmental impacts. However, some of the activities to be financed by Component 1, including construction of small scale economic and social infrastructure could lead to small scale land clearance. Similarly, agricultural activities to be supported by the project can have impacts on biodiversity and ecosystems because of pollution and introduction of invasive species, though this project is not anticipated to invest in conversion of natural or critical habitats. The project will not finance any activity that could lead to conversion of critical habitats. The Environmental and Social Assessment has mapped out sensitive ecosystems and biodiversity hotspot and recommends mitigation measures to enhance conservation of the identified areas. 20. In addition to the measures and instruments prepared and to be prepared, the project’s primary social risk management guideline will be the Project Implementation Manual (PIM). Screening of sub-projects will help identify any potential risks and develop mitigation measures. This will follow the risk mitigation approach to avoid, minimize, or mitigate these risks and negative impacts on the community and Environment. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 21. The project will be implemented by the Office of the Prime Minister (OPM), operating under the guidance of an inter-agency Project Steering Committee comprising relevant agencies of government. Planning and implementation will be fully integrated into government systems, with districts and communities leading the identification and implementation of subproject investments.
