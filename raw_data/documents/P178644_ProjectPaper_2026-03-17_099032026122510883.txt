BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING A. Introduction 1. This Project Paper seeks approval of the Division Director level for an Additional Financing (AF) in the amount of US$40 million, of which US$30 million from IDA Crisis Response Window (CRW), to the Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde Project (“ICIUP” P178644). The AF builds on a well-performing parent operation and is proposed in response to emerging investment needs identified during implementation, as well as urgent post-disaster recovery and resilience priorities following the 2025 heavy rainfall events. The AF leverages existing institutional arrangements, safeguards instruments, and a prepared pipeline of investments, allowing for rapid scale-up without changes to the Project Development Objective (PDO) or core project architecture. The parent project, which PDO was to improve access to climate-resilient transport and urban infrastructure for selected project areas in the Recipient’s territory, was approved by the World Bank Board of Executive Directors on November 21, 2023, and declared effective on February 26, 2024, with total financing of US$40 million equivalent. 2. The proposed AF responds to a formal request from the Government of Cabo Verde (GoCV) to the World Bank, received on January 6, 2026, seeking additional resources to sustain momentum under the project and further support the national strategy to deliver efficient, safe, and climate-resilient transport and urban infrastructure across selected project areas. The request reflects both the rapid commitment of the parent project’s resources and the need to respond to new priorities that emerged after the 2025 climate shocks. 3. The proposed AF comprises three complementary types of activities that together define its scope and rationale. First, the AF supports the scale-up of selected investments, expanding the geographic coverage or scope of interventions identified under the parent project but not fully financed within the original envelope. Second, it addresses higher cost estimates resulting from refined technical designs, notably the incorporation of enhanced climate-resilient standards in urban upgrading and road rehabilitation works. Third, and critically, the AF includes post-disaster recovery and resilient reconstruction activities in areas affected by the 2025 floods, financed through IDA’s CRW. Accordingly, the AF will: (i) expand the scope of priority investments identified following rapid commitment of the initial budget; (ii) support Cabo Verde’s post-disaster recovery and resilient reconstruction needs following the August 2025 floods in the Barlavento islands and the November 2025 heavy rains in Santiago; (iii) cover higher-than-anticipated costs resulting from design changes, including enhanced climate-resilience standards and delays linked to extreme weather and logistical constraints; and (iv) strengthen Environmental and Social (E&S) risk management, labor practices, and fiduciary systems, in line with implementation experience and recent Implementation Status and Results Report (ISR) findings. 1 B. Project’s Background 4. The project was conceived as part of a broader partnership between the GoCV and the World Bank aimed at strengthening transport connectivity and urban infrastructure while improving resilience and territorial development nationwide. The project addresses structural challenges associated with Cabo Verde’s dispersed island geography, limited redundancy in infrastructure networks, rapid urbanization, and high exposure to climate-related hazards, particularly floods, landslides, and erosion. As such, the project targets key urban centers 1The last ISR of the project was approved on December 23, 2025. https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099122325165528428 Official Use Only Page 1 The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde (P178644) and transport corridors that are critical for economic activity, access to services, and social inclusion. Many of these areas—particularly informal or rapidly expanding urban neighborhoods— are in flood-prone catchments or along unstable slopes, making them highly vulnerable to extreme rainfall events. This vulnerability has been repeatedly exposed by recent climate shocks, underscoring the importance of resilient infrastructure investments. 5. Implementation follows a hybrid model, combining strategic coordination and oversight by the Ministry of Infrastructure, Territorial Planning, and Housing (Ministério das Infraestruturas, Ordenamento do Território e Habitação, MIOTH) through its Project Execution Unit (CEP-MIOTH), with delivery by sector institutions and State-owned Enterprises (SOEs)— Road Agency of Cabo Verde (Estradas de Cabo Verde, ECV) for roads and Infrastructures of Cabo Verde (Infraestruturas de Cabo Verde, ICV) for infrastructure works—while the Special Projects Management Unit (Unidade de Gestão de Projetos Especiais, UGPE) supports selected Technical Assistance (TA) activities. This model allows for execution at scale while progressively strengthening institutional capacity, an objective that is further reinforced under the AF. Key lessons from implementation to date—including the importance of early and continuous community engagement, timely preparation and disclosure of safeguards instruments, effective Grievance Redress Mechanisms (GRMs), and proactive contract management—have informed both the AF design and the prioritization of activities. These lessons are particularly relevant given the expanded scope and multi-island nature of the AF. C. Parent PDO and Components and Current Project Status 6. Parent PDO (unchanged): The PDO is to improve access to climate-resilient transport and urban infrastructure for selected project areas in the Recipient’s territory. The AF contributes to this objective by deepening and expanding the scale of investments without altering the PDO itself. 7. The parent project comprises four operational components and a Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC). Component 1 focuses on resilient urban and community infrastructure; Component 2 on transport connectivity and road rehabilitation; Component 3 on TA and sector capacity building; Component 4 on project management and implementation support; and Component 5 provides a CERC mechanism, which has not been activated to date. As of March 2026, the Implementation Progress (IP) rating is Moderately Satisfactory. While PDO indicators have not yet reflected progress—pending the completion of civil works—implementation has reached an advanced stage. Key achievements include the launch of tenders for the first tranche of urban upgrading works (Achada Igreja Lote 1, Mosteiros, and Tarrafal de São Nicolau), with the works for Mosteiros already underway and both Achada Igreja and Tarrafal expected to commence in the first trimester of 2026. From the outset of implementation, the project has applied climate-resilient design principles, including upgraded stormwater and sanitary drainage systems, reinforced slopes, resilient pavements, and Official Use Only Page 3 The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde (P178644) improved public spaces. While these measures are essential to reduce long-term climate risk, they have also increased unit costs relative to initial estimates, particularly once designs were finalized. In parallel, implementation of road works has been affected by equipment mobilization challenges, labor shortages exacerbated by emigration, and repeated extreme weather events. These constraints have highlighted the importance of AF to sustain implementation momentum and address emerging recovery needs without compromising quality or resilience standards. 10. The existing implementation arrangement led by MIOTH, with ICV and ECV responsible for works delivery, has proven broadly adequate and remains appropriate for the AF. However, the expanded scope and accelerated pace of activities under the AF make continued capacity strengthening—particularly in procurement continuity, contract management, E&S supervision and inter-institutional coordination—both necessary and timely. 11. The project has also made progress in strengthening procurement and Financial Management (FM) systems, though some delays persist in audit/reporting and contract management. Fiduciary performance is assessed as Moderately Satisfactory, notwithstanding an elevated fiduciary risk rating driven primarily by procurement‑related capacity and continuity constraints, which are being actively mitigated through enhanced oversight and targeted capacity‑strengthening measures. E&S risk management is rated Moderately Satisfactory, with ongoing efforts to address labor management, compliance, and community engagement. The GRM is operational and accessible, with most complaints resolved in a timely manner. The project has disbursed 44 percent of total funds and ~85 percent committed through signed contracts. 12. Grant Facility for Project Preparation (GFPP): SDR 740,000 grant (US$1 million equivalent) was approved and signed on August 30, 2025, to support preparatory work for the AF, covering activities such as subproject proposals, resilience and environmental/social assessments, strategic diagnostics, strengthening the implementation unit, traffic management review, and improvements to monitoring and communication systems. These on-going GFPP activities have informed the preparation. D. Rationale for Additional Financing 13. Rationale for AF. The proposed AF responds to three converging needs identified during implementation of the parent project: (i) strong implementation performance and rapid commitment of funds, which left several priority investments aligned with the PDO without adequate financing; (ii) higher unit costs associated with design refinements and the systematic incorporation of enhanced climate‑resilient standards following detailed engineering and consultations, which revealed that initial estimates did not fully capture the scope and cost of required resilience measures, rather than increases on signed contracts; and (iii) urgent post‑disaster recovery and resilient reconstruction needs arising from the 2025 extreme rainfall events. The AF allows the project to scale up and deepen its impact while preserving implementation momentum, maintaining the PDO, and relying on established institutional arrangements and safeguards. 14. The primary rationale for the AF is to finance the remaining prioritized urban upgrading investments under Component 1 and to scale up priority road investments under Component 2, while addressing cost increases associated with design refinements and enhanced climate‑resilient standards identified during detailed engineering and consultations. The AF also responds directly to Cabo Verde’s post-disaster recovery needs following the August and November 2025 heavy rainfall events, which triggered flash floods, landslides, and widespread infrastructure damage across São Vicente, Santo Antão, São Nicolau, and Santiago. According to the Official Use Only Page 4 The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde (P178644) government-led Post-Disaster Needs Assessment (PDNA) 2 , physical damages are estimated at approximately US$31 million, losses at US$9 million, and recovery needs at US$84.8 million, while additional assessments following the November rains indicate significant transport rehabilitation needs in Santiago, bringing total recovery needs to approximately US$109.8 million. 15. CRW resources in the amount of US$30 million have been approved at the regional level for Cabo Verde’s response to the 2025 floods, with clearance on behalf of the Regional Vice President on February 18, 2026. CRW-financed activities directly address priority damage and recovery needs identified in the PDNA, particularly failures in urban drainage systems, slope instability, damaged access corridors, and disrupted transport links. All CRW-supported works apply build-back-better and climate-resilient standards, ensuring that reconstruction both restores functionality and reduces future disaster risk. Several CRW-financed interventions were already included in the parent project pipeline and had reached advanced stages of preparation. The 2025 floods caused overlapping damage in these same locations, underscoring both the urgency and eligibility of targeted recovery and resilience financing through CRW. 16. CRW-supported interventions include urban interventions and roads as part of Components 1 and 2. These include the urban rehabilitation of Covada Bruxa and Fernando Pó (São Vicente), Ribeira Brava historical center (São Nicolau), Rincão and João Teves (Santiago), and the rehabilitation and climate-resilient upgrading of the Figueiral road (Santo Antão), as well as a series of roads in Santiago Island (Milho Branco – Nossa Senhora da Luz; secund phase of Ribeira dos Picos; Ribeirão Almaço; Chão Formoso – Boa Ventura; Água de Gato), aligned with PDNA-identified recovery priorities. In total, US$16 million of CRW resources will be allocated to Component 1 for urban recovery and flood-resilient upgrading, and US$14 million to Component 2 for the rehabilitation, upgrading, and construction of priority roads. 17. The AF remains aligned with the government’s strategy and the World Bank’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF)3 for Cabo Verde (FY2020–FY2025), reinforcing the commitment to resilient, inclusive, and sustainable urban development. The AF also complements the World Bank’s broader crisis response in Cabo Verde, which initially focused on liquidity and early recovery through the drawdown of the Second Resilient and Equitable Recovery Development Policy Financing with Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option (CAT-DDO) (P176148) and subsequent activation of the Rapid Response Option (RRO) as supplementary financing to the CAT-DDO; by shifting toward financing medium-term resilient reconstruction investments that safeguard development gains and reduce structural vulnerability. As such, the AF is essential to sustain project momentum and maximize development impact, in a spirit of longer-term partnership. The AF represents a deepening of the collaboration started under the parent project and builds on its foundations, scaling up the scope of prioritized investments. 18. Alternative options were considered to address the remaining financing needs and emerging recovery priorities. Further reallocation within the parent project was not feasible, given that the original financing envelope is already largely committed through signed contracts and ongoing works. Activation of the CERC was deemed insufficient to cover the scale and medium-term nature of the recovery and resilience investments required following the 2025 floods. Preparation of a new standalone operation would have entailed significant delays and duplication of safeguards, institutional arrangements, and implementation structures at a time when rapid scale up is critical. DESCRIPTION OF ADDITIONAL FINANCING A. Changes to Parent Project 19. The proposed AF will introduce the following changes to the parent project: (a) updating the results framework and disbursement arrangements to reflect the expanded scope of urban upgrading and road rehabilitation activities; (b) incorporating lessons learned from the initial phase on how to continue empowering MIOTH (enhancement of the E&S capacity of MIOTH / ECV/ ICV through the mobilization of a cohort of trainees); and (c) revising the project’s implementation schedule to accommodate the scale-up of activities and ensure uninterrupted progress on the remaining interventions. Disbursement estimates have also been adjusted to reflect the AF mobilization. B. PDO and AF Outcomes 20. The PDO remains unchanged. The results framework has been refined to include updated targets for the number of beneficiaries, the extent of urban and transport infrastructure upgraded, and the effectiveness of grievance redress and labor management systems. The AF will enable the project to serve a broader segment of the population, the scope being expanded to include additional road segments identified as part of the priority interventions during the initial phase, ensuring that the project’s impact is both deepened and broadened in line with national development objectives. Despite higher unit costs associated with enhanced climate‑resilient designs and an expanded scope, the AF remains economically justified. Updated analysis confirms robust returns for both urban upgrading and road rehabilitation, with economic rates of return well above applicable benchmarks (see details in Annex 1). The AF therefore preserves—and in several cases strengthens—the economic rationale of the parent project while delivering enhanced resilience and recovery benefits. a. For urban upgrading (Component 1), the analysis of nine interventions across five islands yields a Net Present value (NPV) of about US$52 million and an Economic Rate of Return (ERR) of approximately 24.5 percent over a 20‑year horizon, driven by reduced flood damages, increased property values, and improved economic activity. b. For road rehabilitation (Component 2), the analysis shows an overall Economic Internal Rate of Return (EIRR) of about 17 percent and a positive NPV of approximately US$3.4 million, reflecting vehicle operating cost savings, travel time benefits, road safety effects, and Greenhouse Gas (GHG) impacts. 34. GHG accounting: GHG accounting has been undertaken for the transport component. The analysis indicates net GHG emission reductions over the 20-year evaluation period, driven primarily by improved road conditions and more efficient vehicle operating speeds, with total net emissions estimated at -15,713 tCO₂e (approximately -786 tCO₂e per year). GHG accounting is not required for the urban upgrading activities, as these do not include solid waste management investments. B. Technical and Climate 35. The technical approach for the AF remains consistent with the parent project, using established engineering standards and climate-resilient design principles. The AF will support a scale-up of activities, expanding the scope of urban upgrading and road rehabilitation interventions without introducing significant changes to the technical methodology. The AF strengthens the project’s climate-resilient focus by applying adaptation-oriented design standards used under the parent project—including upgraded drainage systems, slope stabilization, and resilient pavements—to address increasing exposure to extreme rainfall and flooding. In line with the government’s reconstruction and recovery strategy following the 2025 heavy-rainfall events, all new works have been designed to build back better, integrating enhanced stormwater and sanitary drainage solutions, slope-stabilization measures, resilient pavements, and improved protection of vulnerable public spaces. These design updates respond directly to the damages and service disruptions documented in the PDNA—including the failure of drainage networks, erosion of slopes, and destruction of local mobility links—and ensure that reconstructed infrastructure not only restores pre-disaster functionality but also reduces future climate-related risks. As a result, the AF interventions both restore critical infrastructure in the affected islands and strengthen long-term resilience across the project areas. 36. Paris Alignment. The proposed AF is aligned with the adaptation and mitigation objectives of the Paris Agreement, contributing positively to climate adaptation objectives with low mitigation risks. It supports Cabo Verde’s adaptation priorities under its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) and National Adaptation Plan (NAP), which prioritize climate‑resilient transport and urban infrastructure, flood risk management, and institutional Official Use Only Page 11 The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde (P178644) capacity strengthening. It is also aligned with key recommendations of the Country Climate and Development Report, which highlight the importance of resilient infrastructure (such as resilient transport corridors, urban drainage) and public sector capacity to address the country’s high exposure to extreme rainfall, flooding, and landslides, including risk-informed planning in a Small Island Developing State context. a. Assessment and reduction of adaptation risks: The operation directly addresses Cabo Verde’s high exposure to climate‑related hazards (particularly extreme rainfall, flooding, erosion, and slope instability), which were manifested during the 2025 extreme weather events. Climate‑resilient design standards are systematically integrated across all components, including upgraded stormwater and sanitary drainage systems, slope stabilization and erosion control measures, reinforced pavements and hydraulic structures, etc. By prioritizing interventions in areas most affected by the 2025 extreme rainfall events and applying build-back-better principles, the AF supports post-disaster recovery and long-term adaptive capacity. These measures reduce vulnerability to current and future climate risks while enhancing the reliability and continuity of transport and urban services. Adaptation risks are therefore reduced to an acceptable level. b. Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks: The AF activities focus on rehabilitation and resilience strengthening and do not introduce activities that would materially increase GHG emissions or lock in carbon‑intensive pathways. Activities related to road upgrading and rehabilitation, flood management, urban drainage, technical assistance, and training and capacity building are considered are considered universally aligned. The remaining activities are considered low risk. Improved road conditions and more efficient vehicle operating speeds will also drive net emissions reductions over the evaluation period. Mitigation risks are therefore reduced to a low level. 37. Climate Adaptation and Mitigation Considerations. The AF is designed to maximize climate adaptation across all components. Component 2 supports climate and disaster- resilient transport through improved hydraulic structures, reinforced pavements, and slope protection on critical road corridors exposed to extreme rainfall and service disruptions due to flood and sea-level rise. Component 3 strengthens climate-informed planning and institutional capacity, including through the preparation of a National Infrastructure Plan that will integrate climate risk screening and resilience criteria to guide future investments and adapt them to projected climate scenarios. Component 4 reinforces implementation capacity for climate-resilient infrastructure delivery. Detailed operation location(s) and salient physical characteristics relevant to the ESS Assessment (geographic, environmental, social) The Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure Project (P178644) in Cabo Verde is a nationwide initiative targeting both urban and rural areas across the country’s ten islands including in major urban centers such as Praia (Santiago) and Mindelo (São Vicente). These urban centers are exposed to a full spectrum of risks, including recurrent droughts, water scarcity, coastal vulnerability, flooding risks, especially in informal settlements, aggravated by heavy rainfall events in recent years and rapid urbanization without adequate spatial or climate-informed planning. These centers are densely populated with nationals due to rapid rural–urban migration driven by access to services and employment, creating informal settlements and putting pressure on housing and essential services. The population living in informal settlements are often near unpaved streets and disconnected from services. These areas typically host micro and small informal businesses, like street vendors, household services, and small retail with many residents engaged in commuting, informal trade, and tourism linked economic activities. At the same time, these areas host concentrated vulnerable groups, particularly low-income households, informal settlement populations, youth, and women who face multidimensional poverty, limited access to basic services, precarious housing, and heightened exposure to climate risks such as flooding, erosion, and drainage failures. Environmentally sensitive receptors include informal settlements located near erodible slopes, unpaved streets, and coastal zones affected by sea level rise and saltwater intrusion. These populations face risks of Sexual Exploitation and Abuse/Sexual Harassment (SEA/SH) associated with, Labor influx, construction activities, Interaction of contractors with local communities, especially in vulnerable neighborhoods. According to INE 2024 data, Child labor in Cabo Verde remains a moderate but persistent social risk, affecting roughly 4.2% of children aged 5–17, who engage in work across agriculture, street services, domestic work, construction and artisanal fishing, often under hazardous conditions. The rural roads to be rehabilitated and/or constructed are often surrounded by small subsistence farms irrigated by rain, where intercropping of corn and beans is practiced to maximize limited land. Although infrastructure projects continue to improve connectivity in rural areas, the proximity of these crops to roads highlights the country's dependence on seasonal rains and available land, which creates significant production challenges for farmers along these roads.Located 500 km off the west coast of Africa, Cabo Verde is a biologically diverse archipelago of ten islands, hosting 47 officially designated protected areas, 20 of which are terrestrial Official Use Only Page 18 The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde (P178644) and 27 marine/coastal, covering various categories such as Natural Parks, Nature Reserves, and Protected Landscapes. However, these will be avoided through an exclusion list designed for the parent project. Only 10% of its territory is classified as arable land, and the country possesses limited mineral resources. The country’s coastlines - where approximately 80% of the population resides - are very vulnerable to rising sea levels and erosion. Cabo Verde faces significant climate change challenges, including recurrent droughts, water scarcity, coastal erosion, and recently heavy rainfall, resulting in extreme flooding direct damage risks toroads, drainage, and coastal urban infrastructures . The archipelago is highly exposed to climate shocks, making resilience and resource efficiency critical for project design. Climate change disproportionately affects informal settlements lacking adequate drainage, housing quality, and basic services. The Connectivity Project is a response to the above as it integrates climate resilience across its interventions by upgrading and climate proofing urban infrastructure in vulnerable neighborhoods, rehabilitating transport corridors to withstand flooding and erosion, developing georeferenced climate risk mapping to guide territorial planning, strengthening housing and urban information systems, and supporting institutions to incorporate hazard and risk data into decision making. Activities under Component 3 related to State-Owned Enterprise (SOE) assistance are being implemented by the Ministry of Finance through the Special Projects Management Unit "Unidade de Gestão de Projetos Especiais" (UGPE). However, as of October 2025, the social specialist is no longer with the CEP-MIOTH and recruitment for a replacement is ongoing. The environmental specialist is temporarily covering both positions, and recruitment for the new social specialist is expected to be completed in February 2026. Overall, the parent project’s E&S performance has been rated moderately satisfactory, and there are noticeable efforts, such as increased field supervisions, in order close monitor and to lead contractors to improve labor and working conditions, improve waste and traffic management on construction sites and its reporting, and to ensure early identification of resettlement plans implementation gaps with the aim to raise the rating to Satisfactory. The CEP-MIOTH will receive ongoing E&S capacity-strengthening under component 4 of the Additional Financing to enhance its ability to manage these aspects. In 2024, the World Bank conducted an Overview Assessment (OA) of Cabo Verde’s environmental and social framework, focusing on legislative strength and institutional capacity, and issued recommendations that will be applied to this Additional Financing. The assessment underscores the roles of institutions under the Ministry of Family, Inclusion and Social Development (MFIDS) that aims to combat poverty and exclusion and provides protection and services for vulnerable groups: the General Directorate of Labor (DGT), responsible for labor policy implementation and dispute mediation; the General Labor Inspectorate (IGT), which enforces the Labor Code and investigates workplace accidents; and the Cabo Verdean Institute for Gender Equality and Equity (ICIEG), established in 1994 with a central team in Praia and focal points across all 22 municipalities to lead gender policy. Since 2022, ICIEG and the UGPE have operated under a Memorandum of Understanding that sets procedures for addressing SEA/SH/GBV cases during World Bank project implementation, and these procedures will likewise be followed under the Additional Financing. The OA also highlights the role of the Ministry of Culture, through the Institute of Cultural Heritage, whose procedures will also be adopted in the implementation of the AF. The IGT plays a crucial role in monitoring and Official Use Only Page 19 The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde (P178644) promoting Occupational Safety, Hygiene, and Health (OSH) in infrastructure projects, through protocols with entities such as CEP-MIOTH, ICV and ECV, conducting joint visits, studies, surveys, and promoting training, with a view to ensuring compliance with national legislation, protecting workers, and ensuring safe and fair working environments on construction sites. The IGT's supervisory capacity was assessed as insufficient, despite having clear internal inspection procedures, it can only effectively cover the islands of Santiago, São Vicente, and Sal, with the rest of the islands subject to only sporadic supervision visits. The DGT acts as a key regulatory and supervisory body for legal compliance, working together with employers and other entities to create safer and fairer work environments. A substantial part of the DGT's activity is the mediation of labor disputes. The Cabo Verde Institute for Gender Equality and Equity (ICIEG) is the government agency responsible for promoting gender legislation and policies. ICIEG has centers and support for survivors, a toll-free hotline, tools to support the implementation of the Law on GBV, and supports survivors during GBV investigation processes. There is a good track record of cooperation between ICIEG and UGPE, through the signing of memorandum of understanding, which is being replicated with CEP-MIOTH. The Ministry of Culture is responsible for screening and managing any impacts on cultural heritage. Their capacity was assessed to be high and they have a clear procedure for managing the protection of cultural heritage, with capacity strengthened by UNESCO support. Introduction 1. As part of project preparation, a preliminary economic analysis had been conducted for Component 1 (“Resilient Urban and Community Infrastructure”), and a methodology had been proposed for Component 2 (“Enhancing Transport Connectivity and Resilience”). At the time of appraisal, final interventions had not yet been defined, thus a preliminary analysis had been performed, considering a list of initially identified possible interventions. With the economic evaluation of the additional financing, the analysis of the initial project scope has also been adjusted as the interventions have now been selected. 2. For Component 1, the cost-benefit analysis methodology to evaluate the anticipated benefits and costs associated with the urban interventions has been employed that was developed for the project appraisal and follows the guidelines of the World Bank’s Operations and Country Services (OPCS) IPF Economic Analysis Guidance Note.8 3. For Component 2, which focuses on enhancing road connectivity and resilience, the same approach as at appraisal, using a cost-benefit analysis with a cost-effectiveness approach was used. The scope of the rural roads indicated at appraisal, and the new interventions under the additional financing have been included in the analysis. The preliminary evaluation results at appraisal provided the baseline for the re-assessment. B. Economic Analysis for Component 1: Resilient Urban and Community Infrastructure 4. For Component 1 that aims to implement resilient urban and community infrastructure, the updated economic analysis was undertaken utilizing the cost-benefit analysis methodology. This approach evaluates the expected benefits and costs associated with urban interventions. The analysis was performed considering nine interventions across five islands and eight municipalities. The results of the economic analysis for this component indicate that urban and community interventions could generate economic value and positive net benefits. The overall NPV is projected to amount to US$52.13 million, considering a 20-year period and a discount rate of 12 percent,9 while the ERR is estimated at 24.52 percent. 5. Given the project will implement many urban interventions across various municipalities, each with similar purposes, interventions were grouped considering their objectives and location to estimate their collective impact. The interventions were classified into two categories: (i) climate-resilient urban upgrading in urban centers and precarious neighborhoods; and (ii) rehabilitation of historic centers and waterfront areas with tourism potential. For each category a set of benefits has been identified based on the intervention’s nature and purpose. Benefits have been estimated considering available data for each intervention. A World Bank study conducted in four African cities found that flood exposure is linked to decreased rents in three cities.23 The study estimates that the average willingness to pay to avoid flood exposure ranges from 14 percent (in Accra and Antananarivo) to 56 percent (in Addis Ababa) of the average rents. Economic analysis for Component 2: Enhancing Transport Connectivity and Resilience a. Input data and Methodology 16. To ensure that the project generates sufficient economic benefits that warrant the investments, the preliminary Cost Benefit Analysis conducted for the project road sections at appraisal was updated considering the present situation, with confirmed road section, delayed start of the works, increased costs but also increased traffic volumes. 17. Following the confirmation of the road sections included in the parent project and additional financing, the cost-benefit assessment was undertaken using the World Bank’s Roads Economic Decision (RED) Model, which computes annual road agency and users’ costs for each project alternative over the evaluation period, comparing the proposed project investments with the conditions without such investments. No additional cost-effectiveness approach was Page 47 Official Use Only The World Bank Improving Connectivity and Urban Infrastructure in Cabo Verde(P178644) PROJECT PAPER required, considering the confirmed traffic volumes. The roads included in the project have low levels of traffic at present (between 60 and 250 vehicles per day) due to their poor condition and because they are in valleys that have little opportunity to increase agricultural production. However, traffic generated after improvement of the condition is expected to be high, as this is usually the case in remote rural roads. The usual cost-benefit analysis approach for rural roads (using the Roads Economic Decision, RED model34) provides an economic justification for the proposed upgrades and improvements. 18. The quantified net benefits computed for the economic analysis of the project road sections comprise vehicle operating costs, travel time costs, road maintenance costs, road safety costs, and CO2 emissions costs. For the economic evaluation, the following assumptions were applied: • A discount rate of 12 percent and an evaluation period of 20 years starting in 2025. All costs are stated in constant 2025 US Dollars. Economic costs are 80 percent of financial costs, considering the tax rates in Cabo Verde. • Benefits from normal and generated traffic are considered in the analysis. Normal traffic was estimated during the feasibility study to grow to 15 percent per year during the first five years of the evaluation period and reducing thereafter to 5 percent per year for all vehicles, based on the estimated growth of GDP in the country and an elasticity of 1.0. Generated traffic is estimated in the feasibility to be 100 percent of the normal traffic at the opening of the new sections, as significant number of trips will be generated once all-year-round accessibility and reliable transport conditions are granted. • Road safety net benefits were computed using RED for the combined set of road sections that have comparable conditions. The economic cost of a fatality in 2025 is estimated to be US$283,550, based on the estimated GDP per capita in 2025 of US$5671 35. There is no traffic data specifically on these road sections, but the national statistics account for 11 fatalities / 100 000 inhabitants, or 1.37 fatalities by million vehicle-kilometers. On the project road sections, there would thus be estimate of 4.4 fatalities per year based on the beneficiary population, and it is estimated that there will be an increase in fatalities with the project, due to increased traffic volumes and speeds.
