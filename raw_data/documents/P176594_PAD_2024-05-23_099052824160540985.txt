Page 9 of 50 The World Bank Getting Results: Access and Delivery of Quality Education Services in Punjab Project (P176594) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Poverty reduction has slowed amid recent shocks, while growth has remained volatile and slow. Pakistan made significant progress towards reducing poverty between 2001 and 2018 with the expansion of off-farm economic opportunities and increased inflow of remittances. However, rapid poverty reduction has not fully translated into improved socio-economic conditions, as human capital outcomes have remained poor, with high levels of stunting, at 40 percent, and learning poverty at 77 percent. Critical constraints, including a persistent fiscal and current account deficit, protectionist trade policies, unproductive agriculture, a difficult business environment, a heavy state presence in the economy, and a financially unsustainable energy sector, have remained mostly unaddressed, leading to slow and volatile growth. Progress with poverty reduction has recently slowed amid macroeconomic instability, the COVID- 19 pandemic, and the 2022 floods. The estimated lower-middle income poverty rate is 40.1 percent (US$3.65/day 2017 PPP) for the year 2023-24, virtually the same as the poverty rate in 2018, but with 7 million more Pakistanis living below the poverty line. 2. Pakistan experienced heavy monsoon rains in 2022 leading to unprecedented flooding with enormous human and economic impacts. Roughly 33 million people were impacted, and many permanently displaced. More than 13,000 kilometers of roads were destroyed, 2.2 million houses damaged, around 3.8 million hectares of crops were flooded, and an estimated 1.2 million livestock were killed. Limited access to input and output markets and temporary disruptions to supply chains subsequently drove up food prices and added to existing price pressures resulting from reduced agricultural yields and the global rise of food prices. The government’s Post-Disaster Needs Assessment estimated that the need for rehabilitation and reconstruction is at US$16.3 billion.1 3. The country has made recent progress towards macroeconomic stabilization, but risks remain high and faster growth will require substantial reform. Real gross domestic product (GDP) growth contracted by 0.2 percent y-o-y in fiscal year (FY) 23, after growing by 6.2 percent in FY22 and 5.8 percent in FY21. Accumulated economic imbalances, including high fiscal deficits, and increasing debt, depleted Pakistan’s buffers which led to high vulnerability to floods, increasing world commodity prices, and tight global financing conditions. Repeated delays in implementing the previous International Monetary Fund (IMF) Extended Fund Facility (EFF) program and the associated decline in external financing inflows saw foreign reserves fall to critically low levels, amid high inflation and sharp depreciation. Following the expiry of the incomplete EFF program, a nine-month Stand-By Arrangement (SBA) was approved by the IMF, with the IMF Board approving the final review in April 2024. Under the SBA, exchange rate flexibility was restored, import controls were eased with some recovery in foreign exchange reserves, and new measures were introduced to contain the budgeted FY24 deficit. Nonetheless, risks remain high. Short- term stability depends on continued fiscal restraint and new external financing inflows. A new IMF program is currently under consideration to support continued sound economic management and structural reforms, with expected concurrent official inflows. Robust economic recovery over the medium term will require the steadfast implementation of a much broader fiscal, energy and economic reform agenda. 1 Government of Pakistan. 2022. With about 20.3 million children out of school, Pakistan has one of the largest out-of-school populations in the world. Access to schooling is notably different between genders, with an estimated 17 percent of girls versus 13 percent of boys aged 6–16 having never been to school.2 Punjab is one of Pakistan’s most economically dynamic provinces, contributing to 54.2 percent of Pakistan’s GDP (as reported by the Planning and Development (P&D) Board. It is also Pakistan’s most populated province, with 53 percent of Pakistan’s population (110 million people). The education sector in Punjab has made significant progress thanks to ambitious reforms during the last two decades. While these reforms have not targeted girls specifically, successive governments have ensured that girls have benefited from interventions such as conditional education stipends for middle and secondary schools and expansion of public-private partnerships. School participation rates for ages 6–16 in Punjab increased from 61 percent (57 percent for Pakistan as a whole) in 2000–01 to about 78 percent today (71 percent for all of Pakistan).3 The gender gap narrowed to 3 percentage points in 2019–20, down from 13 percentage points in 2000. For Pakistan overall, the gender gap fell from 17 to 10 percentage points in the same period. In Punjab, improvement in the participation rate is most visible in primary grades (age 6–10), as now 82 percent of girls and 85 percent of boys are enrolled in schools. Despite progress in enrollments overall, only 38 percent of children aged 4–5 register in pre-primary education. Dropouts in primary school are high for both boys and girls, with an estimated 15 percent of girls and 14 percent of boys dropping out before reaching grade 6. With 7.6 million out-of-school children Punjab has the largest share of the country’s out- of-school-children, at 37.4 percent (Pakistan Education Statistics, 2021-22). 5. Gender parity has long been a challenge in Pakistan. About 17 percent of girls aged 6–16 in Punjab are unlikely to ever attend school as compared to 13 percent of boys. While boys and girls have almost equal participation (39 and 38 percent, respectively) in pre-primary school, gaps emerge in primary school and widen in middle school. Research suggests that the most common factors limiting girls’ access to school include distance between schools and households, lack of safe transportation, and lack of safe water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) facilities within schools. Additional barriers include unequal intrahousehold investment in girls’ education and higher risk perceptions for girls. Qualitative studies and focus group discussions reveal sexual harassment, if not gender based violence, is prevalent, which negatively impacts girls’ potential to be in school. 6. Pakistan continues to suffer climate-induced crises, with wide-ranging impacts including in the education sector. It is among the most affected countries on the Global Climate Risk Index (2020), and extreme weather events are expected to grow in severity, with high human and economic costs. In 2022, more than 22,000 schools were damaged or destroyed, and 3.5 million children had their schooling disrupted. In Punjab, approximately 1,843 school buildings were damaged. In 2010, floods in Pakistan damaged or destroyed 10,000 schools, impacting an estimated 1.8 million children across all districts. In 2005, an earthquake in Pakistan led to the death of 853 teachers and 18,095 students, and the destruction of more than 7,500 schools. Education infrastructure and services are at risk of physical damage and disruption due to natural disasters, compounding Pakistan, and Punjab’s, current and future development challenges in education and human capital. Recurring school closures due to emergencies have aggravated the existing enrollment crisis, affecting boys and girls in different ways. For example, an estimated 8 percent of girls and 21 percent of boys aged 10–14 years who were enrolled in school before the pandemic are no longer in school.4 While COVID-19 had a greater impact on boys’ enrollment, gender inequity persists in Punjab. 8. Learning levels, which had been extremely low for all children, further decreased during the COVID-19 pandemic. According to official estimates, 65 percent of children in Punjab (77 percent in Pakistan overall) are learning poor, they do not learn to read or understand an age-appropriate paragraph by age 10. Data indicates substantial learning losses due to the pandemic, with the number of grade 5 children correctly answering mathematics questions decreasing from 34 percent in 2018 to 26 percent in 2021.5 The causes of low learning levels include low school readiness, low-quality learning material, a lack of usage of assessment data, and low-quality teaching practices compounded by multi-grade teaching in the early years. A recent survey showed that well over 90 percent of teachers in Punjab are using low- quality instructional practices, whether in public or private schools. An estimated 27 percent of public schools have multi-grade classrooms associated with much lower learning outcomes. This is particularly problematic when it occurs in pre-school where the foundations for school readiness are laid. Classrooms, starting from pre-school, also tend to have a wide range of ages, further undermining effective teaching and learning. 9. Gaps remain in mainstreaming disability inclusive education. In Punjab, 18 percent of all children between the ages 6–16 possess one or more functional disability (in a range of domains, including hearing, vision, communication, and mobility).6 However, Pakistan does not have a well-defined concept of disability inclusion in education, despite some initiatives in select districts. Schools in Punjab also lack disability-friendly educational infrastructure and facilities such as ramps, aids, and equipment. For example, only 44 percent of the surveyed public schools had a ramp in Punjab.7 There is also little focus on inclusive education in the teacher training programs in Punjab. The current Continuous Professional Development (CPD) model implemented by Quaid-e-Azam Academy for Educational Development (QAED) has no focus on training teachers on how to manage children with disability in the classroom. 10. Acknowledging that it needs to continue its reform efforts to address these challenges, Punjab is consolidating its focus on three strategic priorities. To bring more focus to the reform agenda, the province is setting explicit and gender-disaggregated targets for districts and schools, along with incentives for data-usage at school and classroom levels. 11. The first key strategic priority is sustainable and equitable expansion of school participation. Public schools in the province have serious space constraints to enroll students, and learning spaces are not adequately prepared for present and future climate shocks. The Government of Punjab (GoPunjab) estimates that approximately 98,941 classrooms will need to be constructed in public schools to offer primary education with the officially required student–teacher ratio of 40:1. Girls are particularly affected by the low availability of classrooms, especially in rural parts of the province: as distance to the closest eligible school increases, the drop in enrollment among girls is three to four times higher than that for boys. Many schools in Punjab are also located in high flood and seismic zones, with school closures expected to increase in future years without adaptation measures.8 12. As expansion of the public school system is too slow to meet increasing demand and a growing population, the province also plans to scale-up provision of schooling through public–private partnerships (PPPs) under the Punjab Education Foundation (PEF), which now provides education to approximately 2.5 million children in approximately 7,500 schools. This accounts for about 20 percent of enrollment in Punjab. While scaling quickly, PEF schools have not explicitly focused on girls’ enrollment, and girls only make up 45 percent of total enrollment in primary grades. PEF’s budget growth over the last five years has been minimal, and budget fluctuations have impacted some of PEF’s critical functions, including the retention of human resources and strengthening of the existing quality assurance system. Combined with inflationary pressure, these shortfalls have put the sustainability of the current scale of enrollment and operations at risk. 13. The second strategic priority is to bring more coherence in initiatives to improve learning outcomes, while also recovering learning losses from the pandemic. To track learning levels more effectively, Punjab has replaced high-stakes student examinations with an overarching assessment policy framework that includes summative and formative student assessments in both primary and secondary grades. As these changes are being introduced, there is a need to consistently improve the utilization of data from these assessments and shift away from the culture of examinations. The province has launched a strategy on formative assessment and plans to train teachers on how to use assessments in the classroom to track learning and provide remedial teaching for students that are lagging. 14. In terms of teacher support, Punjab made a shift from a cascade approach of teacher training to a school based CPD model, based on regular coaching from teacher mentors, who are called Assistant Education Officers, (AEOs) and classroom observation protocols, along with remote in-service training for teachers. The province aims to further build on these practices to provide better support to teachers and headteachers, to effectively use formative assessment and recover learning losses. 15. Improving school readiness before children enroll in grade 1 is becoming a core focus of the entire school system. Many children in Punjab struggle to learn upon entry in school, having had little to no exposure to early years learning and stimulation. The third strategic priority is strengthening the management of schools. There are several constraints in making meaningful improvements to the school environment. Over the last decade, schools have used Non-salary Budgets (NSBs)—small, school-level budgets—to make marginal improvements in the school’s infrastructure. However, utilization of the NSB remains low due to operational challenges in spending the budget, including value-added tax requirements, which are difficult to meet in rural areas. Most primary schools also do not have a headteacher, and there is no clear regulatory framework for the role of headteachers in schools. Headteachers are typically selected based on their seniority rather than their management capacity and do not receive any training or professional development support for a management role. C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 17. The proposed Getting Results: Access and Delivery of Quality Education Services in Punjab Project (GRADES) is consistent with the World Bank Group Country Partnership Strategy FY15 –19 for the Islamic Republic of Pakistan discussed by the Board of Executive Directors on May 1, 2014 (Report No. 84645-PK) and extended to FY20 by the corresponding Performance and Learning Review dated May 18, 2017 (Report No. 113574). While the preparation of the new Country Partnership Framework was deferred in FY21 due to the COVID-19 crisis and paused in FY23 due to the 2022 floods, the objectives of the CPS remain relevant. The new Country Partnership Framework is expected to be presented to the Board in early FY25. The focus areas and objectives of the current CPS remain relevant and are reflected in the ongoing engagement in the country. The project directly supports Results Area (RA) III – Inclusion: Reaching Out to the Underserved, Neglected and Poor, and sub-outcome 3.2 on reducing vulnerability for groups at risk by addressing constraints to children’s access to schools, particularly that of girls, by making substantial school infrastructure investments and leveraging the PPP model to expand the capacity of the education system; and RA IV – Service Delivery: Accelerating Improvements in Services, and sub-outcome 4.3 of increased school enrollment and adoption of education quality assessment through increased access for out-of-school children, improving teachers pedagogical practices to respond to the learning crisis, and strengthening and using student assessments. The project’s focus on constructing safe and sustainable learning spaces will also contribute towards the cross-cutting foundation of climate change adaptation in the public and private schools. F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 35. The design of GRADES is informed by the lessons learned from the WB’s longstanding engagement in the education sector in Punjab, in addition to other education projects in the country and globally. It also incorporates key lessons learned during the COVID-19 pandemic. The following lessons inform the design of the Project: a. The consolidation of the reform program requires improved data systems that strengthen support to schools and better institutional arrangements to facilitate delivery. Under PESP-I and PESP-II, data and monitoring have been strengthened; data reporting was integrated under PESP- III using data from various departments (PEF, PEC, Program Monitoring and Implementation Unit [PMIU], and QAED). In tandem, a delivery structure was developed with guidance from the Chairman of the P&D Board to coordinate across the various departments delivering these projects, with the PMIU providing data systems across these departments. GRADES will build on these coordination systems with enhanced focus on data use for delivering results. b. Investing in PPPs yields fast results in terms of access and learning, but the province also needs to invest in its public-school infrastructure. PESP-I, II, and III continued to support various PPP models, and PEF grew to support approximately 7,500 schools across Punjab that cater to roughly 2.5 million students. While the PPP model remains critical to urgently address the remaining challenge of out-of-school children in the country with room for growth, the GoPunjab needs to invest in its public-school infrastructure to maintain and expand capacity. Although the province has constructed approximately 2,500 classrooms in the last five years, there is a need for an additional 98,941 classrooms to provide schooling to children in ECCE to grade 5, even with current levels of enrollment to meet increasing demand and a growing population. c. Targeted pedagogical support to teachers has a positive effect on teaching practices. In 2019, a new model of CPD was introduced to strengthen the quality of teacher mentoring and training for primary schools with the use of technology, which also proved to be quite a successful modality during COVID-19 related school closures. Paris Alignment. The project is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement on both mitigation and adaptation. Pakistan has experienced on average a 0.76°C rise in temperature. In some regions, the temperature increase has been higher than national and global averages. The increased temperature manifests in extended and frequent riverine floods, heatwaves that threaten agriculture and health, and increased aridity in arid and semi-arid regions. Pakistan is among the most affected countries on the Global Climate Risk Index (2020) and these extreme weather events are expected to grow in severity, particularly as the temperatures cross the 1.5°C threshold. These events will have high human and economic costs, including to the education sector. Punjab, which has been identified as the second most vulnerable hotspot in Pakistan, is already heavily impacted by heatwaves, with the school year being shortened every year as students faint or suffer exhausted from exposure to extreme heat. Education infrastructure and services are at risk of being exposed to physical damage and disruption, compounding Punjab’s current and future development challenges in education and human capital. Project design has incorporated several construction measures to mitigate these risks including use of natural ventilation, roofing that reflects heat, and upgraded energy efficient appliances and equipment. These measures will also result in lower energy and water consumption, thereby reducing the carbon footprint of schools. Sustainable local building materials will produce fewer greenhouse gas emissions. Afforestation on school grounds will reduce the heat-island effect and conserve energy. Improved teacher guides on climate change will increase teachers’ and children’s awareness of sustainability, climate change issues, and energy-saving measures. These factors all reduce risk and make the construction activities more climate responsive. Hence all the Project supported activities are considered universally aligned as per the current ‘List of Activities Considered Universally Aligned with the Paris Agreement’s Mitigation Goals or Not Aligned with the Mitigation Goals,’ that was agreed among several multilateral development banks in June 2023. On adaptation measures, future school designs would include extreme temperature/flood resilience measures. Construction and renovation activities could also generate risks related to GBV, labor management, and community health and safety, by exposing schoolchildren, school staff and communities to the adverse impacts of construction and related activities. These risks will be mitigated by incorporating suitable provisions in contract documents and subproject design. In this way, adverse impacts and risks may be managed in a predictable manner. The GoPunjab’s extensive experience with Bank-related safeguards (especially those related to civil works) and strong commitment to the education sector will also help with the delivery of E&S mitigation measures. Implementation of all commitments under the (Environmental and Social Framework [ESF]), including for the civil works will be led by the PMIU of the SED, which has an experienced team. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a project supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms or the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly reviewed to address project-related concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non- compliance with its policies and procedures, and the Dispute Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the attention of Bank Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond.
