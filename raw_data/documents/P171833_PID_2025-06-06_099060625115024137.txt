Introduction and Context Country Context 1. In Saint Vincent and the Grenadines (SVG) high levels of economic, social, environmental, and climate vulnerability remain a major concern. Years of underinvestment in environmental resources have left the ecosystem weakened. In a recent survey, 56 percent of marine operators thought the quality of reefs had declined significantly. Furthermore, recurring extreme weather events linked to climate change, combined with the impact of COVID-19 on tourism, could push many SVG citizens into extreme poverty. The fisheries and aquaculture sectors have also suffered severe revenue losses because of the sudden stop in tourism. Improper dumping of waste and lack of sorting facilities and recycling plants contribute to marine pollution, along with other anthropogenic activities. The Government of SVG aims to advance the economic, social, and cultural development of the archipelago by realizing the full potential of its Blue Economy (BE) sectors in an integrated and climate-resilient way while protecting the health and biodiversity of its marine space. 2. On its pathway towards blue growth, SVG has devised several projects and strategic plans that helped protect its blue space and grow the island’s blue economy. SVG’s first Development Policy Credit (DPC) and its second DPC, Page 3 Official Use Only The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) – Additional Financing Request 2 which has a Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option (Cat DDO), the CROP, SVG’s Coastal and Marine Spatial plan and its National Ocean Policy are a few of the strategic initiatives that have adopted the blue economy approach to promote sustainable, climate-resilient, and integrated use of marine resources for economic growth, improved livelihoods, and jobs. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. The Blue Economy promotes sustainable and integrated use of coastal and marine resources for economic growth, improved livelihoods, and job creation while preserving the health of the ocean. It covers a range of interdependent sectors, including fisheries, tourism, and waste management. Fisheries, for example, provide the hospitality industry with quality and affordable seafood, while the tourism sector supports fisher livelihoods and generates employment. Comprehensive waste management systems and services, meanwhile, are crucial to ensure the health of coastal fisheries and other marine resources and ecosystems which are, in turn, critical for the beaches and other natural assets that attract holiday makers. Anticipating and adapting to the impacts of climate change is an essential component of the blue economy, ensuring that low-carbon and climate-resilient measures are incorporated across sectors to ensure the long-term sustainability of living marine natural resources and the myriad ecosystem services and benefits they generate to national economies and coastal livelihoods. 4. Tourism is critical to SVG’s economy, and the COVID-19 pandemic severely impacted the sector. Small businesses in SVG, reliant on tourism, faced considerable challenges, with total visitor expenditures plummeting from US$223.14 million in 2019 to US$83.38 million in 2020, a decline of over 62 percent. In 2019, the sector welcomed 404,097 visitors, including stay-over arrivals, cruise passengers, excursionists, and yacht passengers, with stay-over visitors contributing nearly half of the expenditure. The United States was the largest source for stay-over arrivals (29,737, or 35 percent of the total), followed by the Caribbean (28%) and the United Kingdom (15%). Recovery began in late 2021, driven by vaccinations in key markets and pent-up demand, leading to a record visitor expenditure of US$228.78 million in 2024, surpassing pre-pandemic levels due to a 20% increase in stay-over arrivals.1 5. SVG could position itself well over the medium term in several high-value markets that can leverage Blue Tourism, if several challenges are addressed.2 While the tourism policy framework has improved, gaps remain in comprehensive spatial planning, inadequate zoning for marine activities, as well as insufficient control over development and enforcement of regulations. Climate change impacts, such as beach erosion and reef health decline, threaten the sector if not properly managed. A recent survey of blue tourism enterprises highlighted issues with litter, waste management, and sanitation, alongside the need for reef regeneration and expanded environmental certification and nature-related experiences. Transitioning to a sustainable blue tourism model is a gradual process that necessitates a combination of policy reforms, marketing strategies, capacity building, and tourism infrastructure development, including through adoption of digital technologies, and diversification of market offerings to meet the higher standards demanded by blue tourism markets. 6. Fort Charlotte is the country’s most significant historical site and has significant economic potential as a premium tourism attraction in SVG. The UBEC Series of Projects (SOP) is designed as three phases of five-year operations, implemented over a cumulative fifteen-year period, involving multi-sectoral, blue economy investments in Caribbean countries to capitalize their untapped coastal and marine natural resources, strengthen climate resilience and accelerate economic recovery from the impacts of the global polycrisis. Phase I of the SOP focuses on integrating blue economy activities in the tourism, fisheries, and waste management sectors around a shared goal for regional development. 8. UBEC Phase I, approved by the Board on May 2, 2022, includes financing in the amount of US$90.0 million. The first Phase of the SOP is financed with National IDA credits and grants, leveraging Regional IDA, are supplemented with PROBLUE Recipient Executed Trust Fund (RETF) grants. It and is implemented in Grenada, Saint Lucia (SLU) and SVG, with the Organization of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS) Commission playing a crucial role in advancing regional blue economy activities. 9. The Project Development Objective of the UBEC SOP is “To strengthen the enabling environment for the blue economy, economic recovery, and resilience of selected coastal assets in participating countries and provide immediate and effective response to an eligible emergency�?. UBEC is organized along four components: • Component 1- Strengthening Governance, Policies, and Capacity Building (US$9 million total: US$6 million IDA, US$3 million PROBLUE): This component finances development of regional and national policies, strategies, and institutions necessary to support low-carbon and climate-resilient action, foster economic recovery and job creation, narrow the gender gap, and improve sustainable management of natural resources. It contributes to the integrity and long-term health of coastal and marine natural resources and the ecosystem services they provide. • Component 2- Scale up Access to Finance and Infrastructure Investment (US$45 million: US$44 million IDA, US$1 million PROBLUE): This component finances (1) establishment of a regional Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs) matching grants program and business development services to foster recovery and resilience of MSMEs, particularly those that are women-led or owned, create new jobs in the blue economy, and mitigate the socio- economic impacts of climate change and the pandemic, (2) expanded access to the established Caribbean Ocean and Aquaculture Sustainability Facility fisheries risk insurance scheme to protect fishers’ livelihoods against extreme Page 5 Official Use Only The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) – Additional Financing Request 2 climatic events, and (3) scale-up of coastal infrastructure investments to ensure they are sustainable, technologically advanced, and climate-resilient in the targeted sectors of Tourism, Fisheries and Solid Waste Management. • Component 3 - Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC) (US$30 million IDA) : This component helps participating countries to rapidly respond to eligible crisis or emergency, defined as “an event that has caused, or is likely to imminently cause, a major adverse economic and/or social impact associated with natural or human-made crises or disasters.�? • Component 4 - Project Management, Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E), and Communication (US$6 million IDA): This component supports effective and efficient SOP implementation, M&E, regional collaboration, and communications. It finances goods, equipment, consulting and non-consulting services, training, and operating costs of the Project Implementation Units (PIU) established at the OECS Commission and in each country for expenditures related to implementing SOP activities. 10. SVG is one of the three participating countries under UBEC Phase I. SVG has a net commitment of US$26 million (US$ 25 million IDA credit and US$ 1 million PROBLUE grant). The component-wise allocation is as follows: Component 1 (US$ 2.7m total: US$ 1.7m IDA, US 1m PROBLUE), Component 2 (US$ 11.5m IDA), Component 3 (US$ 10m IDA), Component 4 (US$ 1.6m IDA). 11. UBEC Phase I has completed its third year of implementation. The Project has been restructured twice, the first restructuring was approved by the Regional Vice President on April 16, 2024 and the second restructuring was approved by the Country Director on January 6, 2025. 12. Following the activation of the CERC in May 2023, the focus in the three countries has primarily been on the CERC activities to provide relief to food insecurity due to global polycrisis. Therefore, progress towards all PDO indicators and blue economy related intermediate indicators is lagging and remains at baseline. The CERC activities have shown strong government commitment and robust progress with significant disbursement and clear successes within communities, including in SVG. Following the completion of CERC activities in March 2025, the BE-related activities have been expedited and will be further accelerated. 13. The proposed Additional Financing (AF) of US$2.6 million to the SOP will provide the resources to complete the rehabilitation of Fort Charlotte and support small infrastructure construction as part of the approved Tourism Business Management Plan. As context, the IDA financed OECS Regional Tourism Competitiveness Project (OECS-RTCP, P152117) had prioritized rehabilitation of Fort Charlotte. Due to several extraneous reasons, including COVID-19 pandemic of 2019-2020 and the La Soufriere volcanic eruption in April 2021, the contract for rehabilitation of Fort Charlotte was delayed and civil works were only initiated in October 2023, not allowing adequate time for all works to be completed by the project closing date of May 31, 2024. At the time of the project closing, only 23 percent of the Fort’s rehabilitation had been concluded. Consequently, at the request of Government of SVG, an undisbursed amount of US$2,609,466.21 was canceled from the project on May 31, 2024, with the understanding that the rehabilitation works will be completed through integrating the funds under the ongoing UBEC Project. 14. The AF will support completion of civil works and supervision for rehabilitation of the Fort Charlotte undertaken under the ORTCP. Current Projects on International Waterways OP 7.50 No Projects in Disputed Area OP 7.60 No Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts As for the Parent Project, the combined Environmental and Social Risk rating for the overall project continues to be substantial, mainly due to potential adverse impacts of investments in sensitive areas and those related to waste management; a dispersed geographical area of implementation, potential restriction of access potential elite capture and adverse impact on livelihoods; potential for displacement; investments spread across 3 countries; and coordination of activities at the national and regional levels without current E&S capacity. Based on the screening, the completion of rehabilitation of Fort Charlotte can be classified as Moderate. The Environmental and Social Team of the World Bank has assessed the Environmental and Social Standard Performance of the Parent Project and CERC activities as moderately satisfactory, due to ongoing need to maintain and increase capacity within the national PIUs to manage E&S risks. The CERC activities under the project are in compliance with the ESSs, Finance Agreement (FA), and ESCP obligations. This AF is aiming to undertake the completion of the rehabilitation of Fort Charlotte. The environmental risk is Substantial. Risks related to investments aiming to improve selected tourist sites; harvest and post-harvest fisheries facilities; construction of a waste separation, recovery, and treatment center; and construction of a composting center are expected to be site-specific related to (i) waste management, handling, and disposal; (ii) community health and safety; (iii) workers occupational, health and safety; and (iv) air, water, and noise pollution. However, depending upon their scale and location, construction-related impacts are possible in ecologically sensitive areas both in the terrestrial and marine environments. Activities such as rehabilitation of the natural reef, coral reef development/restoration, tours, and research at Ramsar site which will occur in sensitive areas risk disturbance to Page 7 Official Use Only The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) – Additional Financing Request 2 marine habitat and damage to aquatic ecosystems; impacts to the Ramsar site if tours are poorly managed; and occupational health and safety risks. Risks related to upgrading landfills (to improve the operational condition) include leachate, toxins, and greenhouse gas management. While Fish Aggregating Devises (FADs) can reduce pressure on reefs and improve local food security, FADs can risk making stock vulnerable to overfishing by aggregating individuals together, altering migration routes, and redistributing stocks. Component 1 supports the development of policies, strategies, and plans such as updating the national solid waste management plan, developing national aquaculture policies, and developing a national policy for enhanced management of yachting, cruise shipping, and diving sectors. The potential adverse risks are related to the design and implementation of the policies in relation to terrestrial, coastal, and marine ecosystems. Depending upon the activities to be implemented, E&S risks such as those related to civil work are possible when the CERC is activated. The capacity of the PIUs in the three countries to manage environmental risks is also of concern. The Project's social risk rating is substantial. The main social risks and impacts expected include: (i) dispersed geographical area, which encompasses both onshore and offshore areas of three countries in the OECS, including some small islands and cays that are difficult to reach and can present challenges to monitoring of social standards; (ii) potential restriction of access to fishers, tourism tour operators and yachting sector to areas in which they usually earn a livelihood, due to strengthened regulations and policies; (iii) negative reaction of formal and informal businesses in the three sectors to new policies and standards (e.g., licensing system for tourism operators, phytosanitary standards applicable for those in the fisheries sector, financial framework for waste management) and the potential that they could be cost restrictive; (iv) potential exclusion of some groups from project benefits and activities, particularly access to the MSMEs grant matching scheme, COAST insurance and infrastructure investments, if there is no adequate socialization of project activities, criteria does not account for the wide range of potential beneficiaries including informal businesses, and if no measures are taken to avoid elite capture; (v) potential land acquisition, involuntary resettlement and/or economic displacement at the subproject level, particularly related to infrastructure works under component 2.2; (vi) other limited adverse site-specific impacts derived from construction works; and (vii) community health and safety and labor conditions to be monitored in a wide geographical area. These potential social risks are exacerbated due to the ongoing need to support and maintain the E&S capacity at the country level to assess, manage, and monitor them (some PIU staff are still not hired). Eight Environmental and Social Standards (ESSs) are considered relevant. These are: ESS1, ESS2, ESS3, ESS4, ESS5, ESS6, ESS8 and ESS10. An Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF) has been prepared for the Parent Project and that will apply to the AF activities, to assess and manage the environmental and social risks and impacts covering different typologies of activities. All potential activities will be screened based on the criteria developed in the ESMF and the level of due diligence to be undertaken will be identified, such as undertaking environmental and social impact assessments or developing environmental and social management plans. The project demonstrated ability to apply the E&S instruments during implementation of the CERC activities (Component 3), which was completed in March 2025. The ESMF includes a section to cover Component 3 Contingent Emergency Response. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 17. The SOP is implemented at the national and regional level under a joint framework. The participating countries are Grenada, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, with the OECS Commission as the regional entity. The implementation arrangements are designed to ensure clear ownership of SOP components, proper oversight, and strengthened regional and inter-ministerial coordination. At the national level, the Ministry of Finance of Saint Lucia, Ministry of Mobilisation, Implementation and Transformation in Grenada, and the Ministry of Tourism, Civil Aviation, Sustainable Development and Culture (MTSD) in SVG will be responsible for implementation through national Project Implementation Units, in close collaboration with the implementing line ministries for each sector.
