Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Yemen’s prolonged conflict has devastated living conditions and exhausted household coping mechanisms. Since 2015, real GDP has contracted by 43 percent, pushing Yemen from one of the world’s poorest countries to the third poorest by 2024. Poverty now affects at least 74 percent of the population, and food insecurity remains widespread, with nearly 19.5 million people requiring humanitarian assistance. The conflict has fragmented the country into two economic and administrative zones under the Houthis and the Internationally Recognized Government (IRG), severely weakening public institutions, service delivery, and economic management. The IRG- controlled areas experienced acute data, systems, and capacity losses as well. Parallel institutions, dual public administrations, and the oil export blockade have undermined fiscal revenues, strained public finances, and disrupted salary payments. This fragmentation has intensified regional disparities and heightened fiscal and macroeconomic instability. 2. In the absence of a political settlement, strengthening core state functions remains critical to sustaining essential services and laying foundations for recovery. Incremental, pragmatic investments in official statistics, public financial management, payroll, and cash management systems, including Treasury Single Account (TSA) arrangements, can improve accountability, fiscal sustainability, and service delivery. Strengthening these systems also supports future peace negotiations and helps prevent a downward cycle in which institutional weakness and economic fragmentation mutually reinforce each other. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. The decade-long conflict has severely disrupted Yemen’s official statistical system and core government functions. The Central Statistics Organization (CSO) has been underfunded and split between Sana’a and Aden Page 2 Official Use Only The World Bank Modernizing Public Institutions and Systems in Yemen (P512475) since 2019, eroding capacity and leaving CSO-Aden with limited experience beyond data collection for partner- funded humanitarian and food security projects. While CSO-Aden adopted a 2023 strategy under the 1995 Statistics Act and has reactivated ministry statistical units, launched the 2026 Household Budget Survey (HBS), and revived the Statistical Training Center, it operates with minimal IRG funding that barely covers operations and staff wages. As a result, Yemen’s system of national accounts (SNA) has effectively collapsed: no official national accounts have been compiled by CSO-Aden or Sana’a since 2014; data collection from enterprises is absent; regular statistical business processes (administrative records, collection, processing, quality control, dissemination) are not in place; and donor funding declines have reduced alternative data sources. 4. Public financial management (PFM) and human resource management (HRM) have also been undermined. In IRG areas, the 2019 budget serves as the legal basis for spending, but fragmentation has fueled extrabudgetary transactions, unauthorized revenue retention and hiring, weak cash forecasting, and poor commitment controls, resulting in arrears and dependence on the Central Bank of Yemen (CBY) in Aden. The TSA is severely compromised, with over 121,000 bank accounts in IRG-controlled areas complicating consolidation, while paper- based validation persists despite automated procedures in the accounting manual. The IRG Ministry of Finance (MoF) priorities include a basic financial management information system (FMIS) with an updated chart of accounts and full TSA implementation to restore budget comprehensiveness, consolidate revenue, control expenditures, improve procurement, manage debt, and enhance transparency. Civil service management and _For Official Use Only_ payroll are fragmented and largely manual after losing access to the Unified Payroll System (UPS). IRG rebuilt UPS that now covers about 90 percent of pre-2014 civil servants in IRG areas, but workflows rely on paper and Excel, there is no authoritative employee master database, and discrepancies between civil service and payroll records persist. Although 95 percent of IRG civil servants are reportedly paid via bank accounts and biometric IDs are being issued to reduce fraud, there is no integrated interface among spending units, Ministry of Civil Service and Insurance (MoCSI), MoF, and CBY-Aden; payroll data do not fully capture contractual or deceased staff or locations; the wage bill is fiscally unsustainable at about 62 percent of IRG expenditures; and borrowing from CBY-Aden has created arrears and de facto debt. In Houthi-controlled areas, salary payments remain sporadic and partial, and resuming public sector salaries is a recurrent priority in UN-led peace talks. C. Proposed Development Objective(s) Development Objective(s) (From PAD) 5. To strengthen the public statistical, financial and human resource systems and management. The project will lay the foundation to modernize key core state functions to enable service delivery and foster economic development. Developing the system of national accounts and managing public finances and human resources, including through an accurate, digitized payroll, are key to adequate public resource management, as well as service delivery to citizens and businesses. 6. The project implementation will adopt an incremental approach to adapt to country context. The scope of the activities can be adjusted according to authorizing environment during project implementation. The project will focus on improving the basic functions through adjustable capacity building. The project will support the implementation of foundational information technology (IT) systems and infrastructure, focusing on a few key processes—which could then be expanded to other functions or government entities, depending on what the evolving country context allows. The project will harness innovative solutions to both adapt to country context (e.g., power and connectivity challenges, online training) and support the IRG to leapfrog when possible. The achievement of the PDO will be measured by the following proposed indicators: (a) Improved country statistical systems, measured in percentage based on Yemen’s score in selected indicators of the World Bank’s Statistical Performance Indicator (SPI); (b) Improved PFM transparency, measured through the timely publication of quarterly consolidated budget execution reports; (c) Improved civil service records management and payroll controls, measured in percentage of public sector employees registered in the new payroll system and paid direct to bank accounts; and (d) Improved capacity in PFM and adherence to World Bank financing requirements, measured by the number of public sector employees certified in PFM, fiduciary management, procurement, environmental and social safeguards, and M&E, disaggregated by gender (total number, number of women in the cohort). D. Project Description 8. The operation will lay the foundations to modernize core state functions through a modular, fit-for-context _For Official Use Only_ program focused on: (i) rebuilding Yemen’s official statistical system with emphasis on the SNA; (ii) strengthening core PFM and HRM/payroll systems to restore basic budget execution, cash and commitment control, and traceable salary payments; and (iii) project management, coordination, and implementation support. Initial implementation will prioritize areas under the IRG, with designs and systems structured for phased expansion should a political settlement enable broader coverage. The approach emphasizes incremental roll-outs, basic process re-design, and appropriate technology to “leapfrog�? gaps (e.g., cloud-enabled solutions to mitigate data loss and improve continuity), while maintaining strong fiduciary, environmental, and social (E&S), and monitoring arrangements through UN implementation modalities and third-party monitoring where needed. The Project consists of three components: 9. Component 1: Strengthening Yemen’s Statistical System and System of National Accounts (US$7.75 million). The components address critical gaps that prevent Yemen from producing credible national accounts: large data gaps and the absence of standardized statistical business processes for key economic indicators; weak data sharing and inconsistent classifications across producers; and limited capacity to compute and compile indicators. It prioritizes foundational, high-impact investments where next steps are clear, while using proxies for remaining SNA inputs until further funding is available: ▪ Subcomponent 1.1 strengthens the statistical system through: on-the-job and foundational training aligned with the Generic Statistical Business Process Model (GSBPM), English/programming upskilling, and an official statistics course at the University of Aden; modernization of physical/IT infrastructure (secure data center, hybrid solar power, connectivity, hardware/software, and systems support with study visits to a peer national statistics office); and improved governance/coordination (stakeholder engagement, annual and five-year statistical plans, and budget dialogue with MoF for SNA sustainability). ▪ Subcomponent 1.2 develops the SNA and its building blocks by: providing technical assistance (TA) to update the 1995 Statistics Act to ensure independence, confidentiality, mandatory reporting, and data-sharing for future adoption; creating an SNA Steering Committee and a 10-year SNA strategy with release calendars; adopting international classifications (International Standard Industrial Classification for industries, Page 4 Official Use Only The World Bank Modernizing Public Institutions and Systems in Yemen (P512475) Classification of Individual Consumption According to Purpose for consumption, Central Product Classification for products) and enabling legislation; digitizing administrative data flows and expanding core data sources (business register with unique IDs, sectoral surveys, final HBS phase), with gender-disaggregated indicators when feasible; applying innovative geospatial/machine learning techniques for hard-to-reach sectors; and building compilation capacity (embedding experts, training on SNA 2008/transition to 2025, Supply-Use Tables, deflation/benchmarking) with manuals, source/method tables, revision and quality policies, and performance-based incentives to accelerate publication. 10. Component 2: Strengthening core PFM and HRM/payroll functions and systems (US$7.75 million) . This component establishes the foundations for stronger public finance and payroll management by: (i) supporting sequenced reform roadmaps; (ii) clarifying and institutionalizing budget preparation/execution, expenditure controls, and payroll processes; and (iii) deploying FMIS/HRMIS modules. An ongoing World Bank-executed assessment with MoF/MoCSI financed by the World Bank’s Resilience, Recovery, and Reconstruction Umbrella Trust Fund (the Yemen Fund) guides design and sequencing. ▪ Subcomponent 2.1 (PFM) provides TA to MoF and the forthcoming governance, oversight, and institutional building borking group to adopt a reform roadmap covering cash management, TSA, macro-fiscal forecasting, debt/arrears, and spending controls; updates manuals, decrees, chart of accounts, and reporting; and builds _For Official Use Only_ capacity across MoF, CBY, and selected spending units. ▪ Subcomponent 2.2 (HRM/payroll) supports MoCSI to craft an HRM roadmap; standardizes and documents core HR processes and minimum data (position, identity, pay, bank details); assesses fiscal implications of arrears/retirements/contractual; and strengthens capacity in pilot ministries (e.g., health/education) and governorates. ▪ Subcomponent 2.3 finances enabling IT—context-appropriate FMIS/HRMIS modules and infrastructure (secure cloud/hybrid data solutions, data center, hardware, solar power, networks, sustainable connectivity)—to streamline payroll, cash, and commitment controls, and internal controls, with business process simplification built into design. 11. Component 3: Project Management, Coordination, and Readiness for Localization (US$4.5 million) : ▪ Subcomponent 3.1 finances UNDP’s implementation costs, including project management, fiduciary, audit, environmental and social (E&S) responsibilities, monitoring and evaluation (M&E), and a project-level grievance mechanism. ▪ Subcomponent 3.2 strengthens readiness for potential future direct implementation by the IRG of donor financing by training CSO-Aden, MoF, and MoCSI (in coordination with MoPIC) on fiduciary, project management, M&E/change management, and Environmental and Social Framework (ESF); and by tasking UNDP to develop a plan to incrementally involve counterpart staff in project functions. It also funds change-management and coalition-building support to address non-technical bottlenecks, identify champions, and build consensus for reforms. The project will modernize Yemen’s public sector systems—statistical capacity, PFM, and HRM/payroll through technical assistance, capacity building, IT infrastructure, and systems development, including limited solar panel installations. With no civil works or direct service delivery, overall environmental and social risks are rated moderate. Key environmental risks relate to battery and e-waste management and disposal; social risks include potential exclusion from benefits, Sexual Exploitation and Abuse/Sexual Harassment (SEA/SH), data protection concerns, labor and working conditions, weak stakeholder engagement, inadequate worker grievance mechanisms, and fragile, conflic, and violence (FCV)-related security and access constraints. Risk mitigation measures will be embedded in procurement and implementation: E&S requirements in bidding documents and contracts; a dedicated grievance mechanism with time-bound resolution and appeals; an SEA/SH Action Plan with _For Official Use Only_ mandatory Codes of Conduct and training; robust data privacy protocols; labor management measures incorporated in the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP) and Project Operation Manual (POM); and a Security Management Plan aligned with the United Nations Department of Safety and Security (UNDSS) and the Saving Lives Together framework. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 13. The project will be implemented under para 3(b) of the Policy on Development Cooperation and Fragility, Conflict, and Violence, i.e., at the request of the international community, as properly represented by UN agencies, and subject to the prior approval of the World Bank Group's Board of Executive Directors, with UNDP serving as the grant recipient and implementing agency. UNDP was selected through a rigorous Implementing Agency Assessment of technical capacity and readiness and, under the Financial Management Framework Agreement (FMFA) and Alternative Procurement Arrangements (APA), will use its own financial regulations and procurement procedures aligned with The World Bank’s Procurement Framework (Section III.F). UNDP will engage national and local institutions as implementing partners under Bank-approved terms, assess and ensure their technical and fiduciary capacity (including experienced personnel), and retain full fiduciary responsibility and accountability for procurement conducted by these partners. The World Bank’s fiduciary and safeguard teams will work closely with UNDP, reviewing and approving procurement plans and updates, requiring periodic procurement reporting, and conducting at least two implementation support missions annually.
