STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Sierra Leone’s 7.8 million people have now enjoyed nearly two decades of peace and relatively steady development, but alongside very high levels of poverty and inequality . Since the end of the civil conflict, key state institutions have been rebuilt; four elections at national and local levels have been held; and these have resulted in two peaceful handovers of the Presidency between the two main parties. The economy recorded steady average per capita gross domestic product (GDP) growth of 5.9 percent from 2002 to 2014. However, overall poverty and inequality remain high and GDP per capita at current US$ was only US$530 in 2019. What wealth there is, is concentrated: the bottom 40 percent of the population enjoy only 20 percent of the total household consumption. Poverty is also unequally distributed with rates of poverty in rural areas that are more than twice as high as those in urban areas (73.9 percent versus 34.8 percent);1 and the largest reduction in poverty has occurred in urban areas around the capital, leaving rural areas even further behind. 2. In this context, human development sectors have seen a trend of increased financing of inputs, but the Government's current fiscal position means that scope for additional financing is limited . A combination of government and donor funding has significantly raised spending on health and education over time. Current health expenditure as a percentage of the GDP has increased from 9.9 percent in 2008 to 11 percent in 2020. Over this period, total per capita spending on health has doubled in real terms, as has government expenditure on health. Education spending as a percentage of GDP has increased from 2.4 percent in 2008 to 22 percent in 2020; and the share of overall public spending on education has doubled, from 14.9 percent in 2008 to just over 33 percent in 2018. However, the cumulative effects of a collapse in global prices for Sierra Leone's main commodity exports, the Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) epidemic (both 2015-16), and the current Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) have dramatically affected the Government's fiscal position. 3. While Sierra Leone’s economic growth rebounded after the Ebola epidemic, COVID -19 worsened macroeconomic conditions with the economy contracting by 2.2 percent in 2020.2 The fiscal deficit and public debt increased sharply. On the supply side, the services sector shrank by 11 percent due to the combined adverse effect of international and domestic restrictions on trade, travel and tourism. The fall in gross fixed inves tment and net exports due to the disruption of global trade explained the contraction of growth on the demand side. Inflation stayed in double digits as nonfood prices declined due to lower demand. Fiscal deficit almost doubled to 6.4 percent of GDP driven mainly by revenue shortfalls (due to the slump in economic activities) and health-related spending pressures to respond to the pandemic. Public debt increased sharply, largely reflecting the increase in fiscal deficits financed by additional domestic loans. However, the current account deficit narrowed to 15 percent of GDP on the back of increased official transfers to support the Government’s response to the COVID -19 pandemic, which also increased gross external reserves. Expenditure by administrative units reveals strong commitments to education, infrastructure, and health but also the adverse impact of domestic debt on recurrent expenditures. 4. Sierra Leone scores 0.36 in the 2020 Human Capital Index (HCI), 3 which is lower than the regional average for Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and the average for low-income countries. The country has some of the most inefficient health and education spending in the world in terms of turning inputs into outputs using a product ion-possibility frontier analysis. In the absence of significant sources of new funds, the focus must now be on seeking to improve the effectiveness of existing spending. Improvements in basic service delivery link directly to Sierra Leone's political and security situation. If poor human development outcomes continue indefinitely, they will generate avoidable human misery and impede economic development; as well as the risk of undermining citizens’ already fragile trust in governing institutions. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. Local councils (LCs) have a significant role to play in delivering basic services. Since 2004, reinvigorated LCs have progressively taken on numerous formal legal and administrative functions regarding the management of basic services. LCs are now formally responsible for the delivery of a range of services, including: primary and junior secondary education, primary and secondary health care, rural water, sanitation, solid waste management, agriculture, youth services, social assistance services, and firefighting services (See Annex 4 for more details). Central- level ministries and their deconcentrated local offices theoretically retain responsibility mostly for sector strategic planning, setting standards, quality control, and monitoring of service delivery. LCs therefore also have an important 'convening' role, given their position as both the democratic representatives of local communities, and as institutions with a formal mandate to deliver services in partnership with other public sector agencies . 6. However, the multi-level governance4 that currently characterizes the policy and administrative framework governing basic services delivery leaves LCs caught between contradictory laws, rules, and regulations. Successive basic services policy reforms have been laid on top of each other without due regard for overall system-level consistency. Policy decisions over recent years have decentralized some functions, while recentralizing others , leading to a complex multi-level governance arrangement. Different elements of basic services delivery (e.g., staff, operational funding, capital investment, procurement of key commodities, data collection and reporting) are each under the authority of different organizations at different levels of government. This results in an unclear and sometimes conflictual relationship between LCs, the deconcentrated offices of sector ministries, Chiefdom administrations, and central government. Unsurprisingly, accountability for results is reduced when responsibility is unclear. 7. Government has re-committed to resolving these policy issues and has announced a number of reforms to clarify and strengthen the role of LCs and for the use of better data in basic service delivery. The Government has explicitly recognized the role that LCs have to play in delivering the basic services ambitions that are set out in the 2019 – 2023 Medium-Term National Development Plan (MTNDP) vision. It has announced a series of reforms designed to clarify and streamline the roles and responsibilities of different agencies involved in service delivery and set out a policy reform process to do so. Within this reform agenda, three key issues stand out regarding LCs’ ability to support basic service delivery. These represent areas where the change has remained limited, including where the Government has committed to reform in principle, but would benefit from additional expertise and/or financing to accelerate the delivery of change. Improvements in these areas would also lead to notable systems improvement regardless of the speed of progress in other areas of legal and policy reform. The challenges in these areas are also interrelated and collectively work against effective local service delivery. They comprise: a. Ongoing institutional weakness of LC financial management (FM) and planning process. It reduces the attractiveness of LCs as the route for central government to invest in basic services. b. Several diagnostic reports note long-term improvements in the LC FM and planning system, albeit from a low base. The preceding World Bank decentralization project (Decentralized Service Delivery Program (DSDP 2) used a composite tool to measure institutional capability in LCs. It registered an increase in aggregate scores from 42 percent in 2012 to over 60 percent in 2017 (although this remained below the target level for the project). LCs have made progress in strengthening their core financial systems as demonstrated by audit results where 99 percent of LC accounts were submitted for audit, and 69 percent of these were unqualified, over the period 2012 and 2018. The 2018 Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability Assessment (PEFA) notes that the core formula-based allocation system by which central government provides regular grants to LCs for their operations is well designed, scoring an ‘A’. 10. A number of FM and planning system weaknesses remain, however, that directly affect the ability of LCs to deliver their local development mandates. Local-level planning is weak, with a disconnect between councils and deconcentrated service-delivery agencies in drawing up their respective local plans. The execution of budgets to finance service delivery is untimely, erratic, and bureaucratic, leading to ‘stop -go’ financing and unnecessary delays in disbursement. Central- level processes for the procurement of key commodities for education and health are not standardized and do not use technology-enabled solutions. The national financial system that tracks LC expenditure does not run live, leading to entry errors, gaps in reporting, and updating delays. LC financial systems are not well- suited to supporting local revenue collection and do not align with national revenue-raising policies. Financial audit performance has improved, but LC s’ response to audit recommendations is poor and there are no performance audits at the local level. 11. There are also weaknesses in the capability of LC staff who manage these processes. The FM and planning processes above are predominantly delivered by core council staff who work directly for the council and are paid from its own funds. The Local Government Service Commission (LGSC) has completed a verification exercise of the core staff of LCs and found that there is a mismatch between staff qualifications and positions. Although there are relatively few vacancies (93 percent of sanctioned core staff were in place within LCs), the lack of clear systems and processes for staff recruitment, transfers, promotion, and targeted trainings have served as a demotivating factor for staff at the local level. This has negatively impacted on the ability of LCs to effectively deliver on their core administrative functions. Overall, there are gaps in the capability of existing staff working in LCs that makes operating effective FM and planning systems even more challenging. In absolute terms, the discretionary financing available to councils to deliver their local development mandates is extremely limited. Development (capital) spending by councils is negligible and has declined in recent years. Only US$6 million in development spending was available for all councils in 2015, and this amount dropped to just over US$2 million in 2018. Even the small, budgeted transfers are typically under-executed: for example, in 2019 only just over half (Le 4.5 billion or US$0.56 million) of the total allocation (Le 7.65 billion or just under US$1 million) for development grants was actually transferred to councils. This equated to development expenditure of roughly US$0.07 per capita in that year. Non-salary recurrent fiscal transfers are small, and councils have limited control over them. In 2019, actual recurrent transfers for expenditure in health and education (excluding centrally procured key commodities like teaching and learning materials (TLMs) and medical supplies) were US$13 million – less than US$2 per capita. Furthermore, most central government funding to councils is now subject to a ‘pass -through’ to service delivery units (SDUs) (primarily education). This means that councils have no effective control over this expenditure. Direct donor support to councils comprises only a few million dollars over the past three-to-four years and is heavily concentrated in Freetown. Figure 2. LC development expenditure, Figure 3. In relative terms, the share of basic services funding going to LCs does not reflect their policy responsibilities. Analysis for the years 2015-2018 suggests that around 90 percent of spending occurring at the local level in Sierra Leone was happening outside the authority of LCs. The vast majority of expenditure on local services goes to fund the salaries of staff working in health, education, agriculture, and water services. These staff are managed and paid by central ministries and not by the LCs who remain ostensibly responsible for delivery of many of these functions. In recent years, overall levels of transfers to LCs have been a very small proportion of total expenditure, and a tiny proportion of GDP. There is a substantial mismatch between the formal role of councils in monitoring service delivery and their ability to do so. Existing mandates for LC service delivery give them a clear role in overseeing, monitoring, and reporting on local services delivery; working in partnership with central ministries who are supposed to set service standards and retain overall responsibility for sector performance. This monitoring function is also in line with LCs' democratic role representing the interests of local people in their area. However, in practice, LCs struggle to deliver this role: • There is effectively no funding for LCs to deliver their monitoring mandate. Low levels of financing for LCs, as discussed above, means that their critical functions of oversight, inspection, and supervision of service delivery are heavily constrained, or simply not delivered. • Existing systems for service delivery monitoring are weak. There are weak national PFM systems in place to track the resources transferred to SDUs, with all aspects of performance information for service delivery scoring a ‘D’ in the 2018 PEFA. This means that neither central - nor local-level institutions receive consistent and comprehensive information on what is being delivered as a result of public services spending. • Oversight tracking systems for key service delivery inputs are insufficient. Public Expenditure Tracking Surveys (PETS) completed in 2018 found that only 56 percent of facilities charged with managing distribution of agricultural, health, and education key commodities kept complete records. Partly as a result of this, the same survey found that 42 percent of centrally procured but locally delivered textbooks could not be located; and 54 percent of medical supplies did not ultimately reach their destination. 15. These three factors negatively reinforce each other, leading to a low-output equilibrium. The absence of any substantial funding for their local development role means that LCs have little incentive to invest in their FM and planning systems. The lack of investment in such systems means councils are not well equipped to perform the local development roles that they do currently have, including the critical role of monitoring and reporting on service Page 12 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) delivery performance. This reduces the attractiveness of LCs as potential recipients of additional financing and policy responsibility; and makes long-term investment in the quality and quantity of the LC core workforce a low priority. As a result, LCs receive relatively little funding or policy attention – and the cycle continues. At a political level, this low- output equilibrium provides convenient justification for the historic ambivalence shown by many governments to the overall value of decentralized government and of LCs as primarily responsible for local development and basic services. If FM and planning systems at both the supportive central and local levels can be improved, then LCs will have a greater ability to use their financing effectively. Investment in stronger monitoring systems and data tracking will help LCs to better deliver basic services as well as raising their importance in the eyes of other service-delivery bodies. 17. C. Relevance to Higher-Level Objectives 18. The project is fully aligned with the Country Partnership Framework (CPF FY21-26, Report No. 148025-SL). The CPF, which was presented to the Board of Executive Directors on June 5, 2020, emphasizes accountable governance in the use of public finances as a core focus area, in addition to governance being a cross -cutting theme across the country engagement. The CPF itself notes that efficiency of existing spending on education and health sectors has been low; and that unless these are addressed, deploying further resources towards the MTNDP’s human capital agenda will risk poor value for money. The project will support the CPF’s stated goals under Focus Area 1 on Sustainable Growth and Accountable Governance (Improve government accountability for results in use of public finances) and Focus Area 2 on Human Capital Acceleration for Inclusive Growth ( Deliver quality and inclusive education and health services). The project will build on the existing program of World Bank support to PFM reforms and other accountability systems at the central level by expanding it to the local level. 19. The project’s emphasis on improving local government effectiveness in basic service delivery addresses a critical gap and complements other operations in the country portfolio . During CPF preparation, local government officials and civil society organizations (CSOs) pointed to the gaps left by the discontinued World Bank projects on decentralization and service delivery. These gaps will be addressed through this new project. In addition, it will complement the recent Board-approved Free Basic Education Project (P167897), the pipeline Quality Essential Health Services and Systems Support Project (P172102), and the Resilient Urban Sierra Leone (RUSL) Project (P168608). The components will be delivered in a phased and incremental approach over the project lifecycle to maximize impact, manage risk, and promote sustainability . The proposed operation is designed to dynamically evolve over the six-year timeframe of the project (see Figure 9, under section on project sustainability). The project's impact will be maximized by progressively strengthening institutional systems as a benchmark condition for progressively larger grants to support local development. This dynamic process offers a growing financial incentive for councils to engage with the technical assistance (TA) support through the project to further strengthen and maintain systems and processes over the course of five grant cycles. Risks are managed within this approach by ensuring that councils with sufficiently strong systems and a good track record of delivery and reporting are able to access the largest development grants in the later years of the project. Equity concerns are addressed by primarily focusing on the rural district councils for the local development grants, 9 where there is a higher concentration of the poor and systems remain weak across councils. Increased transparency and availability of data will be an additional key output area for the project, by mobilizing community participation and citizen engagement. The phased nature of the project results means that notable positive impacts on strengthening institutions for service delivery can be achieved even while the Government is taking forward legal and policy reforms in the area of local government and basic services delivery. 10 There is flexibility, however, built into the project to adapt to engage with a revised policy and legislative framework. 11 29. C. Project Beneficiaries 56. The principal beneficiaries of the project will be poor Sierra Leoneans who rely on publicly delivered basic services, notably the disproportionately higher numbers of citizens living in poverty outside urban areas. Poverty in Sierra Leone is concentrated in rural areas, with the estimated rural poverty rate (73.9 percent) over twice that of urban areas (34.8 percent) in 2020. Indeed, rural areas have seen no particular change in poverty rates in the period 2011- 2018, and the greatest declines in poverty have been in urban areas outside Freetown. Citizens in rural areas are most likely to be dependent on publicly provided basic services given the lack of a private market for such services outside of major cities, and lack of household income to purchase services if they are available. Improved basic services delivery by all councils will therefore be pro-poor by disproportionately benefiting the poorest citizens living in rural areas. F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 66. This project builds on many years of World Bank investment in LC and basic services delivery which provides numerous opportunities to learn lessons . The completed IRCBP and DSDP 1 and 2 projects, combined with the successive PFM reform programs and ongoing relevant sector projects (particularly RUSL and Free Basic Education (FREE)), provide a rich set of experiences and lessons learned that have informed the design of this project. Reviewing the lessons from over many years of World Bank-financed inputs into local government reform and service delivery suggests the following: • A long-term and carefully phased approach to local governance reform pays off (IRCBP; DSDP 1; DSDP 2) . This program intends to continue the previous investment in making local government work in Sierra Leone. The project itself builds on an early predecessor project (IRCBP, that ended in 2010). The Implementation Completion and Results Report (ICR) for DSDP, which was less focused on institutional systems strengthening of LCs than the IRCBP, further noted that the short timeframes for project implementation set unrealistic goals. This project is due to last for six years, providing a longer timescale, even in its design, over which to deliver its results. • There needs to be a focus on central-local level linkages in policy and administration for successful improvement in service delivery (FREE). In many ways, the project is a direct response to this realization. Several components are directly aimed at improving the way central and local financial and administrative systems interact. • Sharing data between levels of government and different agencies involved in service delivery using technological solutions can have a significant positive impact (DSDP 2). The components of the project focusing Page 31 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) on improved data management and integration of local and central data systems respond directly to this conclusion. • The PMU deliberately supports institutional strengthening of existing government systems rather than creating parallel institutional structures. The project management arrangements for the project have been designed to integrate with existing arrangements and avoid unnecessary duplication of key functions. This will allow for cost savings on implementation, a faster start to program implementation, and encourage the realization of positive synergies between the PMU and key implementing agencies. Past World Bank projects have used, for instance, comprehensive tools of LC performance monitoring that were complicated, project-specific, never institutionalized, and ceased to be implemented by government with the close of project financing. • Capacity development needs to be carefully sequenced and peer-exchange, peer-coaching, 'exchange visits' and circulation of effective staff between LCs can be an effective and relatively low-cost approach to developing capability (DSDP 1 and DSDP 2). The project will support capacity development of existing staff at the LCs and within select supporting MDAs at the central level, such as in the NPPA and in ASSL. The project design has consequently deliberately reduced significant primary dependence on externally recruited consultant firms to implement key activities within the project, but instead to build the technical skills of existing officials within government. • Community participation at the ward level builds ownership over World Bank-funded council-delivered interventions. The project activities aimed at improving local development planning (Sub-component 1.1), supporting community-driven development subprojects through project-financed local development grants (Component 2), and improving data systems engaging with citizens (Component 2), all contain elements that aim to strengthen the links between communities (including Ward Development Committees (WDC)) and LCs or other government management information systems. • Pairing of governance improvements with development grants from other global experience provides positive, important lessons. The World Bank has engaged in several projects in different types of country contexts that use a performance-incentive on institutional strengthening for unlocking additional local financing. The impact has been powerful in contexts with significantly more complex arrangements as well, especially as the number of subnational units within such projects can be, in many cases, several times more than in Sierra Leone. 16 Two key challenges get highlighted also based on the lessons learnt: First, the implementation arrangements can be over-designed with a large number of performance-indicators tracked, which complicate the transparency and credibility of the process. Second, the use of an outsourced independent verification agency (IVA) is sometimes deemed necessary because of ensuring timely coverage in the grant cycle of several subnational governments and in order to manage the project performance-assessment tool for local governance; however, the expense of the IVA and monitoring arrangements is less mainstreamed into government M&E systems and consequently less sustainable beyond the project. The project design here has incorporated elements from the review of similar World Bank experiences and has adapted to the country context of fewer LCs in Sierra Leone. 16 See, Bangladesh Local Governance Support Project (LGSP-1, 2006-2010; LGSP-2, P124514, 2011-2017), which covered 484 Upazila Parishads (Sub-districts); West Bengal Support to Institutional Strengthening of the Gram Panchayat Program - Phase II (P159427), 2017- 2022, similarly, uses an Annual Performance Assessment to cover a large number of sub -national units, based on the positive experience of Phase-1 of the program. Within Africa, recent examples include the Ghana Local Government Capacity Support Project (P122692) that tracked a performance score, similar to other projects in Tanzania, Kenya and Uganda. Some of the most powerful examples of institutional strengthening supported by the World Bank have been in the Latin America region – see, ICRs for the Bolivia Participatory Rural Investment Project (P040085), Dominican Republic Municipal Development Project (P095863), which adopted the phased, simple institutional strengthening approach similar to this project. C. Sustainability 76. At a political level, the likelihood of sustainability of the project's benefits is strengthened given their alignment with the Government's own objectives. The present Government has invested a significant amount of its political capital in improved basic service delivery, notably through its flagship 'Free Quality Education' initiative. This suggests a high degree of political commitment to the project's overall ambition of making basic service delivery systems work better at local level. More specifically, the Government has publicly committed to several reform strategies that are aligned with specific components of this project. Reform of the local government legal and policy environment is committed to under the draft 2020 National Decentralization Policy. The MTNDP includes a range of commitments to basic services delivery financing and improved local government, including better prioritization of sector spending. The government's establishment of NaMED and its focus on using technology-enabled national monitoring systems to drive better service delivery is reflected in components of this project that seek to improve the dissemination and use of data for local services delivery. The current national PFM strategy contains specific commitments on reform to LC PFM systems in the context of better national systems, both of which are core focus areas of this project. 77. The project explicitly aims to build better systems for local services governance for the long -term, and not simply provide one-off injections of finance or expertise . The specific aim of several components is to improve the application of laws, regulations, practices, and data flows so as strengthen the overall local services delivery governance environment. The positive benefits of these new processes, combined in many cases with technology-enabled ease of operation, are likely to be sufficient to incentivize government to maintain them after the project has ended and therefore ensure sustainability of benefits. This sustainability incentive applies also to the time-limited development grant financing element of the project. While the provision of World Bank-financed project grants is expected to last for the duration of the project, these grants are explicitly conditional on fulfilment of eligibility criteria that incentivize improvements in the underlying systems that manage these grants. As these grants are expected to run over five or six annual cycles, these improved systems will have a chance to embed fully in daily operations and will continue to provide benefits even after the World Bank-financed element of local development grants finish. The Government’s own revisions to the Local Development Grants mechanism under a new IGFTS realized within a new Fiscal Decentralization Policy (supported under this Sub-component 2.2), will also ensure continuity of the grants beyond the project timeframe. Emerging evidence from similar operations indicates that the reforms supported by the proposed operation will likely reduce transaction costs associated with the delivery of local services thanks to improved predictability and reliability of transfers from the central government to local governments; improved PFM capacity at the local level resulting from better budget planning and execution and better reporting and accounting practices; and improved local administrative capacity, leading to improved LC res ponsiveness to the needs of citizens. Although the project is expected to contribute to improved service delivery, the benefits accrued are not easily quantified. Data are not available to quantify the direct financial and economic impact of the TA support through the project; however, an economic analysis has been undertaken of the potential benefit of the development grants. Even through the capacity building elements supported through Components 1 and 3, this project should lead to concrete economic impact through the expected improvement of the quality and efficiency of government spending to foster access to public services and, by extension, to human capital development. International experience shows that subnational governments have an important role to play in the implementation of public policies aimed at fostering social and human development. The key argument for reliance on subnational governments for service delivery is that they are closer to the intended beneficiaries of public programs. For this reason, subnational governments are believed to be better equipped and more effective to extract information on local preferences and needs than the central government, and to be more accountable to residents. With greater voice in the design and implementation of public policies, residents can also benefit from greater choice in the goods and services delivered to them by the government. This is because of risks associated with the political and governance, current institutional capacity for implementation, and fiduciary assurance challenges. 100. Political and governance risk is rated Substantial. The government of Sierra Leone is currently reviewing legislation and policy relating to the allocation of roles and responsibilities for service delivery among different levels of government. The intent is to decide reforms that will clarify and confirm the responsibilities of LCs in relation to other levels of government. However, historical experience with government reforms in this area has demonstrated that reform does not consistently lead to decisions that will empower LCs in comparison to other levels of government, for example central ministries and Chiefdom administration. There is a risk, therefore, that Government's review of legal, policy, and administrative mandates for service delivery among different government institutions does not result in further support to strong LCs; and instead rolls back (or fails to advance) the decentralization agenda. To mitigate this risk, the World Bank engagement with the Government includes policy dialogue and support through analytical and advisory activity related to ‘Local Financing and Fiscal Decentralization for supporting Sierra Leone’s NDP’ (P175685). In addition, the project has been designed to strengthen existing institutional arrangements, as they currently exist, at both the local and central levels without a requirement of increased decentralization. 101. Macroeconomic risk is Moderate. Sierra Leone's fiscal situation is fragile following the successive macroeconomic shocks of the fall in commodity prices, the EVD outbreak, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Economic growth predictions have been substantially downgraded, and government debt is rising (although not yet to unsustainable levels). While the Government has taken steps to access emergency financial support and reschedule its official debts, it will remain substantially constrained with regard to fiscal space for the medium term. This creates a risk that – despite investment in better systems through this project – LCs are not provided with any meaningful additional funding to deliver their basic services role. This risks undermining investments in better systems if they are simply not used over future years. The project mitigates this risk by providing financing itself through the development grants components so as to strengthen and use these systems; and by providing support to the development of the next -generation IGFTS as a means of strengthening the architecture of fiscal decentralization. The project timeframe of six years also ensures that that the Government will be supported when it is in most need of supporting local development financing but will have a longer period to overcome the present macro-economic challenges to directly increase support to local development. 102. Sector Strategy and Policy Risk is Moderate. Expanding the PFM reforms that have been underway at the central level to the local level has been a government priority, especially also in the context of the Government’s renewed commitment to education and other basic service delivery under the MTNDP. Decentralization has also been high on the current Government’s agenda, but progress has been mixed. The limited capacity at the local level and poor coordination between national and local governments continues. To mitigate these risks, the project has included, in its design, activities to increase the capacity of LCs (in planning and budgeting, procurement, and budget execution), even while a review of the enabling legislation and the policy frameworks (e.g., revised National Decentralisation Policy, Fisc al Page 42 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) Decentralisation Policy) are pending. FDD, within the MoF, plays an important role in the government’s fiscal decentralization agenda but suffers from weaknesses, such as long delays in notifying LCs about their allocations. To mitigate this risk, the project will provide support to strengthen FDD in undertaking its core activities of finalizing the policy framework of a revised intergovernmental fiscal transfer system and its roll out. 103. The institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability is rated Substantial. The quality of public administration in Sierra Leone is variable. There are islands of excellence, but these exist against a generally low-capacity backdrop. At a high level, Sierra Leone's political settlement only weakly incentivizes delivery of quality public services and effective public administration. Donors have made substantial investments in core state institutions in the post-war era; but outside a limited number of central government agencies and their essential processes there are widespread process inefficiencies, skills shortages, and a general lack of commitment to delivery. This project is specifically designed to actively build capacity among key local government institutions so as to ameliorate this issue in the long term (which has been limited since the close of IRCBP in 2010). The project has structured TA inputs principally to improve government systems and skills to ensure sustainability, while it ensures that external capacity can be accessed and deployed in the delivery of the project, where this is lacking within government institutions. The project design additionally includes both a deliberate aspect of sequencing and phasing to ensure sustainability in financing of institutional systems and capacities to manage resources transparently and accountably on local services. While the institutional capacity and sustainability risks are expected to reduce to Moderate during project implementation, due to the support provided through project activities, it will remain Substantial at the time of project effectiveness. 104. Environmental and social risks are rated Moderate. These risks are considered Moderate because the project’s anticipated risks are not expected to be adverse given that civil works will not be complex and will be limited to upgrading, and rehabilitation of existing public assets with some possibility of new construction in the final years of the project. Environmental and social impacts will largely be site-specific, localized and can be easily managed. However, coordination and capacity for environmental and social management of the existing portfolio under MoF supervision appears to be insufficient, which could pose risks of delays and inadequate supervision and monitoring of project activities in the field. The PMU will include an Environmental Specialist and a Social and Gender specialist, with support from the LC environmental and social officers to monitor, supervise and report safeguards implementation by the contractor on a regular basis. The project has developed an ESCP and a draft SEP. Rehabilitated facilities will include safe and universal designs to accommodate the unique needs of women and persons with disabilities. 105. Fiduciary risk is rated Substantial. This rating is due to: (i) project design and approval require the successful cooperation of several different entities at the central and local levels; (ii) LCs with identified capacity concerns will be primarily responsible to implement the investment subprojects, including undertaking FM tasks and any procurement; (iii) the limited capacity of PFMU staff compared to the workload; and (iv) general weaknesses in Government's procurement capability compared to World Bank standards at the district council level. Project institutional and implementation arrangements 1. The MoF will have the overall responsibility for project implementation with the support of the PMU. The PMU led by a Project Coordinator, will be anchored in the MoF and report to the Financial Secretary. The PMU will directly interact with the technical leading units within MoF and other MDAs, mainly PFMRD for Component 1, FDD for Component 2, and NaMED for Component 3. Coordination with, and among, participating entities/units will be facilitated by a High-level Steering Committee for decision making and oversight, Thematic TWGs to provide hands-on support to the PMU, and focal points formally nominated by each participating entity to support day-to-day implementation. 2. The project will be implemented under the overall leadership of a High-level Project Steering Committee. The Project Steering Committee will be chaired by the Minister of Finance, and it will be composed of representatives of targeted ministries, MoPED, Office of the (NaMED, DSTI), MOLG, and other relevant agencies in charge of executing the project’s activities and representatives of the LCs Association of Sierra Leone (LoCASL). The Project Steering Committee will oversee the project, approve AWPB as well as its progress reports, and ensure that the broader project objectives are being met. 3. At the technical level, the project will have a Technical Committee and Thematic TWGs created for each project component. The Technical Committee will be chaired by the Financial Secretary, and it will include the delegates from the different agencies, with the primary responsibility to facilitate coordination and decisions on key technical matters. TWGs will be chaired by the PMU Coordinator and will include the heads of the leading technical units or its delegates, and focal points from participating entities. The TWGs will be primarily tasked to use monthly meetings to address common challenges of implementation through also participation of key relevant PMU staff. As project implementation progresses, the TWGs can also be used to share experiences and good practices for helping standardized approaches, where possible. 4. The PMU will comprise technical, fiduciary and safeguards specialists to support project implementation. The PMU will be composed of personnel recruited on a competitive basis with ToRs, qualifications, and experience acceptable to the World Bank. Core staff within the PMU will include: a Project Coordinator, a M&E Specialist; Communications Specialist; Safeguard Specialists, and an Operations Specialist to support technical leading units and liaise with focal points in participating entities/units on day-to-day implementation issues. The PMU will also establish a Grant Management team that will support FDD in the implementation of Sub-component 2.1. 5. The PFMU of the MoF will provide fiduciary services to the project. The PFMU was established within the MoF to provide fiduciary services to World Bank-financed projects in Sierra Leone. As such, the PFMU is familiar with World Bank fiduciary requirements and has basic arrangements in terms of processes, procedures and tools. In order to properly and timely support project fiduciary needs, a dedicated fiduciary team with the likelihood of comprising of an experienced FM Specialist, a FM Assistant and an experienced Procurement Specialist would be recruited by the PMU but would functionally report to the PFMU. Page 57 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) 6. LCs are the key project actor. LCs are legally in charge of service provision at the local level; thus, building their capacity is at the heart of this project. The LCs are the direct beneficiaries of the capacity-building activities supported through the project. As such they are expected to actively participate in the implementation and later sustainability of the envisioned reforms, either as direct operators of management systems, or as conveners of local monitoring data. Under Sub-component 2.1 of the Project, the LCs will be in charge of executing the subprojects financed by the development grants. 7. Ward-level structures will help project implementation in communities. LCs and WDCs would be the main mechanisms through which the project interfaces with the community. LCs and WDCs would be responsible for community mobilization, facilitating community planning and other community-level capacity-building initiatives to support project implementations, including participation in social audits for the local development grants. In accordance with Section 95 of the Local Government Act 2004, of the 12 WDCs members, five of them should be women. Such gender representation would help the project to make investments that considers the specific needs of women. Representation of young people would be encouraged in WDCs and in the community mobilization drives. 8. Implementation of Component 1 will be led by the PFMRD of the MoF. PFMRD is a division created to coordinate and oversee the implementation of the PFM Reform Strategy in the country. Systems and skills strengthening for local services requires interventions at central and local levels, and the involvement of several entities/units responsible for each technical area. 9. Implementation of Component 2 will be led by the FDD and LCs will be in charge of executing the subprojects financed by the development grants. FDD supported by the PMU Grant Management team will be responsible for: (i) managing and coordinating the activities required under each grant cycle; (ii) ensuring subprojects’ technical quality and compliance with safeguards and fiduciary requirements; and (iii) providing guidance and oversight throughout the process. At the local level, LCs will be required to designate a grant team comprising technical and fiduciary staff to undertake responsibility of subprojects implementation. LCs will be in charge of the following: (i) leading the district planning process; (ii) identifying investment priorities in a consultative and participatory manner; (iii) carrying out procurement of works, goods and services for investment subprojects; and (iv) managing subproject contracts. 10. Implementation of Component 3 will be led by the NaMED, working in collaboration with other partner MDAs. For the use of simple technology applications and the development of the data platforms under the project component activities, NaMED will partner with the Directorate of Science, Technology and Innovation (DSTI). On education-related data monitoring platforms on TLM and textbooks, NaMED and DSTI would work closely with the M&E units of the MBSSE. For the data platforms on drugs, medical supplies and equipment, NaMED and DSTI will engage closely with the National Medical Supplies Agency and the Ministry of Health and Sanitation (MoHS). For the capacity building and support to LCs M&E Officers, NaMED will partner with the M&E cell of the MoLGR and with the training mandate of the LGSC. Planning and budgeting. Each Annual Work Plan and Budget (AWPB) will be compiled by PFMU with preparation and approval based on the policy and procedural guidelines contained in the PFMU FM Manual incorporated by reference in the PIM, which should be consistent with the provisions of the PFM Act, 2016 and the related Regulations, 2018. Information systems. PFMU will use the TOMPRO accounting system to record project transactions and produce a trial balance as a basis for preparing financial reports. 15. Accounting policies and procedures. Accounting will follow a cash basis and the main accounting policies and procedures will be included in the PFMU FM Manual to provide effective guidance for recording transactions and balances and preparing IFRs and annual financial statements. A chart of accounts will be used to code expenditures consistently and provide for (a) reporting in accordance with requirements; and (b) the effective tracking of budgeted expenditures in the AWPB against actual amounts for each line item. Reporting will be presented in US$. Payments made in SLL will be exchanged to US$ for reporting based on the effective rate of the transfer from the US$ Designated Account to the SLL Project Account based on the first-in-first out basis (i.e., there are no foreign exchange gains or losses). For the use of funds by the sub-grantees these will be reported on a schedule at the same rate applied to the payment of the sub-grant to the project account. 16. Financial reporting and monitoring. PFMU will be responsible for preparing IFRs for each calendar quarter throughout the life of the project. They will be due 45 days after each quarter ends and be in the format provided in the Disbursement and Financial Information Letter (DFIL) and include (a) a statement of sources and uses of funds ; (b) uses of funds by activity; (c) Designated Account statement; and (d) a schedule of use of funds provided under the local development grants component (provided by the Grants Manager in SLL and translated to US$ by PFMU). IFRs should be submitted along with supporting documentation that includes at least the following (a) a register of fixed assets ; (b) bank reconciliation summaries; (c) a schedule of all signed project contracts showing related commitment and payment amounts; and (d) the cashbook for both local currency and US$ accounts. Annual project financial statements will be prepared in accordance with accounting policies acceptable to the World Bank and based on Financial Reporting under the Cash Basis of Accounting issued by the International Public Sector Accounting Standards Board. Statutory financial statements of LCs that have received sub-grants under the project will be required to be prepared within three months of the end of the fiscal year and, consistent with terms to be included in a sub-grant agreement, will include in the notes to these statutory financial statements a summary of expenditure related to the use of sub-grant funds. 17. Internal controls. The internal controls framework will be described in the PFMU FM Manual. Key internal controls to be applied for the project will include (a) appropriate authorizations and approvals of project commitments and transactions; (b) segregation of duties (with no single person having the responsibility for all phases of transaction); (c) Page 60 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) regular reconciliations between records and actual balances, as well as with third parties where relevant; (d) proper safeguard of project assets; and (e) monitoring overall project performance and progress on results including monitoring the actual expenditure against expectations from the approved AWPB. A dedicated Internal Auditor will be recruited to join the PMU on a ToR acceptable to the World Bank and undertake planned internal audit work. An internal audit report will be prepared at least every 6 months and submitted to the PMU, the project Technical Committee and the Bank. The internal audit plan will include coverage of the sub-grantees and procedures will be applied to assess the use of funds in compliance with the sub-grant agreements. 18. External audit. ASSL is by law responsible for the audit of all government finances and projects. However, in view of prevailing capacity constraints, it is possible that the ASSL will elect to outsource th e audit of the project’s accounts to a private firm of auditors. The audit opinion will cover the project’s annual financial statements and the audit will be conducted in accordance with the International Standards of Supreme Audit Institutions or the International Standards on Auditing. Where the audit is outsourced, the PMU will appoint independent auditors acceptable to the World Bank on ToRs acceptable to the World Bank. The external auditor should be appointed prior to the end of the period for which they will audit to allow the auditors sufficient time to complete their audit before the due date. The audited financial statements will be submitted to IDA within six months after the end of each fiscal year. The cost of the audit will be financed from the project proceeds. Project external audit will also include the expenditures incurred under the investment ’ s subprojects financed out of the development grants. LCs have in place basic FM systems following the requirements established in the PFM Act and LG Act. LCs are headed by a Chief Administrative Officer supported by a Financial Officer and Accountant for FM, a Procurement Officer, Internal Auditor, and a Planning and M&E Officer. LCs’ budget is prepared in accordance with the Budget Call Circular issued by the MoF combined with a bottom-up approach involving consultations with local stakeholders. Budget execution is subject to similar requirements followed at the central level, including the use of PETS Forms 1 and 2 for activity requisition and expenditure authorization. Budget execution though is affected by some cumbersome internal procedures and mainly by unpredictable releases from the MoF for devolved functions. 20. Annual financial statements are prepared in accordance with the requirements of the LG Act, 2004, and with the Cash-Basis International Public Sector Accounting Standard (Cash -Basis IPSAS). LCs use the PETRA accounting system, which is functional, but is operated by the Finance Officer on a stand-alone basis; thus, mainly being used for record keeping and not necessarily for transaction processing. The FM capacity of LCs, including internal audit, remains weak. 21. The 2019 Audit Report of the ASSL reports on FM at LCs and underscores the lack of FM capacity and weak internal controls. The report, which was submitted to Parliament in December 2020, identifies significant FM concerns that will be considered through the project design and fiduciary risk-mitigation measures. The report identifies Le5.3b (US$530k) in “cash losses” across the 22 LCs, and comprises mostly (a) fuel not accounted for; (b) withholding taxes not paid to the National Revenue Authority; (c) payments without supporting documentation; and (d) payroll irregularities. Flow of funds and disbursement arrangements. The procedures relating to the flow of funds will be described in detail in the PFMU FM Manual and will clearly outline all steps of the process, as well as specifying authorized signatories for administering bank account transactions. The project will provide for the use of advances to Designated Accounts, reimbursements, direct payment, and special commitments as applicable disbursement methods, and these will be specified in the DFIL. Most funds are expected to be disbursed through advances to Designated Accounts in US$ with unaudited IFRs providing supporting documentation. Once the project becomes effective, two segregated Designated Accounts (US$) will be opened at an acceptable commercial bank, to which the funds will be transferred. One Designated Account will be used exclusively to disburse local development grants under Component 2 of the project and the other Designated Account will be used for all other components/sub-components of the project. The Designated Accounts will be managed and operated by the PFMU along with two matching segregated project accounts (i.e., one for local development grants; one for expenses under other components/sub-components) denominated in Sierra Leonean Leones (SLL). The PFMU will include balances on all project-related bank accounts in the quarterly IFRs. The ceiling for the Designated Accounts is indicated in the DFIL and was agreed on at negotiations. Applications for replenishment of the Designated Accounts will be submitted at least quarterly and should be done more frequently where required. For local development grants, the PMU Grant Manager will provide the PFMU on a quarterly basis a six-month rolling forecast of development grants to be paid, which PFMU will use as a basis for determining the amount of the requested advance to the related US$ Designated Account. Payments made from any bank account will be in accordance with the PFMU FM Manual and in relation to the local development grants also in accordance with an approved Grant Implementation Manual which will require LCs to open a segregated bank account for receipt and use of the development grant. 25. The current context of decentralization in Sierra Leone represents 'work in progress'. Formal decentralized government in Sierra Leone was ended in the 1970s, and the ravages of the prolonged civil conflict in the 1990s destroyed most traces of formal governance systems outside of Freetown and the Western Area. With significant World Bank support, LCs were formally (and in many cases, physically) re-established across the country in 2004, with successive and successful rounds of elections for LCs, mayors or district chairpersons held since then. However, while there is no serious political voice calling for their abolition, local governance in Sierra Leone can also be seen as half completed. The formal legal, policy, and administrative system of local governance is not fully coherent, with unresolved tension regarding the scope of authority of LCs, central government, and Chiefdom administrations. Political decisions by some central government administrations have consciously rolled-back some decentralization reforms. The flow of finance to LCs through various channels is both fractured, in terms of different streams of funding for different responsibilities , and limited in terms of overall amounts reaching LCs. 2. The proposed project attempts to address some of the binding constraints affecting the current decentralization situation, acknowledging other reforms ongoing in the sector . The project’s three main components of: (i) reforming FM and planning systems at the central and local levels; (ii) providing development grants to incentivize use of these systems and to deliver development results; and (iii) improving flows of data between levels of government to support stronger accountability, will collectively tackle many of the most significant outstanding issues within the 'work in progress' institutional context of decentralized governance in Sierra Leone. The project does not attempt to address all legal, administrative, and financial issues within the sector. This is out of recognition that other reform processes are already in place that are addressing these issues. Notably, and as discussed in Annex 4, the legal and administrative mandate and responsibilities of LCs is being addressed through a revised Decentralization Policy and its medium-term implementation. 3. The discussion below outlines challenges in key areas of decentralized governance in Sierra Leone and explains how this project seeks to address them to improve basic services delivery . These areas relate to the: (i) organizational structure and funding streams of local services delivery; (ii) financing that LCs receive through these structures; and (iii) monitoring and tracking of resources and critical inputs for service delivery. These issues are then considered in terms of the project's proposed contribution to their improvement. Organizational structure 4. The organizational structure of local services delivery in Sierra Leone is complex. This architecture has developed organically and iteratively through numerous piecemeal additions, amendments, and changes to law, policy, and legislation since LCs were re-established in 2004. Numerous actors at different levels of government are involved in delivering services. They often have overlapping and/or unclear formal responsibilities for delivering their contribution to basic services delivery. The complexity of the management and accountability chain builds in transactions costs which brings the risk that services are inefficiently delivered. Different institutions in the local services delivery chain have inconsistent mandates, which blur accountability. LCs have formal policy responsibility for basic health and education service delivery in their districts, as mandated by law. Central ministries are supposed to fulfil a small range of functions such as quality oversight, strategic sector planning, and sector policy guidance. In practice key finances and authority required for LCs to actually manage basic health and education – notably payroll and workforce management – have not been devolved and remain with central ministries. Chiefdom administrations have a very limited formal role in delivering basic services but in practice local service planning and delivery benefit from, and in some cases require, their support. New central- and district-level bodies have been added to the delivery structure without formal changes to the mandates of other institutions. Accountability lines suffer within these complex relationships since in the current picture, it is not clear which institution can realistically take on ultimate responsibility for effective basic services delivery. Box A2.2 – Managing education and health services at the local level Structure of the education sector at the local level The delivery of education services at the local level illustrates the challenges in current organizational structures. There is no single document, policy, or guidance note that comprehensively outlines the roles and responsibilities of each institution at local level. Di fferent laws and policies from various time periods prescribe parts of the system, but often contain e lements of contradiction and omission. For example, some institutions are provided for in legislation (e.g., District Education Boards) but are not active in practice. In other cases, new actors have been added (e.g., the Free Quality School Education Coordinators) without full description of how they relate to existing structures. Other ke y institutions that are active at the local level (e.g., district offices of the Teacher Services Council) operate with no formal guidelines or policy documents regarding their role. In practice, education services are indeed being delivered at the local level and local actor s are often able to informally agree a schedule of roles and responsibilities between themselves. However, the current structure of local level education management and governance lead to avoidable duplication, confusion, and tension between actors with s imilar responsibilities. Management of health recurrent funding LCs and DHMTs share responsibility for operational funding of primary and secondary health services within a particular distric t. In a high- capability system with effective supporting syst ems, this might work well so that local citizens’ views (from the LC) are merged with central health ministry objectives and expertise (from the DHMT). In practice, weak coordination among low -capability bodies tends to result in delays to key financial processes. As an example, in practice the period from the approval of first quarter funding release from the finance ministry, through to the DHMT actually withdrawing funds from its account to pay for services, can be up to 15 weeks. 6. In general, there is a pattern of LCs being given policy responsibility for activities over which they have limited effective control. Workforce management and oversight is perhaps the most obvious example of this. Central ministries control the health and education workforce, with both district management and frontline service delivery staff hired and deployed by the central ministry. Given that the health and education workforce are by far the most important inputs to service delivery in both sectors, this leaves LCs with limited levers to manage the services they are ostensibly responsible for. This pattern persists in a number of health and education sub-functions, such as procurement and distribution of drugs and medical equipment, and of TLMs; which are predominantly delivered by institutions other than LCs. Box A2.3 – LC financial audit LCs are audited by the ASSL on an annual basis and local government audit regulations have been progressively strengthened over recent years. The level of ambition for financial audit in councils has been rising. Both the PFM Act (2016) and the PFM Regulations (2018) have, among other things, expanded and increased the audit scrutiny facing LCs. There has been long-term improvement in audit processes and outcomes at the subnational level. In 2019, all councils submitted financial statement for auditing and 17 of these were considered unqualified (77 percent). Although this represents a decline from the previous year, where 20 out of 22 councils (90 percent) received unqualified opinions, it compares well to the period 2005 to 2010. During this time, only 52 per cent of accounts were submitted for audit at all, and only 41 per cent of these received an unqualified opinion. This improvement is due largely to long-term investment in LC FM capacity – much of it from the World Bank. The ‘qualified’ or ‘unqualified’ nature of an audit opinion provides some understanding of the strength of FM in a LC, but other information is also required to inform a full judgement. An unqualified audit opinion does not necessarily mean an institution’ s FM works perfectly. Equally, a qualified audit opinion may be as a result of a lack of institutional capacity such as a lack of competent fiducia ry staff and/or ignorance of the existing legislations and procedures rather than a deliberate intention to m islead. ASSL’s financial audits of LCs in 2019 identified a range of specific issues. Financial audits across all councils identified a cash loss of SLL5.2bn (US$0.5 million) in that year from issues relating to overpayment of sitting fees, ineligible ex penditures, uncollected revenue, withholding taxes not paid to the revenue authority, missing fuel, payments without supporting documents, and other issues. In addition, the audit report noted significant and continued procurement irregularities across all councils relating to lack of documentation, splitting of bids to avoid procurement thresholds, irregularities in the bidding process, and procured items not being fully delivered. The majority of the findings of the latest audit have been recurring almost throughout the entire decentralization period and four issues continue to be noted in a large number of LC audit reports: • Own-source revenue , including lack of collection and poor systems for identifying liability; • Procurement , including lack of documentation to support procurement decisions, as noted above; • Insufficient financial record keeping; and • Ineffective or absent Internal Audit capability. The 2018 ASSL LC audit report concluded that implementation of previous audit re commendations was poor. Less than half of the audit recommendations from the 2017 audits had been fully implemented. Upon publication of the 2018 financial and compliance audit reports, the MoF instructed the FDD of the MoF to follow-up with the councils to ensure that they implement the recommendations of the auditors. While PEFA notes that the Auditor General has begun undertaking some performance audit work, this remains in its infancy. 18. Responsibility for monitoring service delivery standards at local level is divided and in some cases overlapping . Both LCs and deconcentrated ministry bodies have some responsibility for monitoring service standards. Standard setting and monitoring policy itself is formally the role of central ministries. In practice LCs have very limited funding and capacity to undertake inspection work; indeed, funding levels are so low that it can be assumed that in many cases inspection work is simply not happening. A recent study into education monitoring and inspection noted that the function is chronically under-resourced; but where inspeciton is being done, it is delivered by multiple parties for some schools, while hard-to- reach schools are almost never visited. The study also notes that the absence of clear public quality standards and standardized monitoring/assessment tools means that inspection is not systematic; and data from such activity is not routinely shared. 19. Regarding procurement, recent studies showed significant misprocurements in terms of volume, cost, and type of goods procured by central-level authorities. A 2019 PETS looking at agriculture, health, and education key commodities and expenditures for 2018 and 2017 (comprising just under half of total non-salary recurrent expenditures in those years) identified significant weaknesses in the process of procuring key commodities in these sectors. The report estimated an average overpayment in 2017 and 2018 of 67 percent for fertilizer, 28 percent overpayment for exercise books in 2018, and 40 percent for textbooks in 2018. This represents a significant efficiency loss on government’s already stretched budget for basic services delivery. Furthermore, it is not clear how routine information and data on these poor procurement results is being produced, given that they seemingly came to light only through a one-off PETS exercise. 20. Regarding distribution, the same study show sobering levels of loss, misdirection, and/or poor record keeping within a system for which LCs are partly responsible. LCs have a mandate to manage the distribution of key commodities to basic services delivery units, working in partnership with the deconcentrated agencies of central ministries. However, significant problems have been been identified within this delivery chain. The PETS study noted above found that only 40 percent of fertilizer procured centrally was received at the intended destination; around 40 percent of textbooks intended for schools could not be accounted for in terms of their ultimate distribution; and over half of drugs procured ce ntrally for basic services did not reach their intended destination. Again, it is not clear how and to what degree this kind of vital service delivery information is being routinely created and used at the local level. (P172492) ANNEX 4: The Policy Mandate and Administrative Competencies of Local Government in Sierra Leone 1. The mandate and competencies of local government have evolved through several legislative acts and policy documents. The Local Government Act (2004) formally re-introduced the local government layer to the structure of the post-war state. This Act set out the broad nature of LC powers, roles, and their relationship with central government. Most of its provisions continue to describe and regulate LC activities today. Further legislation within the Public Procurement Act (2004), public FM regulations (2007), Public FM Act (2016), and subsequent public FM regulations (2018) have provided some additional detail on the roles and responsibilities of LCs with regard budgeting and FM. Alongside formal legislation and regulation, successive governments have also set out policy positions in the 2010 National Decentralization Policy; and made ministerial decisions regarding the roles and responsibilities of LCs and Chiefdom Administrations in the course of regular day-to-day public administration. 2. The gradual accretion of laws, policies, regulations, new delivery initiatives, and ministerial decisions regarding LC mandates have led to complex multi-level governance environment. As discussed in other sections of this document, the process of decentralization set out in the 2004 Act remains 'half finished' in many ways. Certain powers have been devolved to LCs, but not others; and there is a mixture of formal and informal requirements that LCs cooperate with the deconcentrated offices of central ministries in the administration and financing of basic services. Overall, councils remain formally responsible for many functions for which they have very little capacity and even less finance. The relationship with Chiefdom administrations remains complicated with some clear division of responsibilities between LCs and Chiefdoms, but also areas where their operations overlap where they are required to work together. 3. Government has re-committed to empowering LCs and announced a number of reforms to clarify and strengthen LCs’ roles and responsibilities. The Government has explicitly recognized the role that LCs have to play in delivering the basic services ambitions that are set out in the MTNDP. To this end, the 2020 National Decentralization Policy has been agreed by Cabinet and it explicitly recognizes the tensions within the current decentralized governance framework and commits to resolving them, including through passage of new local government legislation. This includes changing the policy responsibilities of councils; resolving the complex lines of accountability for LCs; clarifying the incomplete and inconsistent devolution of policy responsibilities to councils; and – over the medium-term – addressing the insufficient level funds for delivery of mandated responsibilities. It also notes the ongoing policy and financing conflicts between LCs and Chiefdom administrations; and between councils and central government ministries. As well as reforms to the conflicting administrative and financial rules for delivering local services; it promises to invest more fully in LCs' own capacity to deliver. 4. While this project is not focusing directly on these issues, their positive resolution will support delivery of the project's objectives. The project focuses on the FM and planning systems of LCs and central government; the provision of financing for development grants; and improved data tracking and monitoring of local service delivery. These do not directly attempt to clarify and refine the legal, policy, and administrative framework under which councils operate. However, success in the project's three reform areas will make the existing multi-level governance system work better in any case, as well as in supporting future success in the parallel legal and regulatory reform effort: Page 92 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) • Improved FM and planning processes. Better functioning councils will be more able to take on increased administrative mandates. Improved council systems will allow for greater certainty and reduced risk in the management of greater amounts of money. Strengthening the local-level planning process will increase the ability of LCs to work productively with the decentralized offices of central government ministries and other basic services stakeholders. More and better core council staff will lead to a general across -the-board improvement in how councils carry out their functions. Overall, more capable LCs will be more attractive candidates for the increased authority and financing envisaged for them in the ongoing legal, policy, and administrative reform process. • Grants for small-scale development projects. The 'something for something' approach of requiring improved council systems in return for development project financing will strengthen all the institutional processes being targeted in the workstreams above. Giving councils the ability to actually deliver discretionary local-level development projects will raise their profile and legitimacy among both their constituents and other basic services stakeholders, and again increase the value of councils in the eyes of central government policymakers. • Improved monitoring, data tracking, and information flows on service delivery . Councils will be better able to plan and deliver local development with improved data flows, and therefore strengthen the development planning process and the overall impact of the grants noted above. Better flows of data between LCs and central ministry systems will again increase the usefulness of the role of LCs in the eyes of central government. 5. Regarding their current mandate, LCs have the legal power to raise their own revenue by various means. The Local Government Act (2004) specifies a number of sources from which LCs are formally empowered to collect revenue. Over time, this basic schedule of revenue raising powers has been elaborated through ministerial decisions, although decisions over the sharing of revenue between councils and other organizations – notably Chiefdom administrations – have not always been to the advantage of LCs. Sources of LC revenue include: • A share of local taxes, to be determined by the LC, raised by Chiefdom administrations, and the revenue split between the Chiefdom and the council in accordance with government rules. • Property rates, to be levied and collected by the council itself. • Licenses, for establishing trade and professional work. • Fees and charges, for the use of markets, lorry parks, natural resource extraction, and services provided by the council. • Share of mining revenues, to be divided between Chiefdom authorities on a schedule determined by government. • Interests and dividends, from investments made in accordance with guidelines set by central government . • Any other revenue assigned by government. 6. LCs have wide-ranging service delivery mandates as set out in law, including in key basic services sectors . The Third Schedule of the Local Government Act (2004) specifies 79 policy areas to be formally devolved to LCs. These cover critical areas including health, education, agricultural extension services, and water supply and sanitation. Between 2004 and 2007, 54 of these functions were formally devolved to LCs. Some were subsequently recentralized, meaning that by the start of 2019 only 43 of the functions were formally devolved, with 36 outstanding. In 2019, the Inter-Ministerial Committee on decentralization formally devolved a further 28 of these functions to LCs, leaving eight of the original 79 policy areas with central government. Within this framework of decentralization, the mandate of central government agencies is supposed to be significantly restricted . The Local Government Act (2004) grants central government ministries the authority to inspect and monitor the performance of LCs in delivering their decentralized functions, provide support and capacity building for improved service delivery, and mediate in disputes between councils in its policy area. The Act empowers central ministries and ultimately the Presidency to intervene in LC operations where a council requests it, or where it is deemed to be unable to deliver its functions effectively, for a time limited period. Otherwise, delivery of these functions is formally the responsibility of LCs. Funding from central government to LCs is supposed to cover the cost of formally devolved functions but has typically not done so . The Local Government Act (2004) Articles 46-49 state that tied grants to support these devolved functions should form part of the national budget, be tied to the overall level of the budget, and be sufficient to provide a level of service equivalent to what was provided before devolution of that function and at an ‘appropriate standard’ going forward. Changes in the level of these grants should be no less advantageous than changes in the national budget, the grants must increase in line with inflation each year and be distributed on the basis of equity between councils. The discussion in Annex 2 outlines in more detail the reality of local government grant financing within the current policy framework. 9. The ability of LCs to effectively deliver on their administrative mandates has varied under different governments. Local government was formally re-established in 2004 with cross-party political agreement and with significant financial and technical support from the international community, primarily through successive World Bank projects. While the abolition of LCs and a return to centralized administration of the country is not a credible policy option, the political will to genuinely empower and support LCs to deliver their mandate has waxed and waned. Successive governments have varied in their commitment to building on the 2004 starting point and delivering well- financed and well-capacitated LCs. Policy reforms such as the re-introduction of Districts Officers as a mechanism of central government oversight of LCs, the decision to make LC Chief Administrators centrally appointed positions, and rules that councils must increasingly share revenue with Chiefdom authorities have undermined LC ’s potential for independence and authority in delivering their mandates. 10. When discussing technical issues of making basic services delivery work, it should be remembered that LCs were also intended to have a long-term stabilizing effect on the broader political economy of Sierra Leone. The re- Page 95 of 104 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) establishment of LCs and the process of decentralization more broadly was explicitly aimed at rebalancing the distribution of political power in the country so as reduce the intensity of political competition and therefore promote national stability. This was intended to be twofold: to move power and resources towards the regions and away from the Western Area, and therefore reduce the relative political and economic weight of Freetown; and to reduce the ‘winner-takes-all’ concentration of power in the Presidency by creating multiple arenas for political authority, and therefore lower the stakes around winning central government elections. This means that while LCs play an important technical role in their local areas; empowered local government is thought to offer a broader long-term political economy benefit to the country's stability, above and beyond their day-to-day managerial functions. Context: Health, Social and Economic Impacts 1. Sierra Leone was one of the last SSA countries to report its COVID-19 index case (March 31, 2020). Government’s management of the pandemic has had early successes but the country remains vulnerable to periodic spikes (such as in December 2020 and January 2021).35 After reaching a peak in January, there has been a substantial drop in cases. 36 Vaccination has started with the delivery of the first batch of COVID-19 vaccine shipped via the COVAX Facility. 37 Following Government’s request, the World Bank is pre paring an AF to the COVID-19 Project for vaccine procurement and deployment with US$5 million in IDA and an additional US$3.5 million in trust fund resources (RVP Approval scheduled for May 31, 2021). 2. The economic costs of the pandemic have been substantial with significant impacts on growth: -2.3 percent to -4.0 percent (2020) and 4 percent (2021) compared to the pre-COVID projected growth rate of 5.4 percent. With the State of Emergency, a partial lockdown and restrictions on movement, the disruption of economic activity also heightened existing ethnically aligned tensions. Schools were closed over the period March-October 2020, disrupting the education of about 2 million learners, 38 partially mitigated by the government’s radio teaching program. Restrictions on movement have been lifted (as of June 2020) with the airport and land borders reopened in October 2020 and February 2021, respectively. 3. With the confluence of historic fragilities, 39 existing macro-fiscal vulnerabilities and prevailing COVID risks, the pandemic has inflicted high human and economic costs threatening to set back the post -Ebola recovery . The key channels of economic impacts were tourism, transportation, manufacturing and mining. The fiscal and current account deficits increased due to increased government spending and reduced exports. Non-food inflationary pressures also increased due to supply-side bottlenecks. The fiscal gap is projected at 2.1 percent of GDP (2020). While Sierra Leone’s debt remains sustainable, the risk of debt distress remains high, and the COVID shock has elevated these risks. 40 Under the G20 Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI), GOSL contacted all bilateral creditors requesting debt relief through June 2021. No private creditors have been engaged. 4. COVID has put additional stress on poor households due to slowing food produ ction, shortages in food imports and higher food prices with disproportionate impacts on the poorest and most vulnerable households including people with disabilities threatening to worsen the already high levels of poverty and food insecurity in the country. Most households have seen a decrease in income since March 2020 from all sources: nearly two-thirds of rural households reported planting less rice this season, further increasing food insecurity and lowering agricultural production. 41 5. To respond to the COVID pandemic, the Government prepared the Quick Action Economic Response Program (QAERP) and the COVID-19 Preparedness and Response Plan for the health sector. The five pillars of the QAERP were directed at maintaining macroeconomic and financial stability, maintaining supply of critical commodities, mitigating the shock on businesses, boosting health sector spending and mitigating the shock on households.
