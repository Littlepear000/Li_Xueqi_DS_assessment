STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. The Lao People's Democratic Republic (PDR) Pollution and Waste Management Project (PWMP) is a US$37.85 million Investment Project Financing operation that aims to enhance the country's capacity to manage waste, plastics, and pollution and improve municipal solid waste (MSW) services in Vientiane Capital (VC). The six-year project also looks to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and generate jobs for vulnerable communities as a result of these interventions. The project supports the Government of Lao PDR’s (GoL) commitment to a low-carbon, resilient, and cleaner development pathway, the country’s medium-term Green Growth Strategy to 2030, the 9th National Socioeconomic Development Plan 2021-2025, and its Nationally Determined Contributions (2021), which include a commitment to reduce GHG emissions and enhance climate resilience. The focus on VC, the country’s capital and largest waste generator, ensures that the project's outcomes serve as a model for other cities, thereby amplifying its impact across the country. 2. The PWMP will be the second among the national level projects in the World Bank-financed Southeast Asia Regional Program on Combating Marine Plastics (SEA-MaP) (P175659) Series of Projects (SOP) to support marine plastic solutions at the national level. Marine plastic pollution is a transboundary issue, with shared river systems such as the Mekong River acting as conduits for plastic waste, and shared coastlines receiving plastic waste carried by ocean currents. Plastic pollution from Laos is transported via the Mekong River to the marine environment. Accordingly, single country solutions to manage marine litter carry the risk of not being sufficient; there is a need for regional collaboration to combat marine plastic pollution in critical countries with currently undermanaged plastic waste. The program follows a SOP approach under two pillars: Pillar 1 includes a US$20 million regional IDA-financed project that will help strengthen institutional capacity, harmonize standards and guidelines, and promote innovative financing and technological solutions. Pillar 2 focuses on plastic-related investments and policy support at national levels. The regional IDA-financed SEA-MaP project under Pillar 1 was approved in June 2022. In addition to the PWMP, Pillar 2 of the SOP includes the US$67 million Cambodia Solid Waste and Plastic Management Improvement Project (P170976) which was approved in May 2023.1 Implementation of the SEA-MaP is progressing steadily and is on track to achieve its objectives of strengthening plastic policies and promoting innovative solutions. An objective of the Cambodia project is to improve plastic management in selected areas. It is currently procuring key consultancy packages, including for the development of a regulatory framework to better manage waste and plastics, and site selection for waste and plastic recovery facilities and landfills. Laos will draw from the IDA Regional Window to support reduction of plastics, along with ASEAN and Cambodia. 3. The PWMP is being undertaken within a challenging macroeconomic environment. Economic growth in Lao PDR averaged about 7 percent over the two decades leading up to 2019, driven by capital-intensive, resource-based, and debt- financed patterns.2 High public debt levels have contributed to ongoing macroeconomic instability, impacting development prospects; the World Bank assessed under the Low-Income Countries Debt Sustainability Framework that Lao PDR is currently experiencing debt distress with an unsustainable outlook.3 The country faces both solvency and liquidity challenges due to substantial financing needs, limited financing options, low foreign exchange reserves, and notable depreciation pressures. Headline inflation slowed from 25 percent in March 2024 to 11.2 percent in March 2025 due to a stable exchange rate and continued monetary tightening. Since August 2024, the Lao kip has strengthened against major currencies, narrowing the gap between official and parallel markets. Air and water pollution have been longstanding concerns for the Lao PDR (Laos), while solid and plastic waste management are gaining prominence. National average ambient fine particulate matter (PM2.5) air pollutant levels are four times higher than World Health Organization guidelines. Exposure to air pollution results in about 10,000 premature deaths per year or approximately 17 percent of total annual deaths in Laos.4 The annual cost of health-related impacts of air and water pollution, poor sanitation and hygiene, and lead exposure was estimated to be 14.6 percent of 2017 GDP.5 Waste generation has been increasing in recent years at an alarming pace, particularly in urban centers. Between 2000 and 2015, the country’s major cities saw a 40 to 60 percent increase in waste generation, with MSW generation reaching approximately 0.75 kg per capita per day and a projected 54 percent increase in annual waste generation across Laos between 2020 and 2035 if actions are not taken. In VC alone, 1,065 tons of waste are generated per day – making it the highest rate in the country. Plastic comprises about 24 percent of MSW mainly from food and drink packaging and plastic bags.6 Toxic smoke generated from open burning of waste, including plastic, contributes to GHG emissions, air pollution and respiratory issues. Waste in Laos’ rivers and urban drainage systems impedes drainage and exacerbates flooding, affects fishing activity, and spreads plastic waste to marine areas via the Mekong River, damaging local and downstream ecosystems. Popular tourist destinations, face reputational risks and decreased attractiveness, due to widespread plastics pollution with consequent revenue impacts. 5. The GoL has established legal instruments for environmental risk management to address air and water pollution. These include a 2024 updated Environmental Protection Law (EPL) that emphasizes Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) as the main tool for pollution control, and a Prime Minister’s Decree on Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA, revised in 2022). As a result, and with World Bank support,7 the number of ESIAs approved by the ministry in charge, namely the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE), increased significantly between 2012 and 2020. 6. With hydropower, mining, agriculture, and industry being the main pollution contributors, the GoL has been prioritizing its regulation enforcement in these sectors. Agricultural biomass burning and industries such as cement factories have been identified as some of the main sources of PM10 and PM2.5. Water pollution has been linked to mining effluent and agricultural runoff.8 9 Investment projects in these sectors are categorized as Category 2 projects that require an ESIA to be approved. VC’s Solid Waste Management Strategy and Action Plan (2021-2030) targets 100 percent solid waste collection, maximizing material recovery, and financial and operational efficiency. The Vientiane City Office for Management and Service (VCOMS) is the urban development authority for VC, and oversees waste management services, with ten private operators contracted to collect and transport waste from households and businesses across the city.12 About 31 percent of MSW generators have contracts for waste collection.13 Waste is transported by the private companies to a 50-hectare landfill in Xaythany district of VC called the Kilometer (KM)32 landfill. VCOMS collects waste from some areas in Vientiane and this waste is transported to the KM16 transfer station in Saysettha district where it is compacted before it is sent to the KM32 landfill. KM32 landfill receives about 44 percent of MSW generated in VC and is managed by a private operator contracted by VCOMS. The Vientiane government also has in place a concessional agreement with the Vientiane Waste Management Company (VWMC), a private operator adjacent to the landfill, to provide Refuse Derived Fuel production services with the residual waste disposed of at the landfill for a tipping fee.14 VCOM’s main revenue streams include fees from waste contracts, tipping fees, and a small annual subsidy that is provided by the Vientiane government, albeit not consistently. Approximately 264 seasonal and permanent waste pickers from nearby villages operate at the KM32 landfill, 47 percent of whom are female, generating income from selling recyclable waste to a waste management community center at the KM32 landfill.15 10. Consequently, leachate is discharged into the surrounding area, impacting ground and surface water quality. Consequently, waste pickers operate in high- risk conditions and face health risks. The GoL, led by MAE, has in place a National Plastics Action Plan (NPAP) for 2024-2030 focusing on single-use plastic reduction, enhanced recyclables collection, and recycling sector improvement. The GoL has also implemented waste banks in a limited number of schools and GoL offices in VC for plastic waste collection. Small-scale efforts have been undertaken by the GoL to reduce the use of plastic bags along with plastic reduction campaigns. Domestic recycling is carried out mainly by private companies that process plastics into pellets for export, mostly to Thailand and Viet Nam. 12. However, key challenges facing the GoL in addressing plastic waste include lack of data and information for plastic waste monitoring, low technical capacity for implementing plastic recycling interventions, and lack of financial resources for implementing programmatic plastic waste reduction activities. Further, domestic recycling lacks compliance regulations and monitoring of plastics collection and recycling by the private sector, raising environmental and health concerns. These issues will become more pressing with impending plastic import bans by Thailand and Viet Nam. 13. The PWMP aims to resolve these challenges by improving the efficiency, effectiveness, and financial sustainability of Laos’ pollution, waste, and plastics management systems, scaling up the infrastructure and capacity of the VC government for MSW service delivery, and improving the working conditions and livelihoods of formal and informal waste workers (IWW). These will generate important regional outcomes as the amount of pollution flowing into shared water bodies will decrease significantly. Theory of Change and PDO Indicators 16. The PDO reflects two sets of challenges faced by Laos that the project will address relating to pollution control, and solid waste and plastics management. 17. To address pollution management challenges, the project will support the development and early implementation of a compliance monitoring system that will be used to help the GoL better enforce pollution control regulations among waste management facilities and other high-pollution risk enterprises. The project will also upgrade existing air and water quality stations, to increase the accuracy and reliability of air and water quality information that is made available to the public and used in the GoL’s decision-making. 18. The project will address waste management challenges through several interventions starting with development of a Solid Waste Management (SWM) Decree to strengthen the legal framework for waste management in Laos. These reforms are critical for the sustainability of waste collection and treatment infrastructure and equipment financed by the project. Through PWMP, the GoL will also invest in low-cost expansion of SW collection equipment, waste transfer and treatment facilities, including upgrading the KM32 landfill, and provide vocational and occupational health and safety (OHS) training, facilities, and protective equipment to waste workers. This will result in an increased amount of waste collected and safely treated, and more persons with access with waste collection services. 19. The longer-term outcomes of the project are expected to be national and regional in nature, namely low-carbon, resilient and cleaner development pathway for Laos, and increased alignment of Laos’ policies, guidelines, and standards with the ASEAN Regional Action Plan. 20. Combining national capacity building with targeted interventions is key for a comprehensive approach to pollution control and waste management. This encompasses the integration of policy and regulation development, institutional capacity building, and infrastructure investment at the national level. Concurrently, engaging local communities through awareness campaigns and participatory methods enhances compliance and sustainability. This dual approach ensures both systemic improvements and effective management of immediate challenges. This lesson is reflected in the design of components 1 and 2. 51. Strengthening local government capacity is key to success in SWM. Empowering district governments through capacity building and widening their political, legal, and implementation roles are key to the success of SWM service delivery. Therefore, Subcomponent 2.1 is designed to provide a comprehensive TA package to VC that would enhance its capacity for SWM planning, operational management, cost recovery, and monitoring. 52. Financial sustainability is fundamental in SWM. SWM services typically incur greater operating expenses compared to other public utilities due to the complex logistics of waste collection, processing, and disposal. Coupled with a challenge of low-cost recovery rates, SWM often necessitates government subsidies to remain operational. To address this, it is crucial to secure a reliable and sufficient financial foundation, blending service fee revenues with governmental budget contributions. Consequently, Subcomponent 2.1 activities will support the VC government to improve its financial management (FM) and fee collection system by helping to formulate and deploy a financial sustainability model for VC’s SWM system, improving its monitoring, and financing a public outreach program that encourages household participation. 53. Support for IWWs ensures integration and inclusiveness in SWM service delivery. IWWs are integral to the recycling value chain, yet they often receive insufficient recognition and support within waste management policy frameworks. Recent World Bank-financed SWM projects have shown success in integrating IWWs into the SWM process by providing them with welfare support, such as vocational training, and protective equipment. Macroeconomic risk is rated High. Laos’ Public and Publicly Guaranteed debt has risen to unsustainable levels.42 Macroeconomic instability, declining investment in human capital, and an unfavorable business environment are hindering economic growth prospects, while the economy is also vulnerable to external shocks. Macroeconomic imbalances, driven by public debt accumulation and generous tax incentives, have contributed to a significant depreciation of the Lao Kip since 2021, and resulted in high inflation. While the exchange rate stabilized in 2024, this was in significant part attributed to a mandatory repatriation of export proceeds that increased the availability of foreign currency holdings of exporters in the domestic banking system. While this reflected a stock adjustment, future inflows of foreign currency remain uncertain while the gross financing needs to repay foreign debt are substantial. Pressures on exchange rate and inflation may return and inflate the price of local currency purchases. Procurement strategies and the pricing of contracts in appropriate currencies will be designed to provide the best possible value for money for project resources. This instability reduces the ability of the GoL to fund the maintenance of public assets, including waste management infrastructure, and it thus becomes a risk to the long-term impact of the project. The PWMP will help mitigate these risks by developing a cost-efficient waste management system for VC and supporting reforms to waste collection fees and waste tipping fees which are the key sources of revenue for the country’s waste management system. The project will also enhance waste recycling as another revenue stream to support the waste management system. 92. Fiduciary risk is rated Substantial. MPWT and the EPF have experience in implementing World Bank-financed projects, however their FM and procurement capacities vary, and they will likely be overstretched with additional work. Procurement delays under ongoing World Bank-financed projects implemented by MPWT highlight capacity issues as well as lengthy approval processes, leading to delayed payment to contractors, which affects the project’s credibility and result achievement. While oversight capacity has improved in recent years, there are still risks of conflict of interest, fraud and corruption, and collusive practices. The EPF procurement team is understaffed, which could lead to procurement delays, inability to manage and monitor contracts, delays in making payments and preparing financial statements, and an increased risk of expenditure becoming ineligible for financing. The proposed risk mitigation measures include: (i) recruitment of FM and procurement support consultants for both the PCT and PMU; (ii) repeated FM and procurement training of PCT and PMU staff starting before effectiveness; (iii) a requirement for enhanced disclosure of procurement information to the World Bank; (iv) and a requirement that staff involved in procurement, including procurement committees, sign a declaration form noting no of conflict of interest. 93.
