STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Syria is making progress in its transition towards stabilization, recovery, and reintegration, while continuing to face major challenges after 14 years of conflict. More than one year after the December 2024 political transition, the new Syrian government under the leadership of President Al-Sharaa has maintained overall political stability. Modest economic growth is projected for 2025 and 2026, and Syria has benefitted from the lifting of major economic sanctions and renewed participation in international financial systems and investment discussions. At the same time, the security situation remains fragile, with occasional outbreaks of violence, and the country faces massive development challenges and continued humanitarian needs, furthered by increasing returns of refugees and internally displaced people. Continued international engagement and financing will be critical to support Syria in its post-conflict transition. 2. Sound management of public funds and transparency about their use are prerequisites for the Syrian state to gain the trust of its citizens and the international community as it seeks to mobilize resources and deliver services to the population. Fourteen years of conflict and economic mismanagement have weakened the capacity and legitimacy of Syria’s state institutions. A disciplined and transparent approach to public financial management (PFM) will be a critical foundation for Syria’s path to recovery and development. The Ministry of Finance has identified support for PFM reforms as a priority, highlighting the immediate need to update the relevant legal framework, rebuild human capital for core government functions, advance digitalization, and establish accountability and transparency. Sound management of public funds will also be needed to mobilize external assistance for the country’s massive reconstruction needs, with expectations from the international community of adequate oversight of reconstruction investments, payroll, and the flow of funds to provide these resource flows. 3. The World Bank is increasing its support to Syria, with a growing engagement being developed in close collaboration with the government. The World Bank approved a $146 million grant from the International Development Association (IDA) in June 2025 for the Syria Electricity Emergency Project as its first financing to the new government. The government and the World Bank are now discussing a strategy to expand this engagement, aligned with the principles and priorities of a draft national recovery plan being developed by the government. The emerging strategy is focusing on three foundations for recovery and sustained economic growth: stabilizing the economy and core government systems; restoring essential services and social stability; and enabling growth and private sector-led recovery. PFM is one cross-cutting priority identified in the draft national recovery plan aligned with these three foundations for recovery and growth. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Fiscal space is severely constrained, undermining the government’s ability to meet people’s needs. In the past fifteen years, revenue collection has shrunk from close to 20 percent before the conflict to less than 5 percent of GDP due to lower oil and tax revenues, a collapse in trade under sanctions, the expansion of the informal economy, and a weak collection capacity. Custom duty collection is marginal despite the heavy economic reliance on imports of commodities since 2011 (World Bank, 2024) and tax revenue relies almost exclusively on direct taxation. Limited fiscal resources and the prioritization of security expenditures crowd out other government spending, especially capital expenditure. From 2012 to 2024, the budgeted fiscal deficit of the Syrian government averaged 10 percent of GDP, excluding off-budget military spending and electricity subsidies, despite subsidy cuts in recent years. Syria’s total debt was around US$27 billion at the end of 2024 (128 percent of GDP), of which US$22.3 billion (104 percent of GDP) was external. Domestic debt sharply increased to about US$5 billion in March 2025 (24 percent of GDP).1 1 The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT 5. The Legislative Decree 54 of 2006 on Basic Financial Law (BFL), still valid until its planned review, establishes the core legal framework for Syria’s PFM system. This legal framework governs the preparation, execution, and oversight of the state budget by various institutions. As part of the Ministry of Finance (MoF’s) commitment to PFM reforms, it has initiated steps to amend the BFL and the project plans to provide a structured and consultative approach to ensure alignment between the law and the envisaged medium to long-term reforms. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank issued a report in 2010 on “Modernization of Public Financial Management”. The report noted modest implementation of the recommendations of the IMF Fiscal Affairs Division and World Bank mission on PFM in Damascus in January/February 2006 and the PEFA report dated March 2006 noted that while recognizing that certain progress was made, the 2006 mission concluded that further modernization of the PFM system was needed. Many of the reform initiatives, including budget integration, were at a very early stage. The focus was on budget preparation, while treasury reforms lagged. As a result, important treasury functions, such as cash planning and management, remained rudimentary, and there was no proper monitoring of budget execution. The introduction of a new chart of accounts (CoA), budget classification, and a double-entry accounting system— key basic requirements for an effective PFM system—were delayed. 6. Syria’s PFM systems, institutions, and processes continue to face significant challenges. Recent World Bank rapid assessments identified the following challenges: automation - transaction processing is mainly manual/paper- based; budgeting - weak preparation, leading to budget overruns; procurement – the legal framework has various gaps and outdated practices, hindering competition and transparency. The procurement management capacity is limited, and institutional arrangements are inefficient; human resource and payroll managements processes – in need of digitalization and integration; treasury management/Treasury Single Account (TSA) – no consolidated government cash management and absence of a digital interface between Treasury and Central Bank of Syria (CBS); accounting and reporting – outdated standalone system with ad hoc reports. Reports are also not published online; revenue management – low tax revenue collection; public investment management – low budget allocations for capital projects; internal audit – need for systematic risk-based audit approach; and external audit – audit reports are not timely and not published. 7. Without TSA, government cash resources are dispersed across multiple bank accounts. This fragmentation leads to inefficient cash management, as it becomes difficult to consolidate cash balances and optimize their use. It also increases borrowing costs and reduces transparency and accountability. Line ministries and municipalities operate some 3,400 bank accounts at the CBS (as of May 2025). The Central Bank of Syria (CBS) currently uses an in-house developed core banking system (Nahas) that has significant limitations and needs to be upgraded to provide the required capabilities for accounting, finance, and payments, as well as the appropriate IT and cybersecurity standards. Taxes are collected through MoF branches located in various governorates. In most cases, payments are made in cash, while in some instances, they are made in cheques to the MoF. Non-tax revenues, such as administrative fees and licenses, are typically paid via bank transfers. 8. Between 2021 and 2023, there were instances of significant variance between approved budget and actual expenditures reaching at aggregate (14 percent), functional (29 percent) and composition levels (59 percent). These variances show that the budget is not being implemented as intended at the approval stage. The root causes of these variances are the degree of realism of revenue forecasting, time taken to complete procurement and payment, and inadequate in-year reporting to identify issues for corrective action. Macro-fiscal analysis and forecasts are still nascent and require development to support annual budget management. There are no economic plans to guide budget preparation. 9. Public procurement is governed by a fragmented legal framework. The legal framework is uncertain and lacks provisions on key aspects such as transparency, conflict of interest, independent complaint mechanisms, value for money; nor has a procurement manual been issued to assist practitioners in proper implementation and compliance. Management capacity and institutional arrangements suffer from inefficiency and a culture of avoidance of responsibility, while procedures and practices show the preferential treatment of state-owned 2 The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT enterprises, weak procurement planning, and a lack of transparency in bid evaluation and contract management. Some gaps and areas of uncertainty in the legal framework have been dealt with by issuing interpretative circulars, an approach that, while providing a valuable service, can eventually contribute to the fragmentation of the legal framework as circulars proliferate in the absence of a workable database. Despite the presence of some modern procurement principles, the public procurement system continues to face the same weaknesses and shortfalls noted in the World Bank’s Country Procurement Assessment Report (CPAR) of September 2010. 10. Public sector employment in Syria is governed by a dedicated legal framework defined by Law No. 50 of 2004on the Basic Law for Public Employees. Decentralized personnel management and limited digitalization of HR and payroll records have hindered the government from creating a unified personnel database. This has weakened payroll controls and led to uncertainty about the size of the public sector workforce. Government expenditure on salaries is considerable, representing between 15 percent and 30 percent between 2017 and 2023. While the personnel roll in the various line ministries and paylists are reconciled manually every month, there is currently no unified and comprehensive database of public sector employees. The latest data from the Ministry of Administrative Development (MoAD) reports 772,314 government employees1 as of August 2025 (of which 54 percent are female). The government is attempting a series of public sector employment reforms, including rationalizing the public sector workforce and adjusting salaries to the cost of living. The MoAD is reviewing the personnel register and HRM rules and currently verifying staff through personnel files provided by line ministries. A Microsoft Access Database is being used with the aim of compiling data for digitizing the personnel roll/establishment register. Maximizing Finance for Development 11. The project is aligned with the World Bank Maximizing Finance for Development (MFD) approach. The following components are verified as MFD-enabling: (i) sub-component 2.2 aims to prepare a new public procurement law to support competitive bidding, prevent corruption, enhance sustainable procurement and promote the efficient use of public resources (value for money). It also supports establishing a Single Procurement Portal (SPP) that will be critical for promoting transparency and providing all necessary information on public procurement to the government, the private sector, and the public. Additionally, a Complaint Handling Mechanism aims to build trust with the private sector and encourage competition; (ii) component 3, while focused on public sector efficiency, improved PFM systems, and transparency, directly contributes to a more stable and predictable business environment. Reducing government arrears and building confidence in payment systems can directly encourage private sector suppliers and contractors to engage with the government, potentially freeing up their capital or increasing their willingness to invest. Climate and geophysical hazard context 12. Syria faces increasing climate and geophysical hazards, including extreme heat and drought, which —along with climate-exacerbated stress on the energy grid—can disrupt electricity supply and affect digital infrastructure and service delivery. These risks could disrupt the continuity of operations for key PFM functions, including budget execution, payroll, and procurement. Based on the Climate and Disaster Risk Screening, these risks are considered moderate but have been reduced to an acceptable low to moderate level through the project’s design. Key resilience features include appropriately tiered standards for data center upgrades, solar-powered backup systems, and remote access and business continuity protocols. II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Theory of Change and PDO Indicators 14. The proposed project will follow an incremental approach by focusing on the ‘basics first’ to build foundational systems and capacities and prepare the ground for more advanced reforms. To address the binding PFM problems, the PDO covers the three main thrusts of the project scope: (i) coordination capacity through the reform and coordination structures; (ii) budget controls through improved compliance from the menu of just-in-time support, in- built SIFMIS controls, and PFM-procurement professionalization; and (iii) transparency through publication of accurate and timely fiscal information with two-way interaction for citizen engagement. 15. The essential PFM functions that will be targeted by the project are budget preparation and execution, procurement, and financial reporting including simplification and digitalization of the related business processes. Also, the upskilling through professionalization of the PFM and procurement functions will be institutionalized to ensure sustainability. Improved payroll, procurement, and budgeting will ultimately contribute to better health, education, or infrastructure services. By mid-term of this project, a PEFA assessment is proposed to be conducted to develop a medium to long-term PFM reform strategy and action plan for future outcome-oriented reforms with gender and climate considerations. 16. The achievement of PDO will be measured through the following proposed indicators, which are calibrated to the current administration’s absorptive capacity while the country is in transition from fragility and conflict. The direct project beneficiary is the MoF, and the ultimate beneficiaries will be the Syrian citizens who will benefit from better strategic allocation and use of public funds for socio-economic development in a transparent manner. As per Article 2 of the BFL, the project will cover the “State Budget” by focusing on the budgetary aspects of “Public Entities of an Administrative Nature” which comprise of public administrative legal entities responsible for delivering public services, including justice, health, educational and social services, as well as safeguarding public property through its executive organs. D. Project Components Component 1: Public Financial Management Reform Oversight (US$2 million). Sub-component 1.1: Public Financial Management Reform Coordinating Unit (PFMRCU) (US$1 million). 20. To address the challenge of lack of a dedicated government team to oversee PFM reforms, this sub-component will support the establishment of a PFM reform oversight, coordination, and implementation structure. The Public Financial Management Reform Coordinating Unit (PFMRCU) that will be established in MoF will report to the PFM Reform Oversight Steering Committee (PFM-ROSC) chaired by the Minister of Finance. This committee will offer policy 5 The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT guidance on PFM to high-level political leaders, which may help address potential resistance to reform. A Government PFM Reform Coordinator with project management experience will oversee the daily coordination of reform activities including monitoring and evaluation, stakeholders change management and communication campaigns with support from Technical Assistants (TAs). This coordinator will serve as the primary point of contact for the Reform Implementation Teams (RITs) to ensure the appropriate sequencing of activities funded by various donors. The RITs will comprise of government designated focal staff to absorb skills and knowledge to promote the sustainability of the reform efforts. The PFM Donor Group (PFM-DG) will ensure mutually reinforcing support based on a government-led PFM reform agenda and foster policy dialogue. The PFM-DG will table key issues at the Syria Partnership Coordination Forum. 21. Over time, and in addition to its core coordination role, the PFMRCU will support the integration of climate- sensitive/resilience measures across PFM reforms, as relevant. This may include coordination of initiatives such as climate budget tagging, tracking of adaptation- and mitigation-related expenditures, and exploring opportunities to strengthen the public financial system’s capacity to support the country’s climate-related objectives over time. Sub-component 1.2: Syria External Assistance Fiduciary Section (SEAFS) (US$1 million). 22. To support the MoF’s readiness to handle large-scale external financing for recovery and reconstruction efficiently and transparently, the project will support the operationalization of a Syria External Assistance Fiduciary Section (SEAFS) in the MoF. The SEAFS centralized structure in MoF comprising government designated staff will manage the fiduciary functions for this project and for externally financed projects using country systems reinforced with risk mitigation measures. For Procurement, SEAFS will not operate as a central procuring department for all contracts financed by external donors. Instead, its role will be pivotal in providing fiduciary and capacity-building support to Project Implementation Units (PIUs), which will retain overall responsibility for procurement execution. This approach ensures that procurement remains decentralized at the project level while benefiting from strong upstream fiduciary support, thereby streamlining processes and reducing unnecessary delays. This sub-component will also fund technical assistance to the Central Commission for Control and Inspection (CCCI) to conduct internal auditing using risk-based methods for the project and to Central Organization for Financial Control (COFC) for financial audits. Financial management and procurement experts will be hired to build the capacity of the government staff assigned to the SEAFS. This support is especially important in establishing upstream fiduciary management and oversight capacity by strengthening Syria’s PFM systems to avoid fragmentation/proliferation of fiduciary arrangements. Component 2: Capacity development for essential public financial management and procurement functions. (US$10 million). Sub-component 2.1: PFM Professionalization (US$2 million). 23. Beyond sound legal and institutional frameworks, PFM improvement requires capability at the level of individual PFM practitioners. A Training Needs Assessment (TNA) will be undertaken for the Central Finance Agencies (MoF, CCCI, COFC) to identify the knowledge gaps of officials working in PFM. This assessment will inform the training program and development of a comprehensive capacity building plan to support the professionalization of the PFM cadre. The strategy will be monitored through a plan for post-training evaluations (Kirkpatrick Training Evaluation Model) to identify the extent to which the skills and knowledge gained are improving individual and organizational performance. A well-defined PFM cadre/scheme of service will enhance the sustainability of the proposed professionalization program as it will encourage staff to acquire the PFM qualifications with opportunity to rise through the hierarchy of the PFM cadre. 24. A PFM Competency Framework based on job analysis will be prepared as the key instrument to ascertain the required capability and learning outcomes to effectively perform the key PFM functions. The Syria PFM curriculum will aim to cover the PFM Process Architecture according to the ‘whole system approach’ of the Chartered Institute of Public Finance and Accountancy (CIPFA). However, as a forward-looking education program, the curriculum will be broadened with fiscal forecasts, budget preparation and management, sector strategies, performance budgeting, 6 The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT procurement, fiscal transparency and participation, and PFM legal framework. Whilst the curriculum and learning modules will comply with internationally acceptable standards, the design will respond to Syria’s applicable legal framework. The professional training program will include elements of climate-responsive public financial management, covering topics such as climate budget tagging, climate finance tracking, fiscal risk assessment for climate shocks, and green fiscal policy design. These training modules will build public officials’ capacity to integrate climate risk and opportunity into fiscal planning. Face-to-face training will be supplemented by mentorship and on- the-job learning to ensure attainment of the desired competencies. 25. The Accounting and Audit Council (AAC2) as the sector regulator (under the MoF) and the Association of Syrian Certified Accountants (ASCA3) as the Professional Accountancy Organization (PAO) will play critical roles in the proposed professionalization program. The ASCA will set curriculum, administer examinations, and issue certificates. The project will provide advice on strengthening Law no. 33 of 2009 (Law Regulating the Accounting and Auditing Profession) and the institutional arrangements for ASCA to meet the International Federation of Accountants (IFAC) Statements of Membership Obligations (SMO4). Formal accreditation/certification would fall under the ASCA as the PAO since this will maintain academic rigor and credibility. To ensure sustainability, the PFM training program will be constituted as a PFM Academy within the Financial Technical Institute (FTI5) in MoF. Training of Trainers (ToT) will build sustained capacity to deliver in-county training. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis Technical overview 54. The project is designed based on the ‘Basics First’ approach in PFM reforms which emphasizes building foundational systems and capacities before attempting advanced reforms. This means focusing on the foundational elements first, recognizing that these elements are interconnected and must be implemented in a coordinated manner to achieve the desired outcomes. This is particularly relevant for the Syria context with limited technical and managerial capacity. Therefore, the basics covered are: foundational cash-based accounting; ensuring annual budgets are realistic, timely, and adhered to; implementing systems such as TSA to track and control cash flows effectively; training staff in core financial management and procurement skills; developing institutional frameworks and legal structures to support PFM; introducing basic internal controls to prevent misuse of funds and strengthening external audit to monitor compliance and performance; making budget and spending information publicly available to enhance accountability by producing timely and accurate financial reports. The project design is technically simple by focusing on the following three components. Component 1: Public Financial Management Reform Oversight 55. In fragile situation such as Syria, it is critical to first establish a robust structure for project oversight, technical leadership, and management of the overall reform program. This will ensure that specific project objectives are met on time, on budget, and with high quality results. PFM reforms deliver results when three conditions coincide: when there is a strong political commitment to their implementation; when reform designs and implementation models are well tailored to the institutional and capacity context; and when strong coordination arrangements – led by government officials – are in place to monitor and guide reforms. This component will support the establishment of a PFM reform oversight, coordination, and implementation structure. The Public Financial Management Reform Coordinating Unit (PFMRCU) that will be established in MoF will report to the PFM Reform Oversight Steering Committee (PFM-ROSC) that will be chaired by the Minister of Finance. 15 The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT Component 2: Capacity development for essential public financial management and procurement functions 56. Sound management of public funds is a prerequisite for the state to gain the trust of its citizens and the international community so that it can mobilize external assistance for the country’s reconstruction. While acknowledging the urgent need for a wide-ranging set of reforms, considering the current capacity challenges, this component adopts a programmatic approach over the medium-term as it provides a better chance of building a solid foundation to scale up later. Beyond availability of good laws and institutional structures, PFM improvement requires capability at the level of individual PFM practitioners. In this regard, the PFM and Procurement Professionalization Program is geared towards creating a cohort of suitably qualified PFM and procurement practitioners with the skills to perform a broad range of tasks and contribute to improving PFM and public procurement performance in a sustainable manner. This component is also well-focused in terms of its technical coverage in the following PFM areas which recognize that the various areas are interconnected - budget preparation and execution, procurement, and financial reporting including simplification and digitalization of the related business processes. Component 3: Syria Integrated Financial Management Information System (SIFMIS) 57. The proposed digital transformation will enable PFM functions to be exercised in an efficient and transparent manner. The implementation of the core modules of SIFMIS is assessed as doable within three years since the coverage is limited to ‘public entities of an administrative nature’ within the central government. The simplified business processes and basic functions provide a good opportunity for bidders to propose a fit-for-purpose solution. Such a similar approach is expected to deliver quick-wins to improve commitment controls and publish timely and accurate fiscal reports including donor-financed projects. Tools will be prepared to support the upload of in-year budget reports and annual financial statements on government websites/Syria Budget Transparency Portal in user- friendly formats for easy public consumption. Financial reporting will be based on an improved Budget Classification and Chart of Accounts (BC-CoA) with separate segments for administrative, economic, fund source, functional, and geographical classifications consistent with International Monetary Fund Government Finance Statistics (IMF-GFSM 2014). 58. The baseline emissions are primarily sourced from the combustion of fossil fuels during interagency vehicle travel for document submission and the energy-intensive processes of paper production and disposal (Environmental Paper Network, 2020; IPCC, 2019). Over a three-year period, these activities are projected to generate a total of 23.1 kt CO2e. This figure represents the carbon footprint of the system prior to any digital intervention. In contrast, the new digital system is designed to generate significant emission reductions across multiple domains. The primary reductions are achieved by eliminating the need for vehicle travel, which avoids tailpipe emissions, and removing the entire lifecycle carbon footprint of paper production. Furthermore, the system's remote access features enable employees to reduce their daily commutes, and the use of energy-efficient infrastructure and solar power backups lowers reliance on grid electricity, thereby reducing energy-related GHG emissions. While the digital system produces its own emissions from device use and data storage (Kez et al., 2022), these are minimal in comparison. Over the same three-year period, the total emissions from the digital infrastructure are estimated at just 0.0165 kt CO2e. This results in a 99.93% reduction from the baseline, confirming that the project is an effective strategy for emission reduction. References • Environmental Paper Network. (2020).
