Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Sierra Leone’s 7.8 million people have now enjoyed nearly two decades of peace and relatively steady development, but alongside very high levels of poverty and inequality . Since the end of the civil conflict, key state institutions have been rebuilt; four elections at national and local levels have been held; and these have resulted in two peaceful handovers of the Presidency between the two main parties. The economy recorded steady average per capita GDP growth of 5.9 percent from 2002 to 2014. However, overall poverty and inequality remain high and GDP per capita at current US$ was only $530 in 2019. What wealth there is, is concentrated: the bottom 40 percent of the population enjoy only 20 percent of the total household consumption. Poverty is also unequally distributed with rates of poverty in rural areas that are more than twice as high as those in the urban areas (73.9 percent versus 34.8 percent)1; and the largest reduction in poverty has occurred in urban areas around the capital, leaving more rural areas behind. 2. In this context, human development sectors have seen a trend of increased financing of inputs, but the government's current fiscal position means that scope for additional financing is limited. A combination of government and donor funding has significantly raised spending on health and education over time. Current health expenditure as a percentage of the GDP has increased from 9.9 per cent in 2008 to 11 per cent in 2020. Over this period, total per capita spending on health has doubled in real terms, as has government expenditure on health. Education spending as a percentage of GDP has increased from 2.4 per cent in 2008 to 22 per cent in 2020; and the share of overall public spending on education has doubled, from 14.9 per cent in 2008 to just over 33 per cent in 2018. However, the cumulative effects of a collapse in global prices for Sierra Leone's main commodity exports, the Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) pandemic (both 2015-16), and the current Covid-19 disease outbreak has dramatically affected the government's fiscal position. There is little prospect of significant increases in public resources for basic services delivery. 3. Sierra Leone scores 0.36 in the 2020 Human Capital Index (HCI)2, which is lower than the regional average for sub-Saharan Africa and the average for low income countries. The country has some of the most inefficient health and education spending in the world in terms of turning inputs into outputs using a production- possibility frontier analysis. In the absence of significant sources of new funds, the focus must now be on seeking to improve the effectiveness of existing spending. Improvements in basic services delivery link directly to Sierra Leone's political and security situation: if poor human development outcomes continue indefinitely, they will generate avoidable human misery and impede economic development; but they will also risk undermining citizens’ already fragile trust in governing institutions. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Local councils have a significant role to play in delivering basic services. Since 2004, reinvigorated local councils have progressively taken on numerous formal legal and administrative functions regarding the management of basic services. Local councils are now formally responsible for the delivery of a range of services, including: primary and junior secondary education, primary and secondary health care, rural water, sanitation, solid waste management, agriculture, youth services, social assistance services, and firefighting services. Central level ministries and their deconcentrated local offices theoretically retain responsibility mostly for sector strategic planning, setting standards, quality control, and monitoring of service delivery. Local councils therefore also have an important 'convening' role, given their position as both the democratic representatives of local communities, and as institutions with a formal mandate to deliver services in partnership with other public sector agencies. 5. However, the multi-level governance3 that currently characterizes the policy and administrative framework governing basic services delivery leaves local councils caught between contradictory laws, rules, and regulations. Successive basic services policy reforms have been laid on top of each other without due regard for overall system-level consistency. Policy decisions over recent years have decentralized some functions, while recentralizing others; leading to a complex multi-level governance arrangement. Different elements of basic services delivery (e.g. staff; operational funding; capital investment; procurement of key commodities; data collection and reporting) are each under the authority of different organizations at different levels of government. This results in an unclear and sometimes conflictual relationship between local councils, the deconcentrated offices of sector ministries, Chiefdom administrations, and central government. Unsurprisingly, accountability for results is reduced when responsibility is unclear. 6. Government has re-committed to resolving these policy issues and has announced a number of reforms to clarify and strengthen the role of local councils and for the use of better data in basic services delivery. The government has explicitly recognized the role that local councils have to play in delivering the basic services ambitions that are set out in the MTNDP vision. It has announced a series of reforms designed to clarify and streamline the roles and responsibilities of different agencies involved in service delivery and set out a policy reform process to do so. Within this reform agenda, three key issues stand out regarding local council’s ability to support basic services delivery. These represent areas where the change has remained limited, including where government has committed to reform in principle, but would benefit from additional expertise and/or financing to accelerate the delivery of change. Improvements in these areas would also lead to notable systems improvement regardless of the speed of progress in other areas of legal and policy reform. The challenges in these areas are also interrelated and collectively work against effective local service delivery. They comprise: a. Ongoing institutional weakness of local council finance management and planning process. Basic systems for planning and delivering basic services interventions exist, but are not operating well. This results, in part, from the poor interface between certain central and local financial management and planning systems. It reduces the attractiveness of local councils as the route for central government to invest in basic services. b. The lack of meaningful discretionary finance through which councils could deliver development results. The absence of funds to actually provide local services and development impact means that councils have little incentive to invest in better financial management and planning systems; and their role as local leaders and conveners of the development process is undermined. c. An absence of useful data with which to plan and monitor basic services delivery . This compounds the issues above by reducing the quality of local council financial management and planning processes; and it means that local councils have limited ability to prove their positive local development impact. This again reduces their incentives to invest in better financial management and planning systems. Local council financial management and planning processes 8. Several diagnostic reports note long-term improvements in the local council financial management and planning system, albeit from a low base. The preceding World Bank decentralization project (Decentralized Service Delivery Program (DSDP 2) used a composite tool to measure institutional capability in local councils. It registered an increase in aggregate scores from 42 percent in 2012 to over 60 percent in 2017 (although this Mar 17, 2021 Page 5 of 13 The World Bank Accountable Governance for Basic Service Delivery (P172492) remained below the target level for the project). Local councils have made progress in strengthening their core financial systems as demonstrated by audit results where 99 per cent of local council accounts were submitted for audit, and 69 per cent of these were unqualified, over the period 2012 and 2018. The 2018 PEFA notes that the core formula-based allocation system by which central government provides regular grants to local councils for their operations is well designed, scoring an ‘A’. 9. A number of financial management and planning system weaknesses remain, however, that directly affect the ability of local councils to deliver their local development mandates. Local level planning is weak, with a disconnect between councils and deconcentrated service delivery agencies in drawing up their respective local plans. The execution of budgets to finance service delivery is untimely, erratic, and bureaucratic, leading to ‘stop-go’ financing and unnecessary delays in disbursement. Central level processes for the procurement of key commodities for education and health are not standardized and do not use technology-enabled solutions. The national financial system that tracks local council expenditure does not run live, leading to entry errors, gaps in reporting, and updating delays. Local council financial systems are not well-suited to supporting local revenue collection and do not align with national revenue raising policies. Financial audit performance has improved; but local council’s response to audit recommendations is poor and there are no performance audits at local level. 10. There are also weaknesses in the capability of local council staff who manage these processes. The financial management and planning processes above are predominantly delivered by core council staff who work directly for the council and are paid from its own funds. The Local Government Service Commission (LGSC) has completed a verification exercise of the core staff of Local Councils and found that there is a mismatch between staff qualifications and positions. Although there are relatively few vacancies (93% of sanctioned core staff were in place within local councils), the lack of clear systems and processes for staff recruitment, transfers, promotion, and targeted trainings have served as a demotivating factor for staff at the local level. This has negatively impacted on the ability of local councils to effectively deliver on their core administrative functions. Overall, there are gaps in the capability of existing staff working in local councils that makes operating effective financial management and planning systems even more challenging. Financing for local development results 11. In absolute terms, the discretionary financing available to councils to deliver their local development mandates is extremely limited. Development (capital) spending by councils is negligible and has declined in recent years. Only $6m in development spending was available for all councils in 2015, and this dropped to just over US$2m in 2018. Even the small, budgeted transfers are typically under-executed: for example, in 2019 only just over half (Le4.5bn or US$0.56m) of the total allocation (Le7.65bn or just under US$1m) for development grants was actually transferred to councils. This equated to development expenditure of roughly US$0.07 per capita in that year. Non-salary recurrent fiscal transfers are small, and councils have limited control over them. In 2019, actual recurrent transfers for expenditure in health and education (excluding centrally-procured key commodities like TLMs and medical supplies) were US$13m – less than US$2 per capita. Furthermore, most central government funding to councils is now subject to a ‘pass-through’ to service delivery units (primarily education). This means that councils have no effective control over this expenditure. Direct donor support to councils comprises only a few million dollars over 3-4 years and is heavily concentrated in Freetown. In relative terms, the share of basic services funding going to local councils does not reflect their policy responsibilities. Analysis for the years 2015-2018, suggests that around 90 percent of spending occurring at local level in Sierra Leone was happening outside the authority of local councils. The vast majority of expenditure on local services goes to fund the salaries of staff working in health, education, agriculture, and water services. These staff are managed and paid by central ministries and not by the local councils who remain ostensibly responsible for delivery of many of these functions. In recent years, overall levels of transfers to local councils have been a very small proportion of total expenditure, and a tiny proportion of GDP. Monitoring and use of data in local development delivery 13. There is a substantial mismatch between the formal role of councils in monitoring service delivery and their ability to do so. Existing mandates for local council service delivery give them a clear role in overseeing, monitoring, and reporting on local services delivery; working in partnership with central ministries who are supposed to set service standards and retain overall responsibility for sector performance. This monitoring function is also in line with local councils' democratic role representing the interests of local people in their area. However, in practice, local councils struggle to deliver this role: • There is effectively no funding for local councils to deliver their monitoring mandate. Low levels of financing for local councils, as discussed above, means that their critical functions of oversight, inspection, and supervision of service delivery are heavily constrained, or simply not delivered. • Existing systems for service delivery monitoring are weak. There are weak national PFM systems in place to track the resources transferred to service delivery units, with all aspects of performance information for service delivery scoring a ‘D’ in the 2018 PEFA. This means that neither central nor local level institutions receive consistent and comprehensive information on what is being delivered as a result of public services spending. • Oversight tracking systems for key service delivery inputs are insufficient. Public Expenditure Tacking Surveys (PETS) completed in 2018 found that only 56 percent of facilities charged with managing distribution of agricultural, health, and education key commodities kept complete records. Partly as a result of this, the same survey found that 42 percent of centrally-procured but locally-delivered textbooks could be not be located; and 54 percent of medical supplies did not ultimately reach their destination. 14. These three factors negatively reinforce each other, leading to a low-output equilibrium. The absence of any substantial funding for their local development role means that local councils have little incentive to invest in their financial management and planning systems. The lack of investment in such systems means councils are not well equipped to perform the local development roles that they do currently have, including the critical role of monitoring and reporting on service delivery performance. This reduces the attractiveness of local councils as potential recipients of additional financing and policy responsibility; and makes long-term investment in the quality and quantity of the local council core workforce a low priority. As a result, local councils receive relatively little funding or policy attention – and the cycle continues. At a political level, this low-output equilibrium provides convenient justification for the historic ambivalence shown by many governments to the overall value of decentralized government and of local councils as primarily responsible for local development and basic services. Investment in these three areas can help shift this towards a positive virtuous circle of improvement. If financial management and planning systems at both the supportive central and local levels can be improved, then local councils will have a greater ability to use their financing effectively. Providing additional finance through these better systems will provide a real incentive for their continued improvement and generate a powerful ‘learning-by-doing’ effect; particularly if the volume of finance offered is progressively increased in return for evidence of continued improvement. Investment in stronger monitoring systems and data tracking will help local councils to better deliver basic services as well as raising their importance in the eyes of other service delivery bodies. The impacts of climate change put the livelihoods and food security of a large share of the rural population at risk, creating additional pressure on basic local services . Sea level rise threatens low-lying coastal Sierra Leone; while seasonal flooding and landslides due to heavy precipitation take a toll on agricultural production, infrastructure, and housing. Additionally, flooding may increase the likelihood of waterborne diseases, including cholera. The role of local government to adapt or mitigate the effects of climate change could be potentially significant, resulting for instance, in helping to lower the instances of flooding in coastal and riverine areas and a higher prevalence of diarrheal diseases. Project Description 17. The project consists of complementary components for a total of $40 million4 equivalent to address the interrelated challenges to poor local service outcomes. The project will simultaneously tackle key identified challenges and underlying drivers, at both the local and central levels.5 • Component 1 will strengthen planning, financial management and accountability systems within and between local and central government agencies, as well as provide capacity building support to core fiduciary staff at the local level. • Component 2 will channel funding for small-scale capital investment through these systems, as the institutional strengthening through Component 1 continues to prepare LCs to manage larger development grants. The flow of finance through these systems will both incentivize their development – since grants are conditional upon meeting certain system conditions; and strengthen their use – through a 'learning by doing' approach that actually gives district LCs discretionary finance to work with. This project component will also support any future fiscal decentralization planned by the Government using the inter-governmental fiscal transfer system. • Component 3 builds stronger local and national monitoring and data tracking systems; while also providing information that will support planning, management, and delivery of basic services and development grants by LCs. This project component will prioritize increasing community engagement and citizen awareness of the data platforms supported. • Component 4 supports a project management framework and implementation support to reliably deliver, with particular investment in results-monitoring. 18. The components will be delivered in a phased and incremental approach over the project lifecycle so as to maximize impact, manage risk, and promote sustainability. The proposed operation is designed to dynamically evolve over the six year timeframe of the project. The project's impact will be maximized by progressively strengthening institutional systems as a benchmark condition for progressively larger grants to support local development. This dynamic process offers a growing financial incentive for councils to engage with the technical assistance (TA) support through the project to further strengthen and maintain systems and processes over the course of five grant cycles. Risks are managed within this approach by ensuring that councils with sufficiently strong systems and a good track record of delivery and reporting are able to access the largest development grants in the later years of the project. Equity concerns are addressed by primarily focusing on the rural district councils for the local development grants 6, where there is a higher concentration of the poor and systems remain weak across councils. Increased transparency and availability of data will be an additional key output area for the project, by mobilizing community participation and citizen engagement. The phased nature of the project results means that notable positive impacts on strengthening institutions for service delivery can be achieved even while government is taking forward legal and policy reforms in the area of local government and basic services delivery.7 There is flexibility, however, built into the project to adapt to engage with a revised policy and legislative framework.8 19. As project implementation advances, a flexible project design allows for additional financing to accelerate or scale up activities based on momentum. The project design includes sequencing of concurrent activities of project components and a planned comprehensive review after two years of project disbursements in order to address any emerging needs, including through additional financing. Any additional financing would remain subject to IDA funding availability, dependent on pace of performance during early implementation of the project, and may also need to factor in government legislative and policy reforms that increase momentum towards the decentralization agenda. The Project’s Environmental and Social Risk is rated as Moderate. The risk rating takes into account the potential risks and impacts associated with the civil works that will be implemented, as well as the client’s capacity to implement the requirement of the World Bank’s ESSs. The nature and scope of the project’s anticipated risks under Component 3, are not expected to be adverse given that civil works will not be complex and will be limited to existing public assets. Therefore, the project’s construction activities will only generate minimal adverse impacts that will be site specific and limited to the immediate environs and can be mitigated through the implementation of cost-effective measures. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 20. The MOF will have the overall responsibility for the project with the support of a PMU and fiduciary support from the PFMU. The PMU led by a Project Coordinator, will directly interact with the technical leading units within MOF and other MDAs, mainly PFMRU for Component 1, FDD for Component 2 and NaMED for Component 3. Each participating entity will formally nominate focal points who will be supporting the implementation of project activities and ensure technical coordination and effective oversight of said activities. The focal points will be directly involved in the project activities and will facilitate their day-to-day management and monitoring by establishing regular channels of communication between the PMU and participating entities.
