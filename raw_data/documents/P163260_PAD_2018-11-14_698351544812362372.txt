STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. The Dominican Republic has enjoyed one of the strongest growth rates in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) over the past 25 years. In the period 2001–13, its economy grew at an average rate of 5.1 percent, putting it in fourth place among the region’s top growth performers (after Panama, Argentina, and Peru). This strong performance recently got even better: economic growth rates averaged 7.1 percent in 2014–16, fueled by strong domestic demand. This overall dynamic growth has enabled a convergence of the Dominican Republic’s Gross National Income (GNI) per capita (US$6,259 in 2015 and US$6,574 in 2016) with that of the region.1 2. Poverty has declined substantially over the past few years; yet more effort is needed to sustain these gains and to address the remaining poverty and equity challenges. The overall poverty rate reached 50 percent in 2004 as a result of the 2003 banking crisis and returned to pre-crisis levels only in 2015. According to the latest official data available, monetary poverty declined to 30.5 percent in 2016, from 32.3 percent in 2015. Extreme poverty declined from 7.0 percent in 2015 to 6.5 percent in 2016. 3. Of particular concern are the significant disparities in poverty and vulnerability levels across regions and between rural and urban areas. Poverty rates in the southern and western regions along the Haitian border are the highest in the country, at about 50 percent, and levels of extreme poverty there are more than twice the national rate. Despite a general reduction in poverty since 2004, moderate poverty is still proportionally more predominant in rural areas than urban (38 percent against 26 percent in 2016). Agricultural productivity is low, and natural disasters such as hurricanes and tropical storms are a recurring threat to rural areas and to the living conditions and incomes of the rural population. The quality of water supply and sanitation (WSS) services also remains unequal: as of 2015, 79 percent of rural populations had access to improved WSS services against 86 percent of urban populations. This makes the already poorer populations of rural areas particularly vulnerable to extreme natural events and other impacts of climate change. 4. Despite progress in some regards, Dominican women face barriers to their full inclusion in society. With respect to economic opportunities, participation in the labor force among women aged 29–54 increased from 59.5 percent in 2001 to 71.8 percent in 2016, accompanied by increases in the occupation rate from 47.2 to 60.5 percent in the same period. However, in 2016 more than a fifth of all women were unemployed (20.3 percent), with young women (aged 15–28) experiencing particularly high rates of unemployment (34.0 percent). 5. Like many other countries in the Caribbean, the Dominican Republic is vulnerable to extreme natural events and other impacts of climate change. The country is exposed to weather-related disasters (tropical storms, hurricanes, cyclones, floods, and landslides), droughts, and seismic events. A global comparison of countries suggests that the Dominican Republic is among those most affected by natural disasters in the past 20 years; it ranked 10th in the world in terms of the impacts of climate change and weather-related events in the period 1997–2016 (Germanwatch’s Global Climate Risk Index).2 These impacts include the human and economic losses associated with these events. From 1980 to 2008, 40 natural disasters affected 2.65 million people, almost a quarter of the country’s population. Large dams play a crucial role in flood control, as well as in storing water for multiple uses (hydropower, WSS services, irrigation, and so on). Sectoral and Institutional Context 6. The development of the agri-food sector, one of the engines of growth in the Dominican Republic, puts significant pressure on natural resources. The sector (agriculture, livestock, forestry, fisheries, and agroindustry) contributes 16 percent to the gross domestic product (GDP)3. Primary agricultural production has been contributing a relatively constant 6 to 7 percent to the GDP over the past 10 years. Agri-food exports (animal, vegetable, and foodstuffs) accounted for 20 percent of the total value of official exports in 20124. Three-quarters of the agricultural production is transformed downstream in the value chain by the agroindustry sector,5 adding value and creating jobs in the country. Meanwhile, this sector is contributing to deforestation in upper watersheds, soil erosion, and the pollution of water sources, all of which threaten the environment, agroecosystem health, the country’s economic development, and the health of its population. The majority of deforestation (60 percent) continues to be caused by the expansion of slash-and-burn agriculture and poor, extensive livestock production practices. Improving agroecosystem health and the sustainability of landscape management will thus be critical to supporting agri-food sector development going forward in the face of climate change. 7. The agri-food sector in particular is vulnerable to weather events (floods and droughts) whose severity is likely to increase in the near future due to climate change. According to the National Strategy for Adaptation to Climate Change in Agriculture 2014–20,6 total annual rainfall may decrease to 1,137 millimeters (mm) in 2030 (a 11 percent decrease from 2010). Climate scenarios project an increase of temperature from 0.5 to 1 degree Celsius (°C) by 2030 and from 1 to 2.5°C by 2050. Areas currently subject to drought could become permanently arid with climate change. Access to resilient water supply, services, and infrastructure is key: natural water endowments in the Dominican Republic’s 10 reservoirs, located in 6 of the country’s 31 provinces, are insufficient. 8. The largest user of water in the country is the agricultural sector, which consumes 83 percent of the available volume.7 This amount is considered sufficient for the country to enhance its production; however, the inefficient management of irrigation systems (25 percent of water efficiency) questions this possibility. The WSS sector does not place great demand on the resource: it consumes only 7 percent of the available volume. But it represents the second-largest polluter of water resources, behind the agricultural sector.8 9. The Dominican Republic has effectively expanded access to WSS services, but access remains unequal. As of 2017, 94 percent of Dominicans had access to an improved basic source of water (97 percent in urban and 86 percent in rural areas), and 83 percent to improved basic sanitation facilities (85 percent in urban and 74 percent in rural areas).9 Yet despite increasing (albeit unequal) coverage levels, the quality of service delivery has been deteriorating. While access rates to WSS services remain relatively high, the condition of the infrastructure is threatened by a lack of maintenance. Safely managed treatment and disposal of wastewater lags behind the safely managed provision of the water supply and threatens the quality of water resources used for agricultural purposes. Only 53 percent of the Dominican Republic’s water treatment plants and 26 percent of functioning wastewater treatment plants were estimated to be at adequate operating levels. C. Higher-Level Objectives to which the Project Contributes 11. The proposed project is expected to strengthen the Dominican Republic’s agricultural resilience to extreme natural events and other climate change impacts. It is aligned with Pillar 3 (Building Resilience) of the revised FY15–FY18 Country Partnership Strategy10, the Performance and Learning Review (PLR) of the CPS for the period FY15–FY1811, and with government priorities. The World Bank Group (WBG) will support the Government’s National Development Strategy to increase resilience and improve disaster risk management, through an integrated approach to natural resource management. A Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Option (CAT- DDO (P159351)) operation was approved by the Bank in September 28, 2017 and is providing US$150 million in contingent financing to be used in case of natural catastrophes, including those affecting public health. The proposed project will strengthen the disaster risk management, through improvement of dam operation, and climate change adaptation of agriculture in the targeted areas. The government has declared its commitment to implementing the principles of integrated water resources management to support the sustainable use of natural resources such as soil, water, and forests (including by the agricultural sector). The proposed operation, focused on building resilience in the water and agriculture sectors in two river basins, will support these efforts. 12. The proposed project will also contribute to fulfilling the Dominican Republic’s adaptation and mitigation commitments under the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. The water and agriculture sectors were identified as key sources of the country’s intended nationally determined contributions (INDCs) prepared in August 2015. The elements identified as most vulnerable on the adaptation agenda are: water supply, the energy national system, protected areas, human settlements, and tourism. On the mitigation agenda, agriculture, land use changes, and forest management are key issues. The government has committed to reduce, by 2030, the tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (tCO2e) per capita emissions by 25 percent.12 13. Climate adaptation and mitigation co-benefits. The project will contribute substantially to climate change adaptation13 through activities such as: (i) increasing the resilience of the agricultural sector to climate change and climate variability through the promotion of sustainable landscape management practices such as efficient irrigated agriculture, soil and water conservation, and protected area conservation; (ii) reducing disaster risks and increasing capacity related to dam management and irrigation schemes; and (iii) improving quality and access to water supply and sanitation, and reducing natural resource contamination.. Many activities of the project will also contribute to climate change mitigation14 such as promotion of agroforestry systems and improved livestock management (more production with better ratio of emission by production unit); better dam management and a positive impact on hydropower generation; and improved wastewater management. PROJECT DEVELOPMENT OBJECTIVES A. PDO 15. The project development objective (PDO) is to contribute to improved sustainable landscape management and access to water supply and sanitation services in targeted river basins. 16. The project intervention is based on a multisector and integrated spatial approach to the sustainable management of public assets, goods, and natural resources (soil, water, and vegetation), taking into account upstream/downstream impacts. It combines measures to support sustainable intensification of the use of the most fertile rain-fed and irrigated lands, with habitat restoration and soil and water conservation of degraded land; strengthened resilience of hydraulic assets; a reduction in the pollution load through expanded access to sanitation; and strengthened institutions. These combined measures will target the most vulnerable populations in poor urban and rural areas. The measures will accordingly reduce vulnerability to climate change in target areas, increasing adaptive capacity and reducing the potential for conflict around natural resources, especially water (addressing both quantity and quality aspects). The annex 1 details and illustrates the theory of change that the project foresees. B. Project Beneficiaries 17. III. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Components 23. Component 1: Sustainable Productive Management of Agroecosystems (US$15.1 million from the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development [IBRD]), aims at promoting climate- and environmentally smart practices to improve the resilience of rural producers to flooding, droughts, and other climate change impacts. It is organized under three subcomponents: (i) Subcomponent 1.1: Provision of support for the productive use of natural resources through the carrying out of Sustainable Landscape Management Subprojects. This subcomponent will finance consulting and non-consulting services, goods and works for demand-driven subprojects of investment and technical assistance for sustainable approaches to landscape management. The subprojects will benefit agricultural producers’ organizations, according to the conditions of each agroecosystem, by promoting Sustainable Landscape Management (SLM practices - see details in annex 1) such as: (i) agroforestry systems in the upper section of the river basin; (ii) sustainable ranching systems in the upper and middle sections; and (iii) resilient and efficient irrigated agriculture in collective systems managed jointly by the National Institute of Hydraulic Resources (INDRHI) and by the water users’ associations (WUAs, Juntas de Regantes) in the lower section. The subcomponent will launch targeted calls for proposals and develop productive alliance mechanisms where integrated support is sought to formalize a purchase contract with private actors (supermarkets, exporters, and so on) and/or the public sector (public purchase for schools, and so on). Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 28. Adopt a comprehensive and multisector approach at the river basin level to tackle issues related to the sustainable and productive use of natural resources. In river basins where non-sustainable use of natural resources (soil, water, forests, biodiversity) creates: (i) conflicts between users (irrigation, WSS, hydropower, and so on); (ii) upstream/downstream issues (for example, siltation of dams because of non-sustainable soil management); or (iii) pollution of water sources that affect downstream users (for example, nontreated wastewater in open canal used for irrigation), a comprehensive and multisector approach must be developed at the river basin levels, or at smaller scales such as the watershed or micro watershed levels. 29. Think about investments and institutions. Many implementing agencies in the water sector defer the maintenance of infrastructure amid a lack of revenue. This leads to the implicit adoption of a model of “build- neglect-rebuild.”21 The project will give special attention, especially in the components related to investment in infrastructure (components 2 and 3), to strengthening capacity in order to improve the sustainable management, operation, and maintenance of investments (“Fixing the institutions that fixes the pipes”). The INDRHI, INAPA, WUAs for irrigation, INAPA’s regional water and sanitation providers, and rural water systems communal associations (ASOCARS) will be the main targets of these activities. 30. Strengthen governance and stakeholders’ coordination. Effective approaches to complex and multisectorial problems such as ensuring a sustainable and productive ecosystem or the integrated management of natural resources need to be developed within a solid framework of governance, with effective participation of the different stakeholders. The project will seek to strengthen the existing or future initiatives of local governance (Plan Yaque, Plan Sierra, river basin councils, Water Fund, and so on) and will coordinate with the various projects and programs on the ground to maximize impacts and guarantee sustainability after the project’s closing. 21 Briscoe and Malik, 2006; Lankford and others, 2016. The financial analysis estimated the net incremental income as a result of investments to promote Sustainable Landscape Management. A comparison of “without project” and “with project” situations, considering a timeline of 20 years, was carried out for the agricultural sector and showed a Net Present Value (NPV) of US$23.9 million and an internal rate of return of 20.4 percent. 52. The economic analysis considers three sources of benefits: (i) an increase in agricultural production through the incorporation of SLM and improvement in performances in irrigated areas; (ii) water supply and sanitation benefits, including water services revenues and health benefits accruing from water, sanitation, and sewerage investments; and (iii) carbon sequestration and storage. The economic analysis for the agriculture sector shows a NPV of US$48.5 million, and an internal rate of return of 24.1 percent. The net benefits estimated for water supply and sanitation have a NPV of US$29.2 million and an internal rate of return of 25.7 percent. Finally, the economic benefits for carbon sequestration and storage (over the analysis period of 20 years, the project constitutes a carbon sink of 4.7 million tCO2e) resulted in an NPV of US$103.4 million. 53. The total economic benefit of the project, considering a project cost of US$80 million, is estimated at US$181 million, with an economic internal rate of return of 36.1 percent, which includes the three sources of benefits indicated. Fifty seven percent of the total economic benefits will be public goods (global benefits) corresponding to the value of carbon sequestration, while forty three percent of the economic benefits of the project will be captured by the Dominican Republic, by increasing the resilience of agriculture and WSS services in project areas. 54. Financial and economic returns present a robust behavior, which will create economic and financial benefits even if the project faces contingencies. Investment costs can increase by 30 percent, benefits can be delayed by three years, and benefits can decrease by 30 percent and still generate a positive NPV, in financial and economic terms. Both river basins have serious problems of degradation caused by slash-and- burn agriculture, poor agricultural practices, pollution, and deforestation on very steep slopes, with the consequent loss of soil and siltation of dam reservoirs. Storage losses estimated in 2016 are highly relevant: the storage capacity was reduced by 24 percent for the Tavera reservoir and 23 percent for the Bao reservoir in 43 and 32 years, respectively. 56. Strong interconnection between irrigation infrastructure and WSS aqueducts. In the Yaque del Norte basin, the reservoirs Tavera and Bao are interconnected by a canal which feeds the Cibao-Central aqueduct that supplies water to the cities of Santiago and Moca, and several municipalities with a total population of 3,517,000 inhabitants. Important command area of large-scale irrigation schemes in the lower section of the Yaque del Norte basin that has a positive impact on production but also exerts pressure on the water balance. The total command area adds 77,320 hectares for 16,160 users. 57. Water balance is projected to be in deficit in 2025 in the Yaque del Norte basin. The water balance prepared in 2009 for the formulation of the National Hydrological Plan concluded that the projected water demand in 2025 will substantially increase the water deficit, especially in the Yaque del Norte hydrographic region. The results of the water demand trend and the corresponding deficit consider the average availability of 3,086.46 million cubic meters (m3)/year. 58. Low rates of water conveyance efficiency are mostly due to unlined canals and insufficient canal regulation. In the various systems of the Yaque del Norte basin, there is a high proportion of unlined earth canals: 56 percent of the main canal and 64 percent of the secondary canals. The situation in the Bayaguana irrigation Page 13 The World Bank DR Resilient Agriculture and Integrated Water Resources Management (P163260) scheme in the Ozama watershed is even worse: 79 percent of the main canals are unlined and 48 percent of the secondary canals are earth canals. 59. The combined effects of drought, low conveyance efficiencies, inadequate operation of hydraulic systems, and a lack of coordination between institutions generate important losses. The low water availability during the drought period of 2013–15 affected different areas of the country, affecting both permanent crops and especially seasonal crops produced during the dry/summer season. In the Ulises Francisco Espaillat system (UFE), a reduction of 40 percent of the rice area and 12 percent for other crops was registered in the period 2013–15 compared with historical averages. During the same drought period, the reductions of sowed area in the Las Matas de Santa Cruz systems were: 43 percent for rice; 83 percent for banana; and 71 percent for maize, vegetables, and small fruits (-71 percent). 60. Drainage is insufficient, and salinity is high. The Yaque del Norte Basin is affected by drainage limitations and salinity. Based on information given in the Hydrologic National Plan (2009), estimations show that 26 percent of the area presents limitations and productive restrictions due to drainage problems, while 32 percent of the area is affected by salinity issues both in the soil and water. 61. The country’s installed capacity of wastewater treatment plants is underutilized to the detriment of the environment. In 2015, the country had an estimated wastewater treatment capacity of 11.43 cubic meters per second (m3/s) (which represented 37.33 percent of the flow produced) in 80 existing wastewater treatment plants; however, only 3.32 m3/s were treated, equivalent to 10.52 percent of the flow of wastewater produced. In brief, 39 plants did not comply with the quality standards established by the Ministry of the Environment, of which 6 are located within the Yaque del Norte basin perimeter and currently not complying with quality standards.26 Overall, more than 90 percent of domestic wastewater is not treated, with adverse impacts on the environment: approximately 2.40 million m3/s of untreated wastewater is discharged into the water sources. 62. Women occupy a variety of roles in this continuum, from farm management to farm workers, and compose a slim majority of the smallest farms’ workforce. Data gathered during the closing of the Emer gency Recovery Project show that only 9 percent of the farmers benefitted by the rehabilitation works were female. There are important gaps related to gender equality in the agro-industrial sector, though data are scant. Migration from poor rural areas may disproportionally affect both single and married women. The project aims to address the gender gaps identified above. Under Subcomponent 1.1, several calls for proposals will target women and young farmers, especially for the development of small-scale irrigation subprojects. In addition, a social oversight committee will be established composed of three farmers’ organization representatives, including at least one woman. With respect to WSS investments in small towns, the project will work with local authorities to convene a series of public forums to ensure community input is provided to inform project design and implementation. It will aim to ensure that input is provided from a cross-section of society including women and marginalized populations. In areas with infrastructure investment related to water supply and distribution or collection networks, the project will also carry out consumer research to better understand the demand-side constraints, so that the perspectives of women and marginalized groups will inform project design and implementation. For any investments related to rural water, the project will support INAPA in strengthening ASOCARs. Part of INAPA’s methodology in establishing ASOCARS, is to ensure that women comprise at least 30 percent of the associations. Finally, in order to track progress related to gender gaps in the project, three of the PDO indicators are gender desegregated. 71. Citizen engagement and grievance redress mechanisms. Citizen engagement measures will include: (i) support to the creation and operationalization of water governance organizations, which are a participatory decision-making body made up of water users; (ii) engagement in a proactive communication strategy that will explain to local governments, beneficiaries, and the public at large the benefits from the project for various communities and municipalities; (iii) application of clear and transparent criteria for the selection of subprojects/investments; and (iv) development of robust information requests and grievance redress measures for project activities as a whole (not only for safeguards-related issues). The implementation agency will designate a focal point, that is, the social officer, to act as a liaison for communicating on project-related issues and addressing information requests, feedback, and grievances. Training will be provided to ensure that the focal points have the capacity to share timely information with beneficiaries about project-funded activities, escalate beneficiary feedback to the project management and other decision makers at the local and central levels, and facilitate resolution of grievances. Furthermore, consultations will be carried out where project activities are going to take place and will allow the beneficiary population to receive information, provide feedback, clarify doubts, and express any existing grievances. 72. Labor influx. Risks linked to labor influx are expected to be limited. Worker camps are expected to be set up, especially where civil works take place in rural or remote settings, given that these camps would provide some level of supervision and security, so that public order is maintained, and unlawful conduct minimized. However, these camps are expected to be small to moderate in size. Most of the labor is expected to be local, with a small number of high skilled/technical workers required from outside. The project would incorporate various measures to mitigate potential negative impacts of labor influx, such as requiring that Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs) include labor influx management/camp management measures, enforcing the signing of codes of conduct by all workers, and ensuring that labor-related commitments are reflected in: (i) the bank-borrower legal agreement; and (ii) the contractor’s bidding documents (and subsequently in the borrower-contractor contracts). Proposed intervention Areas. The project will target two of the largest river basins and amongst the most important economically for the country: (i) Ozama-Isabela; and (ii) Yaque del Norte (see map in annex 6). The project intervention areas are vast (7,000 km2 for Yaque del Norte, 2,700 km2 for Ozama-Isabela) and encompasses several provinces (Vega, Santiago de los Caballeros, Santiago Rodríguez, Monte Cristi, Valverde, Dajabón, Monte Plata, y Santo Domingo) and densely inhabited. 86. Yaque del Norte river basin. Land under agricultural uses accounts for 42.6 percent of the total area of the river basin. This includes zones with annual crops that are intensively implemented on 16.7 percent (115,113 hectares), with rice cultivation being the most important, followed by musaceae and tobacco. Subsistence agriculture occupies 9 percent distributed in mountainous terrain, slopes and low rocky terrain. Perennial crops occupy 2.5 percent, almost entirely formed by the coffee crop distributed in the southeastern end of the upper part of the basin area. The pasture surface of the basin represents 14.3 percent and includes intensive and natural pastures, where there is high influence of livestock activity. Other coverages in the basin include savanna vegetation, sparse vegetation, urban areas, dams and bodies of water such as lakes and lagoons. In the area of influence of the basin, there are 77,542 agricultural productive units, covering 1,071,358 hectares. 87. Ozama Isabela River basin. Ozama-Isabela river basin covers a large part of the province of Monte Plata and some municipalities of Santo Domingo province in the lower part of the basin. The basin's vegetation cover, according to the Ministry of Environment and Natural Resources (2012), is composed of an area of 785.43 km² (28.10 percent) covered by forests, represented almost entirely by moist broadleaved forest. The shrub vegetation is minimal, occupying an area of 8.7 km² (0.30 percent). The area devoted to agricultural production includes annual crops where subsistence agriculture predominates, with an area of 929.52 km² (33.25 percent), which mostly includes short cycle crops in rainfed. Other cultivated areas are pineapple with 948ha (0.35 percent) and sugar cane, which occupies 8,734ha (3.15 percent). Perennial crops occupy an area of 24,086ha (8.6 percent), of which 1,989ha (0.7 percent), correspond to citrus, 14,834ha (5.31 percent) to cacao, 7,260ha (2.6 percent) to African palm. The pasture cover is of 48,176ha (17.25 percent), being a high incidence of the cattle activity. Finally, the urban area occupies about 25,103ha. 88. In the Ulises Francisco Espaillat system (UFE), a reduction of 40 percent of rice area and 12 percent for other crops have been registered in the period 2013-2015 compared to historical average. Discharges of untreated urban wastewater with a high content of organic matter tend to decrease dissolved oxygen, mainly in areas of higher population. A case is the Mao - Gurabo Canal, which runs through the city of Mao receiving discharges of solid waste and domestic wastewater in some of its sections. The Mao - Gurabo Canal, was built in 1923, and later lined with concrete in 2004. It crosses the city of Mao where have the area faces a situation of unhealthiness and affectation to the quality of the Water for irrigation due to a combination of factors such as: (i) unplanned urban development, (ii) lack of controls in land uses, (iii) occupation of the canal berm, (iv) deficiencies of solid waste collection and management, (v) the lack sewer system (vi) the construction of houses and the establishment of shops and small industries on the margin of the canal. 101. Quality Parameters in Ozama-Isabela River Basin. Quality of water in the upper basin have acceptable values of electro-conductivity and dissolved oxygen (OD). The OD decreases below the minimum acceptable value (5 mg / l), downstream of the confluence of the Isabel River with the Ozama River. Microbiological analysis records indicate that values of fecal coliforms (CF) and total coliforms (CT) in the Isabela and Ozama rivers, prior to their confluence, are already very high (24,000 NMP/100 ml in the Ozama River in Sabana Perdida and on the 110,000 to NMP/100 ml in the river Isabela at the location of Fabrica de Clavos). Likewise, nitrate levels (increase above the maximum acceptable threshold of 3 mg / l) and nitrites indicate that the deterioration of water quality increases in the lower part of the basin, progressively towards the area of the estuary where Parameters, chemical oxygen demand (COD), and BOD, greater than 2 mg / l, among others reach levels exceed standards35. 102. Flood damages. Irrigation systems located mainly on the lower area of the Yaque del Norte basin suffer repeatedly damages and losses due to extreme events of flooding. The estimations show that floods with recurrence periods of 2 to 5 years affect at least 3,600 hectares of the 20,427 hectares of the Fernando Valerio irrigation system (17.50 percent of the area), affecting 2,800 (42 percent) of the users and generating RD $ 23 million in crop losses per episode. The Chacuey Canal is also affected by floods of the Chacuey River in La Pinta, with return periods of 5 years (floods of just 20 m3/s), generating estimated losses of RD $ 7 million per event, affecting 500 hectares (47.62 percent of the system) and 250 users (41.67 percent). 103. In brief, 39 plants did not comply with the quality standards established by the Ministry of the Environment, of which 6 are located within the Yaque del Norte perimeter and currently not complying with quality standards36. The approach will take a multisector and integrated spatial approach to the management of land, water and vegetation, taking account of upstream and downstream impacts; combine measures to support sustainable intensification on the most fertile rain fed and irrigated lands, with landscape restoration and soil and water conservation on degraded land; aim to create resilience in hydraulic assets and agriculture through a balance of environmental, social, and economic benefits from the use of land, water, forests and trees within a broader pattern of land and water use, and reduce pollution load through expansion of access to sanitation. These combined measures will target to achieve reducing the vulnerability to the adverse impacts of climate change, increasing the adaptive capacity to respond to the impacts of climate change and reducing potential conflict around the resources, especially the water resources (quantity and quality aspects). 105. The proposed project will apply an integrated landscape management approach at watershed level consistent with adaptation needs identified in the INDCs 37. The project proposes an integrated approach for the agricultural sector with the following four entry points: (i) making producers more productive and resilient, while lowering their carbon footprint, through climate smart agriculture38; (ii) integrating producers into domestic and global value chains; (iii) strengthening institutions and the provision of public goods; and (iv) promoting the sustainable use and management of natural resources at the watershed level. 106. Building resilience in the agricultural sector (rain fed agriculture in upper and middle river basin and in irrigated agriculture on the lower river basin) to address increased evapotranspiration and water scarcity during these months will be critical to support food security and competitiveness of agricultural sector. In order to support adaptation of farmers to the detrimental effects of climate change, this project will help farmers to address soil erosion and damaging floods and landslides by strongly promoting reforestation initiatives; will help to secure livelihoods by mainstreaming disaster risk management activities into agricultural interventions; will provide plans and technical assistance to farmers to halt land degradation and to engage in sustainable practices, such as the planting of appropriate crops for steep sloped land; will promote livelihood diversification; and improve irrigation infrastructure management to agricultural areas at the river basin level. 37 In its Intended Nationally Determined Contribution (INDC), the proposal of the Government for mitigation is to reduce the GHG emission by 25% in 2030, in relation to the 2010 baseline (3.6t of CO2 equivalent) and the agricultural sector can play an important role in achieving this objective. 38 Climate-smart agriculture (CSA) is an approach that seeks to increase productivity in an environmentally and socially sustainable way, strengthen farmers’ resilience to climate change, and reduce agriculture’s contribution to climate change by reducing greenhouse gas emissions and increasing carbon storage on farmland. Strategy of prioritization. Considering the vast areas of selected river basins, the project will give priority to its interventions in the following prioritized areas to ensure concrete, reachable and measurable impacts: (i) Priority No. 1: Micro-watersheds of dam reservoirs (mainly Tavera, Bao, Monción, Chacuey, Maguaca, Mijo dams) are priority areas for protection against: (i) sedimentation (due to inadequate use of agricultural soils and pressure on forest cover); (ii) pollution of stored water (due to lack of sanitation upstream of the basin and / or inadequate management of pesticides) and; (iii) reduction of hydrological inflows (due to lack of source protection, reduction of forest cover, etc.). INDRHI has already prepared the Management Plans for Maguaca and Chacuey dams that will be used in the project. (ii) Priority No2: Areas of existing and/or potential conflicts on use of the water resource, both on quality and quantity aspects such as, for example, in the zones of Monsieur Bogaert and Ulises Francisco (Cienfuegos) with competitive water demands between water supply and irrigation (intake for water supply is withdrawing water from the irrigation canal). The project will be able to intervene by financing solutions of water economy for irrigation sector (improvement of efficiency at different levels: conveyance, distribution and/or on-farm application), for WSS (reduction of losses) and/or increase of water availability (mobilization of additional water resources) that would reduce the potential conflicts. (iii) Priority No3: Priority areas resulting from planning exercises at watershed or sub-watershed scale. These intervention areas should be prioritized based on the results of the Master Plans of Watersheds or Sub-basins (to be developed during project implementation) and / or planning exercises of actions carried out by institutions with different scales. The project activities under Priorities 1 and 2 will be well-defined at the time of the project’s submission to the Board, which will ensure a quick implementation of project during the first two years. The activities under priorities 3 will be selected based on planning exercises to be carried out in parallel during years 1 and 2. 116. The Subcomponent will finance Sustainable Landscape Management subprojects for demand-driven subprojects of investments and technical assistance. The subprojects will benefit agricultural producers' organizations, according to the conditions of each agroecosystem, by promoting Sustainable Landscape Management (SLM) practices such as: (i) Agroforestry systems in upper section of river basin in systems including commercial production such as avocado, cocoa, coffee, macadam nuts ; (ii) Sustainable ranching systems in upper and middle sections of river basin, especially for the dairy livestock and; (iii) Efficient irrigated agriculture in collective systems co-managed by INDRHI (Instituto Nacional de Recursos Hidráulicos) and the Water Users Associations in lower section of river basin for rice and banana (exported organic production and non-organic production). The Sustainable Landscape Management practices refer to a combination of at least two technologies and approaches to increase land quality and restore degraded land—for example, agronomic, vegetative, structural, and management measures that, applied as a combination, increase the connectivity between protected areas, forest land, rangeland, and agriculture land: (i) Conservation agriculture is a system characterized by three basic principles: minimum soil disturbance, a degree of permanent soil cover, and crop rotation. (ii) Agroforestry systems is a collective name for land use systems and practices in which woody perennials are deliberately integrated with agricultural crops and/or animals on the same land Page 40 The World Bank DR Resilient Agriculture and Integrated Water Resources Management (P163260) management unit. For example: intercropping with trees, alley cropping with trees, improved tree/bush fallows, shelterbelts, hedgerows, parklands, multistory cropping. (iii) Fertility-boosting technologies aim to improve soil fertility and while enhancing soil structure and water infiltration through organic manures, compost, green manure, or mineral fertilizers. For example: green manure, legume inter-planting, inorganic fertilizers, integrated soil fertility management. (iv) Terraces refers to the practice of reducing slope steepness and/or length. This term also includes permanent vegetative strips, which can develop into terraces over time. For example: terraces, bunds, ditches, vegetative strips. (v) Rainwater harvesting is the collection and concentration of rainfall runoff for crop production—or for improving the performance of grass and trees—in dry areas where moisture deficit is the primary limiting factor. For example: micro-catchments (planting pits, half-moons, V-shaped), diversion/drainage ditches, cut-off drains. (vi) Pastoralism and rangeland management refers to managing livestock through free grazing on open-access natural or semi-natural grassland, grassland with trees, and/or open woodlands. The animal owners may have a permanent residence, but the animals are moved to distant grazing areas; the movement is adapted to the availability of resources. For example: grazing on natural or semi-natural grassland, grassland with trees, open woodlands; movement adapted to availability of resources, including temporal overlap with cropland. (vii) Improved grazing land management refers to the management of cropping systems and livestock on farmland, changing control and regulation of grazing pressure through rotation, (temporal) enclosure, cut and carry, pasture enrichment, and species and fire management. For example: rotational grazing, temporal enclosure (fencing), cut and carry, enrichment planting/improved pastures, eradication of invasive species. (viii) Integrated crop livestock systems refer to producing both crops and livestock in a coordinated framework that includes livestock management, fodder production, and controlled grazing. For example: fodder production/cut and carry, controlled grazing on cropland, manuring, residue, livestock management. (ix) Natural resource management refers to all technologies that ensure the sustainable use of natural areas, either by regenerating or protecting them. For example: enclosure, enrichment planting, selective felling, fire management, and extraction of renewable resources (e.g. timber, fiber, nuts, and saps). (x) Catchment management encompasses a set of different dependent measures in a certain area, with overall planning and management. For example: combinations of technologies, including shelterbelts (wind erosion), dams (macro-catchments), and gully stabilization. (xi) Improved Irrigated practices such as Alternate Wetting and Drying techniques for rice, water savings technologies such as drip or sprinkler. 120. Context 161. The National Development Strategy (END, Country Vision to 2030, Law number 1 of 2012), considers planning and management of water resources, taking into account the river basin as a central element. This is the approach the Dominican Republic authorities are taking to address the various water problems, both due to the complexity of the problems, the interaction of the different phases and the process of the hydrological cycle. This includes the derivations or extractions of water for different uses, and alterations of water quality due to pollution and soil degradation; therefore, the intervention strategy in the main watersheds of the country should be carried out with a multisectoral scope. The sectoral objectives are expressed in the National Development Strategy 2030 (Law No. 1-12), as follows: a) 100 percent of the population has access to water from the public network inside or outside the household by 2020, b) 100 percent of the population has access to improved health services by 2030 and c) a sanitary surveillance system for the quality of water supply to reduce health risks associated with water. The Component 3 will contribute to these higher-level objectives. 162. INAPA’s Role within targeted watersheds. Deconcentrated Model for Small Towns. INAPA currently manages 342 aqueducts distributed in twenty- five provinces, where the main administrative, commercial and operational functions are concentrated at the central level, thus leaving the provincial units with limited operational capacity and little functionality, affecting the service performance. In view of the above, there was a need to look for alternatives that allowed INAPA to ensure the efficiency of the services provided, through the delegation of functions from the central to local levels. The deconcentration entails that fundamental decisions remain at the central level of the institution (large investments, expansion, human resources, etc.), but decisions can be taken locally to make repairs in networks and other aspects of operation and maintenance, including commercial management, where the unit is able to administer and reinvest a certain percentage of the amount collected54. 171. Financial Analysis a. Water Supply and Sanitation Interventions 226. The financial analysis of the water supply and sanitation interventions assesses INAPA’s financial solvency and revenue sustainability, including the financial flows and revenues from user fees or Government’s contributions. Based on INAPA’s monthly financial data of 2017, the total gross revenue from water sales amounts for US$ 11.5 million per year, and the total operating expenses reach US$ 29.8 million a year. The ratio of total operating expenditures to gross water sales revenues is 2.5, which indicates that INAPA is under financial stress over the short-run. However, INAPA also receives RD 74.7 million (US$ 1.6 million) per month on average from Government transfers. Net water sales, represent only 31 percent of the total operating revenues when adding Government’s contributions, since Government’s contributions are close to two thirds of the total operating revenues of INAPA. 227. The structure of INAPA’s operating revenues includes the total operating revenues from net water sales, the Government’s contributions, and other operating revenues. INAPA’s operating expenditures are predominantly concentrated on salaries, wages and benefits (58 percent), infrastructure works, services and supplies (11 percent), payable accounts of services (30 percent), and transfers and taxes (1 percent). At the current financial structure, the financial rate of return of INAPA (operating revenues-operating expenses) is -3.7 percent discounted at 10 percent under a 20-year timespan. Due to INAPA’s high financial allocations to wages, increasing payable accounts and the high proportion of revenues depending largely on government contributions and water sales, some scenarios were built to identify the combination of trends in these budget elements that may lead to positive financial returns. 228. Keeping everything else constant, an increase in payable accounts by 50 percent spread throughout the entire time-span of the project (20 years) will lead to a decrease in the financial solvency, with a financial rate of return of -8.0 percent. However, a progressive reduction in government expenditures by 1 percentage point per year, and an increase of 20 percent in water sales over the next 20 years, can lead to a financial rate of return of 5 percent. Only increasing water sales alone, leads to a positive financial rate of return to 1.2 percent, discounting at 12 percent. b. Agricultural and Sustainable Landscape Management Interventions 229. A cost benefit analysis method was applied for the agricultural component, which considers the increase in net gained income as a result of investments to promote sustainable landscape management. For this, it was compared the situation of farmers without project (business as usual), with a with-project situation, i.e., the incorporation of sustainable landscape management proposed by the project. 230. For obtaining the financial benefits, it was applied the Marginal Productivity Method. This method consists of estimating the net present value of the highest agricultural production given the improved productive capacity of agricultural production systems as a result of the investments to promote sustainable landscape management. In its 2012 Environment Strategy, the World Bank adopted a corporate mandate to conduct greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions accounting for investment lending. The quantification of GHG emission is an important step in managing and ultimately reducing GHG emission and is becoming a common practice for many international financial institutions. The World Bank adopted the Ex-Ante Carbon-balance Tool (EX-ACT), which was developed by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO) in 2010 to assess the impact of agricultural investment lending on GHG emission and carbon sequestration. EX-ACT allows the assessment of a project’s net carbon-balance, defined as the net balance of CO2 equivalent GHG that were emitted or sequestered because of project implementation compared to a without project scenario. EX-ACT estimates the carbon stock changes (emissions or sinks), expressed in equivalent tons of CO2 per hectare and year. In the case of investments in Water, Wastewater and Sanitation the methodology applies a two-prone approach. Investments aim to rehabilitate the potable water distribution will follow the hypothesis that improvements will reduced leakages which decreases energy needs for water pumping following the approach of Hendrickson and Horvath (2014) 72. Investment supporting the wastewater and sanitation treatment will be calculated based on IPCC’s wastewater treatment and discharge tier 1 methodology as described in chapter 6 of the 2006 IPCC guidelines volume 573. 260. The objectives of the project are to promote sustainable management of productive agroecosystems, to enhance resilience of hydraulic assets and to increase quality and access to water supply and sanitation in the targeted river basins. The project aims strengthen the capacity for landscape and watershed-level interventions by: (i) providing financing for demand-driven investments in rain-fed and irrigated agro-ecosystems to enhance sustainable natural resources management (soil, water and forest), to boost productivity and resilience to the impacts of climate change; (ii) rehabilitating hydraulic assets (dams, irrigation and water supply sanitation) to enhance resilience and improve quality of services; (iii) improving institutional capacities and infrastructure of rural water and sanitation services to reduce pollution and health risks. It will also support national programs promoting water security and governance, sustainable forest conservation and soil conservation through innovative instruments, including exiting initiatives of Payment for Environmental Service (PES). Crosscutting support to improve irrigation and water conservation practices will help reduced the impact on water management and will increase resilience of agricultural production. 261.
