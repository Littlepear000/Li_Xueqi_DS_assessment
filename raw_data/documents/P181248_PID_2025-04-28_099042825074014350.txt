Introduction and Context Project Strategic Context 1. The proposed Solutions for the Internally Displaced and Host Communities Project (SOLID) aims to improve access to resilient and inclusive basic services and economic opportunities for internally displaced persons (IDPs) and host communities in displacement-affected Local Government Areas (LGAs) of Northern Nigeria. This operation is crucial for addressing the protracted displacement crisis in the region, where communities have been severely impacted by insecurity, rapid population growth, unplanned physical development, and limited economic opportunities. SOLID shifts from a short-term emergency response approach to displacement, focused on humanitarian assistance, to a long-term development approach focused on system changes (physical infrastructure and governance) and local capacity building to foster resilient and inclusive development and to improve livelihoods in displacement-affected communities. 2. The development relevance of the proposed project is underscored by the significant number of IDPs in Northern Nigeria—over 3.5 million—one of the largest and fastest-growing displaced populations globally. Insecurity, driven by conflict, violence, and climate-induced disasters, has fueled large waves of internal displacement across the North-East (NE), North-West (NW), and North-Central (NC) regions.1 Nigeria, Africa’s second largest 1 Over 65 percent of IDPs in Northern Nigeria are in the NE region (approximately 2.3 million IDPs as of June 2023) and 95 percent of them are in Borno, Adamawa and Yobe (the ‘BAY states’), according to International Organization on Migration (IOM) data. Borno, Page 2 The World Bank Solutions for the Internally Displaced and Host Communities Project (P181248) economy, also has the world's second-largest population of people living in extreme poverty. Since 2018/19, an additional 44 million people have fallen into poverty, making more than half of all Nigerians (55 percent) estimated to live in poverty by 2024, based on World Bank projections. The poverty rate in Northern Nigeria (covering the NE, NW, and NC regions) is projected to be even higher at 73 percent in 2024. Establishing direct causal links between conflict, displacement, and poverty is complex, but there are areas in Nigeria where these factors clearly intersect, especially in Sokoto in the NW and Taraba in the NE, two states affected by displacement that show the highest levels of poverty in the country (over 90 percent). Due to Nigeria’s size and role in Africa, addressing development challenges in Northern Nigeria supports the World Bank’s mission to reduce poverty. The proposed project is consistent with Nigeria's broader development objectives by encouraging inclusive growth and stability through a strategic framework aimed at providing sustainable solutions for IDPs and host communities. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Northern Nigeria faces four significant development challenges that hinder durable solutions for IDPs and host communities; the first challenge, which is a major driver of the other three challenges, is spatial—IDP inflows have reshaped state capital cities - such as Maiduguri (Borno), Yola (Adamawa), and Makurdi (Benue) - and other towns (centers of other LGAs within the states), contributing to urban sprawl,2 poor access to services, and increased vulnerability to natural disasters. Unplanned spatial growth has exacerbated disaster risks associated with climate change, such as increased flooding, especially in IDP settlements constructed near rivers and communities where sufficient drainage and waste management systems are lacking. Absent or inadequate waste collection and treatment in these communities leads to dumped waste clogging drains which is exacerbating flood risk. Analyses of Maiduguri and Yola indicate significant proportions of infrastructure, built up areas and population are exposed to river and rainwater flooding, with IDPs susceptible to repeated displacement due to their location in flood-prone areas. Unplanned spatial growth has also increased heat-induced risks associated, particularly in dense and poor urban communities due to the lack of green spaces, which is driving the urban heat island (UHI) effect. 4. The second challenge is physical—IDP inflows have placed pressure on already strained infrastructure in host communities. For example, in Maiduguri and other Borno LGAs,3 residents lack access to basic services like water and solid waste management (SWM). In Adamawa, insufficient water supply systems have resulted in IDPs having to travel 10 minutes or more to access drinking water, relying mainly on handpumps.4 Spatial mapping reveals limited access to toilets and garbage disposal at the LGA level in Adamawa. While access to school and healthcare facilities is adequate within LGAs in Borno and Adamawa, the quality of education and health services remains a concern.5 The which has been the epicenter of fighting involving Boko Haram since 2014, hosts the highest number of IDPs, with nearly 1.7 million IDPs, representing over one-quarter of the state’s total population and almost half of total IDPs in the North. Additionally, field visits have pointed to a general lack of communal spaces and facilities for collective social activities and gatherings within host communities, which are essential for supporting community building and stabilization efforts. Under future climate change scenarios, highly variable and erratic but incrementally decreasing precipitation and increasing temperatures are predicted within Northern Nigeria. Climate-induced droughts combined with limited water availability or scarcity is another challenge faced by IDPs and host communities which is driving the need for increased water supply capacity and a transition to climate-resilient water management systems. 5. The third challenge is weak institutional capacity to deliver services in host communities. LGAs face overlapping governance structures that complicate service delivery and planning.6 Local governments lack financial and staffing autonomy, limiting their ability to implement long-term development strategies.7 Decision-making is highly centralized at the state level, and local development plans are outdated, 8 with insufficient representation from local communities and IDPs in planning processes.9 This lack of inclusion weakens governance effectiveness to deliver services that address the specific needs of each community. 6. The fourth challenge relates to socio-economic impacts. The rapid and large-scale inflow of IDPs into host communities due to conflicts related to the Boko Haram insurgency, farmer-herder conflicts, and banditry has strained social cohesion10 within communities and heightened competition for limited resources and economic opportunities. Both IDPs and residents face livelihoods struggles, with many relying on subsistence farming and having limited access to land, equipment, and training.11 Livelihood struggles are compounded by climate change, prompting the need for farmers to adapt (for example, through modern climate-smart agricultural practices) and diversify livelihood activities, and address security risks. Women and youth confront additional socio-economic barriers as cultural norms in some communities dissuade women from engaging in economic activities and present greater risks gender-based violence (GBV), and some unemployed youth turning to violence. Citizen organizations to support enhancing livelihoods, such as cooperatives, exist within some host communities and have included IDPs.12 However, cooperatives face significant challenges with providing benefits to their members, such as skills training, finance for operations and expansion, and the ability to provide links and access to markets. The gap between public services and community needs threatens social stability and exacerbates conflict risks. 6 For instance, the Borno State Urban Planning and Development Board is responsible for the initiation and actualization of physical development programs in Maiduguri, but multiple agencies carry out similar functions in the city. 7 Across host communities, local governments face significant gaps in financial resources and management, which also has negative impacts on the provision of services. 8 For instance, the Maiduguri Development Plan (1977-1987) has not been reviewed after its expiration and the law governing the Borno State Urban Planning and Development Board has not been fully implemented. 9 This was evident during site visits in Adamawa, Yobe, and Benue states in which the project team observed almost no representation by IDPs in local government institutions. The Federal Government of Nigeria (FGN), in collaboration with international partners, has provided support to IDPs in Northern Nigeria for over the past decade, focusing primarily on humanitarian relief, emergency shelter, and access to basic services.13 International organizations such as the UN system, International Committee of the Red Cross, and various foreign governments have played a critical role in delivering these interventions. Additionally, the NE states of Borno, Adamawa, and Yobe (the ‘BAY’ states) have contributed to emergency recovery in their states through their state budgets, including through the IDA financing towards the Multi-Sectoral Crisis Recovery Project (MCRP). While these efforts have provided critical short-term support, they have not addressed the long-term development challenges faced by states and LGAs managing protracted displacement, where IDPs have been living in a host community for four or more years. Recognizing the need for a transition from humanitarian aid to sustainable solutions, the FGN adopted the 2021 National Policy on Internally Displaced Persons (National IDP Policy herein),14 which outlines policy goals15 to address internal displacement in Nigeria, and established the National Commission for Refugees, Migrants, and Internally Displaced Persons (NCFRMI) to implement it. These initiatives provide a foundation for long-term interventions but require further institutional and financial capacity at the state and local levels to be effectively operationalized.16 8. The proposed SOLID project shall provide support over and above on-going programs, as an incremental effort to address internal displacement in Nigeria. The proposed project aims to address the development challenges associated with protracted displacement and provide long-term sustainable solutions for IDPS and host communities that go beyond the life of the project. Sustainable solutions include: i) climate-resilient and well-maintained infrastructure; ii) social cohesion and community resilience through community participation - in development planning processes and economic cooperatives - and integration of IDPs; iii) livelihoods support that allows both IDPs and local residents to start new economic activities and sustain them over time; and iv) strengthened institutions to respond to demographic shocks resulting from forced displacement. The proposed SOLID project’s integrated development approach will ensure that IDPs and host communities can transition from dependency on humanitarian assistance to self-reliance and resilience, aligning with the National IDP Policy and the FGN’s long-term development vision. B. Theory of Change and PDO Indicators 8. The Theory of Change for the proposed project (figure 2) is based on addressing the development challenges of displacement-affected states, LGAs, and communities in Northern Nigeria through: (a) investing in climate-resilient infrastructure and strengthening the institutional capacity of state and local governments and communities to improve service delivery; and (b) providing livelihood support and strengthening community integration for IDPs and local residents. The proposed project adopts a three-pronged approach combining a whole-of-government strategy, people- in-place methods, and community engagement for tailored solutions. This approach ensures coordination between national, state, and local governments to implement solutions for IDPs and host communities. It integrates the needs of displaced people and the impacts on the place where they settle while fostering active and inclusive community participation, including vulnerable groups such as women and youth. The proposed project incorporates three Performance-Based Conditions (PBCs) with clearly defined results that include budget allocation at the state level. An IPF-PBC operation aims to encourage institutional reforms, create enabling policies, and build long-term capacity. The three PBCs will support and incentivize the achievement of desired project outputs/outcomes: (i) Improved management of assets by participating local government units (LGUs) through issuance of asset inventory reports and operation and maintenance (O&M) plans; (ii) The data gaps on displacement- related vulnerabilities are addressed comprehensively; and (iii) Steps taken to facilitate full integration and participation of IDPs in long-term development processes. Two factors have been considered in the allocations for the individual PBCs: (i) the relative importance of the indicator in the entire set of indicators; and (ii) the need to match disbursements with cash outflows on project activities. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 22. At the national level, the Federal Ministry of Budget and Economic Planning (FMBEP) will establish a Federal Project Management Unit (FPMU) for overall project implementation, coordination, fiduciary responsibilities (financial management and procurement), monitoring and evaluation (M&E), and E&S management. The FPMU will be headed by a National Project Coordinator and supported by regional technical units (TUs) from the NEDC and NCFRMI, with roles outlined in MOUs between the FMBEP, and responsibilities of the FPMU and the TUs detailed in the Project Implementation Manual (PIM). Leveraging the technical expertise of other federal MDAs, the FPMU will oversee a single Designated (Special) Account for project funds and ensure the required FM infrastructure is in place. A National Steering Committee (NSC), chaired by the Minister of the FMBEP, will provide overall project oversight and institutional coordination, with representation from key federal ministries, state commissioners of budget and economic planning, and humanitarian agencies like IOM/UNHCR. The NSC will be mandated to meet at least biannually. A Federal Technical Committee (FTC), chaired by the Permanent Secretary of the FMBEP, will provide technical advice and include relevant directors from participating federal MDAs, including development commissions in addition to technical experts. 23. At the state level, each participating Tier 1 state will establish a state PCU which will be responsible for state- level project implementation. For the BAY states, the PCUs under the MCRP will transition to serve as the state PCUs for SOLID. Other participating states shall establish their own PCUs, learning from the BAY states’ experience under the MCRP.42 State Steering Committees (SSCs), chaired by respective governors or their delegates, will provide state-level policy guidance and coordinate with humanitarian agencies in Tier 1 states. The SSCs will include commissioners of relevant State MDAs. State Technical Committees (STCs), chaired by state commissioners of budget and economic planning and including the permanent secretaries of relevant state MDAs, will offer technical advice for state-level project implementation. Tier 2 states will assign a focal point rather than establish a PCU while the FPMU will support the implementation of TA with the concerned state MDAs. 42 One of the lessons learned from the MCRP is that coordination between the PCUs and state agencies should start from the project’s inception to ensure successful implementation of project activities. Given the current weak institutional capacity to deliver services in host communities, the institutional capacity risk is rated as Substantial. Overlapping state and local governance structures have complicated service delivery and community planning and development. Community investment decision-making has been highly centralized at the state level, with limited representation of local communities in the planning process, while across host communities, LGUs have faced significant gaps in financial and technical capacity to implement long-term development strategies. The proposed SOLID project aims to build institutional capacity at both the state and local levels and to empower LGUs and communities to take an active role in the planning, implementation and O&M of investments. 28.
