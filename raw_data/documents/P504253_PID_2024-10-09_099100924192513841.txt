Brazil’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is still recovering from the compounded effects of economic stagnation in the 2010s and the COVID-19 recession. Growth has rebounded since 2021 above most regional peers (cumulative +6.0 percent between 2022 and 2023). This has been driven primarily by fiscal stimulus, increased public spending, transfers to low-income households, and record levels of agricultural production, particularly in 2023. Growth slowed in 2024, highlighting the need for further reforms aimed at enhancing competitiveness and efficiency of expenditures. Necessary structural reforms include further improvement in the business climate, reduced financial and market distortions, increased infrastructure investment, greater integration of global value chains, and improved educational quality. 3. Poverty and inequality remain significant challenges. After a sharp increase from 25.3 to 29.4 percent over 2019-21 amid the COVID recession, the poverty rate has decreased since 2021 and is projected to reach 20.5 percent by 2026. This is thanks to the recent economic rebound, labor market improvements (with the unemployment rate falling to 6.9 percent in June 2024), and consistent social protection policies. However, as of 2023, 43 million people (21.3 percent of the population) still lived below the poverty line (US$6.85 per day). Brazil ranks ninth globally in levels of inequality,3 and access to services and opportunities varies sharply across subregions and population segments. On average, rural areas are twice as poor as urban areas.4 Women and minorities, especially Afro-descendants and members of indigenous populations (IPs), have the lowest rates of access to job opportunities and lower wages on average,5 and poverty rates among them are the highest.6 4. Over the past decades, transport infrastructure development in Brazil has faced challenges due to fluctuating public investment policies and periodic economic crises. As a result, Brazil continues to rank low in terms of infrastructure quality,7 which poses challenges to the country’s growth and inclusion efforts.8 The poor quality of transport infrastructure has negatively impacted people’s mobility and increased the cost of doing business, leading to reduced accessibility and high logistics expenses. Brazil is highly vulnerable to climate change, which particularly affects those living in poverty. It ranks 27th on the Climate Risk Index out of 182 countries.11 The country experiences annual floods and droughts, costing over BRL 13.33 billion (US$2 billion), and these events are expected to intensify.12 By 2040, the frequency of intense rainfall is projected to increase by at least 20 percent compared to 1970–1999 levels.13 Climate change will have widespread negative effects, hindering growth and Brazil’s inclusion efforts. It will reduce agricultural productivity, increase rainforest fires, and lead to more severe droughts and floods.14 Biodiversity loss and ecosystem degradation— especially in the Amazon, Mata Atlântica,15 and Cerrado—are expected. In rural areas, access to services, jobs, and markets will become more difficult during rainy seasons as roads become impassable. Although Brazil’s per capita emissions are lower than other countries in the Latin American and the Caribbean (LAC) region, greenhouse gas emissions have risen over the past three decades, with transport being the fastest-growing contributor.16 6. In Brazil, Santa Catarina is one of the states most affected by natural disasters and climate change.17 Its vulnerability to extreme weather has resulted in numerous disasters, causing significant economic and human losses. In the past two decades, over 30 tornadoes have impacted the western region, particularly around Chapecó, affecting more than 70,000 people. Meanwhile, floods have devastated communities in the east, leading to high death tolls and widespread displacement. Over a 32-year period, the state recorded 6,560 incidents, with 311 deaths and 1.1 million people displaced. Forty-six percent of these incidents occurred in the last decade. From 2002 to 2012, floods and landslides in Santa Catarina accounted for 11 percent of similar events nationwide. According to the Atlas Digital de Desastres no Brasil,18 natural disasters have caused BRL 54 billion in losses to the SSC over the past 25 years, including BRL 3.5 billion in damages to the state's industry and BRL 3.7 million in the agriculture sector. 7. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. The road network in Santa Catarina is in poor condition. The road network in Santa Catarina consists of 6,289 kilometers of state highways, of which 82 percent are single-lane paved roads, primarily asphalted. Eighteen percent are unpaved roads. The municipal road system adds another 66,607 kilometers—mostly unpaved. According to the National Confederation of Transport (Confederação Nacional de Transporte – CNT)22 in 2023 approximately 74 percent of the SSC‘s paved roads were in regular, poor, or very poor condition, despite their strategic role for mobility and logistics in the SSC and in Brazil. 9. Poor road network conditions contribute to the deterioration of road safety, a significant issue in Brazil and critical in the SSC. According to Ministry of Health data, there were 33,894 traffic-related deaths in Brazil in 2022, a 6.1 percent increase from 2019. In the SSC the death toll reached 18 per 100,000 inhabitants in 2022, against a national average of 16 per 100,000 inhabitants, affecting life expectancy in the state.23 In total, 925 persons died on roads in the SSC in 2022.24 Beyond the human drama of this figure, road safety also has a high economic costs for the Country resulting from: (i) the management of traffic crashes; (ii) injuries with long-lasting impacts, affecting ability to work or care for others; and (iii) future productivity of young generations, as most of affected individuals are young, educated people. Overall, it is estimated that road safety costs represent a staggering 5 percent of the country’s annual GDP. 10. Considering the significant adverse impact of climate events in Santa Catarina, climate change is a key criterion when prioritizing road selection for the project. In agreement with the Government of Santa Catarina (GoSC), Phase 3 of the MPA will benefit around 1,225 kilometers of roads, organized into clusters to enable more efficient and effective management. This clustered approach will also facilitate the broader implementation of long-term Contratos de Reabilitação e Manutenção (CREMA)—or performance-based contracts for rehabilitation and road maintenance— across the entire state road network. The selected lots are all in areas of moderate to high geological and hydrological risk according to the Brazil Geological Service’s susceptibility maps concerning mass movements and flooding. Those lots are distributed across the Alto Vale (257 km), Brusque (193 km), and Planalto (586 km) regions. The project will support the SSC to embark on a wide adoption of performance-based conditions (PBCs) and to develop a PPP program for roads. The MPA seeks to break the cycle of reactive road asset management by replacing it with proactive maintenance. The proposed Phase 3 of the Brazil Pro-Roads MPA will support the State Secretariat of Infrastructure and Mobility of Santa Catarina (SIE-SC) to improve the management of road assets by delegating road infrastructure management to the private sector. This will be achieved through two modalities of long-term PBCs: CREMA DBM and CREMA PPP (see box 1.1 below). Those contracts will enable improved climate resilience and road safety. The SSC will both benefit from and contribute to the learning agenda that aims at improving the technical and institutional capacity of the MPA participants in the use of PBCs. Phase 3 will also support the SSC in developing a roads PPP Program by piloting one PPP operation, leveraging the positive results 25 of private participation in road management in Brazil. Box 1.1: Brazil Pro-Roads CREMA Modalities The Bank introduced multi-year rehabilitation and maintenance performance-based contracts in Brazil in the early 2000s. These were called Contratos de Reabilitação e Manutenção (CREMA)—or performance-based contracts for rehabilitation and road maintenance. This contractual modality was piloted in several states and at the federal level, often under World Bank-supported operations over the past two decades. They aimed at transferring to the private sector the technical responsibility of road infrastructure management via medium- to long-term contracts paid upon the achievement of pre-determined outcomes. The approach has demonstrated interesting results, improving the effectiveness of road asset management and reducing its cost by 20 percent on average for equivalent or better road conditions than the traditional contracting model.26 Among other limitations, though, contract duration was capped at 5 years in the context of the prevailing public procurement regulatory context. There has been a change, however, in the public procurement laws: contracts can now be authorized for a duration of up to 10 years. This, combined with the possibilities offered by the PPP regulation for even longer contracts27, and a renewed approach to the CREMA contracts, will further delegate responsibility to the private sector for infrastructure management. Along with the extended contract duration, the private sector will assume full technical responsibility in selecting rehabilitation and maintenance solutions. The contracts will also include targeted climate resilience / road safety improvements. In that context, two main CREMA types will be supported under the MPA: - CREMA-Design-Build-Maintain (DBM): This will involve contracts of 8 to 10 years, which will follow a Design- Build-Operate-Maintain (DBOM) approach and will be contracted under the new public procurement regulation. Payment of rehabilitation works will be done upon the delivery of outputs that will follow the contactors’ own design (except improvements), decided by the public sector. Maintenance services will be paid upon achievements of pre- determined standards of road conditions. Mechanisms of guaranty retentions on payments during the rehabilitation phase, which is expected to represent up to 70-80 percent of the contract value, will require the mobilization of private finance in an amount estimated on average at 5 percent of the contracts.28 - CREMA-Public-Private Partnership (PPP): This will involve contracts ranging from 10 years to 25 years and will follow a Design-Finance-Build-Operate-Maintain (DFBOM) approach, contracted under the PPP regulation. The contract’s full financing will be provided by the private sector to implement the rehabilitation works defined by the contractor itself, with improvement works defined by the delegating authority. The Brazil Pro-Roads MPA is expected to contribute to and strengthen Brazil’s competitiveness and resilience, in line with FY24-28 Country Partnership Framework (CPF) objectives (report CPF0000013).35 With over two-thirds of land transport in Brazil done by road, and with high logistics costs from unsuitable infrastructures, improving road sector management is key for the nation’s competitiveness agenda. A core MPA element is the ambition to enhance road infrastructure management by increasing private sector participation, which can lead to more efficient use of public expenditures and expand the use of private financing. This shift will help alleviate the strain on public finances that go to road infrastructure, while consistently improving road network conditions. Implementing the MPA at scale to improve management of the country’s road assets will directly impact the CPF’s main areas of focus: ( i) greater productivity (high-level objective 1, or HLO1) and employment; (ii) greater inclusion of the poor and underserved populations; and (iii) greener economy with reduced vulnerability to climate shocks (HLO2 and HLO3). The MPA is also aligned to the Green, Resilient and Inclusive Development (GRID) global strategy. The MPA will contribute to both, the CPF and GRID objectives, by reducing logistics costs, traffic crashes, long-term road management costs, and CO2 emissions from transport while increasing infrastructure resilience—a key element of the climate adaptation agenda. 15. The MPA Program and Phase 3 are aligned with WBG’s Evolution Roadmap36 – a bigger and better Bank working at scale, seeking replicability, and presenting an ambitious program tackling four global challenges. Brazil’s Pro- Roads Program links to Climate Change Adaptation and Mitigation and Food and Nutrition Security. The MPA will focus on promoting sustainable road asset management, building climate-resilient infrastructure, and reducing GHG emissions. The MPA will support agricultural productivity, enabling a more competitive economy by reducing farm- to-fork logistics costs, and, in turn, food losses and costs. 16. The MPA will significantly contribute to the Bank's corporate and sector agendas by promoting PCM and private capital enabling (PCE) actions, strengthening the resilience of road transport to climate change, and enhancing road safety. The MPA will enable the transfer of road infrastructure management to the private sector through long-term PBCs. This approach is expected to lead to more efficient road asset management, yielding better road conditions at a lower cost—demonstrated by a 20 percent cost reduction in the first generation of CREMA over five years, with updated assessments indicating up to 40 to 50 percent savings over 10 years.37 Proactive road network management will reduce intervention frequency and costs, easing public financial pressures. When contracted as PPPs, CREMAs will mobilize private capital, further reducing public financial demands at the time of road asset recovery. The Federal Ministry of Transport and the Special Secretariat of International Partnerships (SEPPI) has committed to leading the CREMA-PPP development, with IFC well-positioned to support standardized PPP transactions. Improved road conditions will lower vehicle operating and logistics costs, supporting national competitiveness. Proactive asset management will also enhance resilience to climate events and road safety, with targeted contract improvements bolstering these goals. 17. Both positive and negative environmental impacts are expected from routine maintenance, road recovery works (Component 1 – CREMA works), and mobility interventions (Component 3). The anticipated negative impacts include: (i) noise and air quality issues due to increased vehicle traffic and heavy machinery; (ii) generation of solid and hazardous waste from construction activities; (iii) soil erosion and compaction from soil removal and heavy machinery movement; (iv) soil and water pollution from inadequate management of hazardous products and waste; and (v) construction site and traffic risks to workers and communities. Additionally, the expansion of rights-of-way may be necessary to enhance road safety, climate resilience, or mobility adaptations. This could potentially lead to vegetation removal and habitat loss. Other potential negative impacts include the need for deposits, borrow areas, and asphalt plants. However, the Project prioritizes using commercial quarries and plants, which are regulated by stringent national environmental standards. On the positive side, periodic maintenance and rehabilitation activities can improve drainage and erosion control and reduce existing environmental liabilities and degraded areas. In summary, the potential adverse environmental impacts are site-specific, temporary, reversible, and can be mitigated by adopting SIE 2023 Service Instruction (IS-05) and 2015 DEINFRA Manual requirements. As matter a fact, SIE has extensive experience in carrying out similar work, and well-established protocols outlined in Page 14 The World Bank Brazil Proactive, Safe, and Resilient Road Asset Management Program - State of Santa Catarina Project - Phase 3 (P504253) both instruments. The roads included in the Project already have an Environmental Operating License (Licença Ambiental de Operação - LAO) issued by the Instituto do Meio Ambiente – IMA (a competent state licensing entity), with ongoing environmental programs. A proper licensing process will be followed to allow the necessary removal of fragments of the Mata Atlântica biome for any potential expansion of the rights-of-way. Components 2 and 4 will have minimal direct environmental impacts, as they primarily involve TA and administrative activities. 30. The social risk rating is moderate. The risks and impacts on human populations brought by works that will be supported by Component 1—recovery and maintenance of paved highways, adjustments to highway capacity, improvement of access and road safety devices—are predictable, temporary, and reversible. They tend to be of small magnitude and site-specific. They refer to the health and safety of workers and neighboring communities, road safety conditions, disturbances to neighborhoods during the construction phase (dust, noise, obstacles to the movement of people and vehicles, temporary interruption of services and accesses) and the unexpected and unlikely need of removing irregular invasions and occupations in the right-of-way. Routine and widely known operational and road safety measures, worker’s Occupational Health and Safety, signage of working sites and communication with neighboring communities will be sufficient to prevent and avoid accidents and incidents. The Borrower has extensive experience in carrying out similar work, and well-established protocols to ensure the safety and health of workers and neighboring communities, as well as to deal with irregular invasions and occupations in the rights-of-way. The roads included in MPAs for proactive and preventative maintenance do not cut through or intersect indigenous lands and quilombola territories, but there are five stretches of these roads that are less than 10 km from indigenous and quilombola communities. The downstream effects of the TA activities provided for in Components 2 and 3 are expected to be negligible or largely positive for society. 31. The MPA will build on SSC ongoing measures in place to support women’s employment in tr ansport and construction sectors. SSC Law no. 18,300, of December 21, 202145 mandates that public contracts for outsourced services must reserve at least a 5 percent quota for women facing economic vulnerability due to domestic violence, applicable to contracts with 30 or more workers. Federal Law No. 14.133/2021 allows the bidding documents to include a stipulation mandating contractors provide training to women. Due to these laws, the Project can mandate employment of women in the contracted companies. The Project, in partnership with the Secretariat of Welfare Assistance, Women and Family (Secretaria de Assistência Social, Mulher e Familia – SAS) of Santa Catarina, aims to create well-paid job opportunities for women engineers. As part of the project, the contracted construction companies will provide on the job training and in key engineering roles, with participants receiving formal certification upon completion. Additionally, the project will cover the registration fees for these women to join the Conselho Regional de Engenharia e Agronomia (CREA), which is a requirement for women to participate in the job training and to practice engineering related jobs in the future. The inclusion of women in these roles will not only address the existing gender imbalance but also contribute to breaking the barriers that have traditionally excluded women from science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) fields. The Project will emphasize on-the-job training in engineering disciplines relevant for the construction sector (e.g. supervision, topography, auxiliary in paving lab, occupational health and safety, among others to be mapped with the support of the contractors). This will enable to support women’s school to work transition and will contribute to create a pipeline of women professional engineers which will be role models for upcoming generations. This approach is aimed at empowering women with the skills and certifications necessary to build long-lasting careers in the engineering and construction sectors, thus fostering gender equity and increasing diversity in these high-demand fields. 45 Santa Catarina State Law no. 18,300, of December 21, 2021 (which mandates a 5 percent quota for women survivors of gender-based violence (GBV) in teams of more than 30 workers) vs. National Decree no. 11,430, of March 8, 2023 (requiring an 8 percent quota for women, including survivors of domestic violence, in teams of more than 25 workers, and includes transgender women, giving priority to black and mixed-race women, being the national law broader in scope).
