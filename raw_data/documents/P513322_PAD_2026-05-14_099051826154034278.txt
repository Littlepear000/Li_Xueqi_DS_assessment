STRATEGIC CONTEXT 1. Since February 24, 2022, Russia's invasion of Ukraine has had a profound impact on the population, resulting in long-lasting social effects that primarily affect vulnerable households. Over four years of hostilities have caused significant civilian casualties, widespread destruction of critical infrastructure, and large-scale displacement of people. As of October 2025, approximately 5.8 million Ukrainian refugees, representing 14 percent of the population, were recorded worldwide. The number of Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) peaked at 6.5 million in March 2022 and stabilized at about 9 percent of the population by September 2025. The number of persons with disabilities has also increased by over 10 percent, reaching 3 million by early 2024. The ongoing hostilities, mass displacement, rising poverty, and deteriorating economic conditions have created compounded pressures on Ukraine’s social protection system, resulting in an unprecedented demand for support. The Government of Ukraine (GoU) prioritizes continued payments of social benefits, developing social services1 and disability support, even though the budget is under strain. According to the Fifth Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment (RDNA5), Ukraine faces a financing gap of over US$15 billion for recovery and reconstruction priorities in 2026. The estimated needs in the social protection and livelihoods sector amount to US$42.7 billion over 10 years. The situation has had a severe and asymmetric impact on the labor market. On the supply side, the civilian labor force in government-controlled territory shrank by an estimated 22 percent relative to 2021, driven by refugee outflows of approximately 2.8 million workers, mobilization for military service, and reduced participation. On the demand side, private-sector labor demand is estimated to have fallen by 11–17 percent between 2022 and 2023, with online vacancies decreasing by more than half in the first year.. Despite this dual contraction, unemployment declined from a peak of over 20 percent in 2022 to approximately 13 percent by 2024, reflecting in part the scale of supply-side contraction rather than demand recovery, and masking significant mismatches affecting persons with disabilities, women, and displaced populations. This context underscores the importance of the activation agenda embedded in the cash-plus-services approach under the proposed operation, and the need to ensure social assistance design does not inadvertently reduce incentives to work. A. Project Strategic Context 2. The Social Protection Project for Inclusion, Resilience, Innovation, and Transformation (SPIRIT) is a standalone Investment Project Financing (IPF) operation with Performance-Based Conditions (PBCs) that aims to enhance the integration, access, and inclusiveness in Ukraine's social protection system. The proposed SPIRIT project will be financed by an IBRD loan of US$500 million supported by a bilateral guarantee from the Government of the United Kingdom (UK), an IBRD loan of US$360 million backed by the credit enhancement from the Advancing Needed Credit Enhancement for Ukraine (ADVANCE Ukraine) Trust Fund supported by the Government of Japan,2 and expected grant funding of US$20 million from the Ukraine Relief, Recovery, Reconstruction, and Reform Trust Fund (URTF).3 3. Ukraine’s large social protection system was being gradually modernized before February 2022 but now faces simultaneous crises that challenge its structural capacity. Poverty has risen sharply; a large share of the population needs assistance, and significant displacement along with rising disability have placed unprecedented demands on a system designed for a different context. The prospective large inflow of veterans into the system is likely to exacerbate these strains. These challenges are particularly acute in three interconnected domains—social assistance, social services, and disability support—where insufficient integration, accessibility gaps, and limited inclusiveness constrain the system's ability to respond. Because these deficiencies directly affect the living conditions and opportunities of millions of Ukrainians, the reforms supported by SPIRIT can deliver a tangible impact. The project addresses four key developmental challenges: (i) reducing social assistance fragmentation and introducing effective integration with social services to strengthen poverty reduction; (ii) enhancing the capacity of the social services system at both central and local levels; (iii) facilitating a shift toward a human rights-based disability support model aligned with international standards; and (iv) introducing shock-responsive features to the safety net that enable rapid enrollment of crisis-affected populations and better leverage donor humanitarian financing that government systems currently lack the capacity to absorb. 4. Ukraine’s fiscal environment leaves no space for inefficiency, making the rationalization of social protection spending both a fiscal imperative and a development priority. Tax revenues stand at approximately 33 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), while defense expenditures consume close to 30 percent of GDP (roughly US$60 billion annually), leaving a fiscal deficit of approximately 25 percent of GDP (around US$40 billion) that is covered by external donor financing in the form of grants and concessional loans. The 2026 budget is conditional on securing additional donor commitments, with external financing needs exceeding those of 2025, and the International Monetary Fund program serving as the anchor for donor engagement through March 2027. Within this severely constrained fiscal space, the budget is dominated by defense and security, social protection, and interest payments. Combined education, health, and social protection spending is projected to increase from US$16.6 billion in 2025 to US$18.9 billion in 2026, reflecting the GoU's continued prioritization of human development under challenging conditions. State budget social assistance expenditures stand at around 2 percent of GDP in 2026, and with no fiscal space for additional spending, the focus must shift from expansion to efficiency. The SPIRIT project directly addresses this imperative by reducing allocative and administrative inefficiency in the social protection system—consolidating fragmented benefit programs and streamlining delivery mechanisms to ensure that available resources reach those most in need. The project-supported reforms are expected to generate positive economic returns at both the household level, through better-targeted and more effective transfers, and at the macroeconomic level, through a more productive and better-supported labor force. 5. The GoU has requested an operation that supports sustained recovery and lays the institutional foundations for Ukraine's social contract. The proposed SPIRIT project advances a cohesive and transformative social protection agenda across five interconnected priorities: (i) introduction of Basic Social Assistance (BSA) with integrated case management linking cash transfers to social and employment services; (ii) the launch of a national purchasing agency for social services operating on a "money follows the client" model; (iii) enhanced support for families with children; (iv) modernization of disability assessment and improvement of disability support aligned with international standards; and (v) improved administrative efficiency through the creation of a unified system for processing and distributing social benefits. These reforms directly advance Ukraine's European Union (EU) accession agenda by fulfilling critical screening requirements in social policy and disability rights under the Acquis Communautaire. The operation is a natural progression of the World Bank's technical assistance (TA) to the GoU for the design and implementation of these reforms, Official Use Only Page 2 The World Bank Social Protection Project for Inclusion, Resilience, Innovation, and Transformation (SPIRIT) (P513322) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT supported financially by the UK's Foreign, Commonwealth, and Development Office (FCDO),4 and builds on the Investing in Social Protection for Inclusion, Resilience, and Efficiency (INSPIRE) Project (P181081) while leveraging PEACE5 financing. The project closely aligns with Ukraine’s National Recovery Plan and the Government’s Priority Action Plan, supporting critical social protection reforms. Through the proposed operation, the GoU will continue providing social assistance while enhancing institutional capacity at both central and local levels, thereby building resilience to future shocks. The project will provide immediate support to more than 1 million people by financing 18 types of social assistance programs for low-income households, vulnerable families with children, persons with disabilities, older persons, and caregivers. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 6. Ukraine’s social protection system has shown remarkable resilience and reform momentum—demonstrating genuine political ownership. Social protection expenditures comprise the second-largest item in the state budget for 2025-2026, accounting for approximately 15 percent of GDP. Social assistance accounts for around 2 percent, with pensions making up the remainder. The system continues to deliver uninterrupted payments to pensioners, IDPs, lowincome households, and other vulnerable groups, reaching 60-80 percent of the population with at least one social protection measure. The Pension Fund of Ukraine (PFU) administers over 40 types of benefits, including the largest programs such as IDP assistance and Housing and Utility Subsidies (HUS), and is progressively consolidating into a unified payment agency. The GoU accelerated its reform agenda and continued to invest in digital systems, while advancing the social assistance and social services reforms and developing a national roadmap for disability assessment reform. Despite system-wide challenges, the GoU introduced pilots to finance and deliver priority social services. As a first step toward a more diversified model—one that complements local capacities with centrally funded services—the GoU launched pilots to finance services for vulnerable groups from the state budget. The pilots supported by the INSPIRE project aim to test new approaches for purchasing and delivering social services. 7. Despite these strengths, social assistance and social services remain fragmented, resulting in a high exclusion rate for vulnerable households and a lack of effective coordination between these types of support to help people graduate out of poverty. Cash transfers substantially exceed expenditures on social services, and the centralized social assistance system is not sufficiently coordinated with decentralized social services. The social services system suffers from low coverage, a financing model biased toward institutional care, and a governance gap between centrally led reforms and local implementation capacity. For example, decentralization reform transferred financing and delivery responsibilities to communities without commensurate financing or a regulatory framework for procuring services from diverse providers. Since February 2022, local capacities and budgets have been significantly strained due to the ongoing hostilities. Current expenditures cover only 17 percent of the estimated annual need of UAH 153 billion, leaving a third of vulnerable households with unmet social service needs. Resources flow disproportionately to maintaining residential facilities rather than developing community-based alternatives. Local authorities need a functional marketplace and a robust regulatory framework enabling purchases from diverse providers, including non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and the private sector. Social assistance payment levels are pegged to the outdated Subsistence Minimum (SM).6 This indicator, originally developed to measure poverty levels, fails to capture citizens’ needs and is used to calculate over 180 types of payments, many of which are unrelated to poverty. Furthermore, support mechanisms for families in crisis need to be improved to address shocks effectively. Persons with disabilities lack an integrated package of support that addresses functional, social, and economic needs, including income support, social services, rehabilitation, and employment. The disability support system is fragmented across several ministries, numerous budget programs, and produces among the lowest employment rates for persons with disabilities in Europe. The Ministry of Health (MoH) and the Ministry of Social Policy, Family and Unity (MoSPFU), the Ministry of Economy, and the Ministry of Veterans Affairs each administer disability-related policies without a unified framework linking assessment, benefits, rehabilitation, assistive technologies, and employment support. Poor coordination leads to duplication, overlapping mandates, and inconsistent coverage. The current model certifies medical conditions and assigns disability groups, but should be enhanced to effectively assess functional capacity, identify barriers to participation, and determine necessary rehabilitation and services for inclusion. Only 18 percent of persons with disabilities are employed—a 32 percentage-point gap compared to the EU average. Weak interinstitutional coordination and fragmented delivery of benefits and services across programs further constrain access for persons with disabilities, limiting the system’s ability to address individual needs effectively. 9. The SPIRIT project addresses social assistance fragmentation by supporting the full-scale implementation of the BSA program, which consolidates benefits into a single poverty-reduction program with enhanced inclusion, integrated case management, and shock-responsive features. BSA replaces the SM with a more appropriate "basic value" for benefit determination, raises the income eligibility threshold to improve adequacy, and expands means-testing to previously categorical programs. The project will support piloting an integrated model that links centralized social assistance with decentralized service delivery through case management, beneficiary-profiled support packages, and inter-agency coordination. Under the new model, social workers will proactively identify vulnerable families, conduct comprehensive needs assessments, make cross-agency referrals, and monitor progress—providing direct help and meaningful pathways out of welfare dependency. As children make up the majority of BSA beneficiaries, the project also supports a new and expanded benefits package for parents and children from pregnancy through school entry. A crisisresponse package for families facing displacement, evacuation, or natural disasters will enable rapid enrollment of populations affected by shocks. These project-supported activities will be complemented by interventions implemented by donors, channeling humanitarian financing through government delivery systems and expanding access horizontally to previously excluded groups while increasing payment adequacy vertically through integrated donor resources. 10. The project tackles the low coverage and institutional bias of social services by introducing a financing model that channels state budget resources to nationally prioritized services through a competitive procurement mechanism. The Fund for Social Protection of Persons with Disabilities (FSPPD)7 is expected to be transformed into a national purchasing agency, establishing a transparent framework that integrates NGOs and private providers into service delivery and enables annual prioritization of services based on evolving needs and available resources. Community-based microprojects financed by SPIRIT will establish locally governed providers and social infrastructure delivering long-term care, supported living, and home-based services—shifting the model from institutional placement to client-centered community support and enabling vulnerable populations to live independently. This approach directly addresses the governance gap between central reform ambition and local implementation capacity by aligning functions, financing, and accountability across levels of government. The national purchasing agency will play a pivotal role in developing the services market. Project Development Objective 12. Project Development Objective (PDO). The PDO is to enhance the integration, access, and inclusiveness of Ukraine's social protection system. B. Theory of Change and PDO Indicators 13. SPIRIT’s theory of change establishes the tight link between the project scope, the activities and associated expenditures, and the project objectives. To achieve the PDO, the project supports a comprehensive package of GoU's reforms. The activities funded by SPIRIT will target key areas of the social protection system, including social assistance, social services, and disability support. The key PDO outcomes will include (i) enhanced integration of and access to social assistance; (ii) improved access of vulnerable groups to social services; and (iii) enhanced inclusiveness of the social protection system. Additional cross-cutting outcome is improved institutional resilience and capacity of the social protection system. The project reinforces the institutional foundations of the social protection system by enhancing legal and policy frameworks that support and sustain programs, shock-response efforts, and coordination mechanisms across various agencies and levels of government. It also support investments in information systems and the training of frontline staff, resulting in a more integrated, accessible, and inclusive social protection system for all beneficiaries. 14. The critical assumptions underlaying the theory of change are as follows: (i) the government remains highly committed to the social assistance, social services, and disability inclusion reforms supported by the project; (ii) the implementation of an integrated model that links beneficiaries of the BSA program to social services will lead to measurable improvements in access and coverage; (iii) adequate human resources, capacity-building efforts, and digital solutions are in place to transition from the pilot phase to full-scale implementation of the ICF-aligned disability determination and needs assessment system; and (iv) adequate fiscal resources, including continued donor support, are available to sustain social protection expenditures and implement reform. D. Project Components 17. SPIRIT is a results-focused operation, with a strong emphasis on measurable impact. It includes both regular investment financing and a results-based component to encourage specific policy actions and outcomes. The use of PBCs is intended to strengthen a project’s results orientation. SPIRIT’s PBCs focus on critical steps in the results chain and are fully embedded in the results framework. There is a strong connection between the traditional investment part of the project and the results-based component. The proposed project has two components (see Table 1). The US$798.75 million in loan funds under Component 1 will be disbursed against eligible expenditures for 18 selected social assistance benefit programs upon the achievement of 10 PBCs. While larger results-based loan financing provides resources to fund social assistance, the US$20 million grant under Component 2 ensures that the capacity for administrative efficiency, digital transformation, and improved service delivery remains robust and effective long after the project ends. This component is aimed at strengthening the institutional capacity of the GoU’s agencies - including the MoSPFU, the PFU, and the FSPPD - and local governments to implement reforms supported by this project. PBC 1 supports a transformative social assistance reform that consolidates Ukraine's safety net, expands coverage, and improves the targeting of minimum income support to respond flexibly to socioeconomic crises. Specifically, PBC 1 supports the full-scale rollout of the BSA program currently being piloted with support from the Bankfinanced INSPIRE project. BSA consolidates five existing benefits (the GMI, social assistance for single mothers, support for children from large families, temporary alimony assistance, and social assistance for older persons and persons with disabilities not qualifying for pensions) into a single anti-poverty program. To enhance targeting, the program will utilize means and asset testing, combined with the use of monthly household income in benefit calculation. The BSA expands coverage by raising the income eligibility threshold, thereby reaching a greater share of vulnerable households to help them meet their basic needs. It improves targeting by subjecting previously categorical benefits to means and asset testing, ensuring that limited resources are directed to those most in need, including large families, single mothers, and older persons. It also modernizes benefit calculation by replacing the outdated SM with a new "basic value" indicator, allowing flexible adjustments to the benefit level without triggering system-wide recalculations across the over 180 payments and fees currently linked to the SM. The program will also enable providing temporary support for families facing crises. Administration of the BSA, including client intake and decisions on benefit provision, will be consolidated under the PFU as part of the broader shift toward a unified payment agency (see PBC 6), reducing the administrative burden on both the system and its beneficiaries. 22. PBC 2 supports a significant shift toward an integrated model of benefits and social services delivery through a case management approach. The MoSPFU and the Bank team are developing an integrated case management model that links BSA program beneficiaries to essential social services, drawing on best practices from similar models implemented with World Bank support in other countries.10 The current centralized social assistance system lacks effective integration with decentralized social services and offers no meaningful pathways out of welfare dependency. To address this gap, vulnerable families will undergo a needs assessment to receive a tailored package of support that includes benefits and services tailored to six profiles of vulnerability. These interventions will be detailed in social agreements11 that outline the conditions and steps beneficiaries should follow. This approach aims to enhance poverty reduction efforts by assisting vulnerable households receiving BSA in achieving greater selfsufficiency and decreasing their reliance on benefits. Specifically, the integrated model will use profiling to identify the vulnerabilities and needs of low-income households, persons with disabilities, older persons, and vulnerable families with children, including large families and single mothers, to provide tailored support packages accordingly. Beyond income support, these packages will encompass social services, employment assistance, childcare, and other relevant interventions based on each family's specific situation. 23. The Crisis Response package of interventions within the integrated model aims to prevent families facing sudden vulnerabilities from falling into poverty by providing rapid, temporarily unconditional support. Under the current BSA, able-to-work applicants must demonstrate recent employment or registration with the Public Employment Service — requirements that can exclude households whose income has been disrupted by displacement, man-made or natural disasters, illness, prolonged unemployment, or unpaid caregiving. The expanded BSA pilot will address these inclusion gaps by: (i) defining a "family in crisis" as one facing sudden or prolonged events that significantly reduce income and limit the ability to meet basic needs; (ii) waiving prior income requirements for such families; (iii) providing shortterm, unconditional BSA for three months with a possibility of one-time extension, after which general eligibility rules apply; (iv) requiring a Crisis Support Agreement committing able-to-work recipients to job search or activation measures; and (v) streamlining application processing and linking applicants to services. Backed by SPIRIT, the reform strengthens Ukraine's capacity to rapidly scale social assistance in response to crises and shocks, including those related to climate change. 24. PBCs 3 and 4 support structural reforms to Ukraine's social services system. This will be achieved by introducing a diversified financing model to address persistent coverage gaps arising from the decentralization of financing to local budgets and fiscal constraints, which have resulted in uneven service availability across communities. PBC 3 establishes a transparent, annually updated mechanism to prioritize and centrally finance the cost-effective priority services implemented through the FSPPD. The diversified financing model, which combines resources from central and local budgets with an increased use of state funds to purchase services from both public and private providers, will allow for the reallocation of resources as needs change. This adaptation will be crucial in addressing evolving displacement and poverty rates, as well as other shocks, including those related to climate events. PBC 4 supports transforming the FSPPD into a national purchasing agency for social services, consolidating ongoing pilots into a scalable system that standardizes eligibility, service packages, tariffs, and quality assurance, thereby improving equity, accountability, and value for money nationwide. 25. PBC 5 enhances the impact of the BSA reform (PBC 1) by strengthening support for families with children, who account for about 60 percent of BSA beneficiaries. Families with children in Ukraine face significantly higher risks of poverty, exacerbated by the ongoing situation. While the BSA provides essential income support, children and their parents often face additional vulnerabilities and needs unrelated to income. PBC 5 supports a lifecycle-based approach to child benefit provision, providing consistent, age-appropriate cash transfers across six programs spanning pregnancy, birth, infancy, early childhood, and the transition into school. Key innovations, such as (i) monthly childcare support for infants under one year old, (ii) the e-Nursery (“eYasla”) program for working or studying parents of young children, and (iii) a Schoolchild’s Package for first graders, provide comprehensive support for families with children, facilitate parents' return to the labor market, reduce unpaid caregiving burdens, and mitigate long-term career penalties, particularly for women. SPIRIT finances these three programs. 26. PBC 6 supports the establishment of a unified and effective administrative system by transferring the Official Use Only Page 10 The World Bank Social Protection Project for Inclusion, Resilience, Innovation, and Transformation (SPIRIT) (P513322) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT responsibility for awarding and distributing 39 benefits to the PFU. Building on recent reforms to modernize social protection administration - including the Bank-supported transfer of functions related to HUS provision under the Social Safety Nets Modernization Project (SSNMP) (P128344) - the system consolidates client intake, eligibility determination, and benefit payments within the PFU. This ensures nationwide access to benefits regardless of place of residence or registration, an essential feature for the millions of IDPs across the country, while strengthening data protection. Leveraging the PFU’s extensive network of over 500 offices and centralized, highly digitalized operations, the reform reduces administrative burdens on beneficiaries, addresses longstanding inefficiencies associated with fragmented local administration, and improves transparency, consistency, and oversight of benefit payments. At the same time, it enables a functional reorientation of local social welfare staff toward social services provision, case management, and social work, thereby strengthening the overall delivery system and ensuring timely and reliable payments across all SPIRIT-financed benefit programs. 27. PBC 7 supports regulatory changes to establish centrally funded social services for children with disabilities. The pilot, financed from the state budget and implemented by the FSPPD, will test service delivery procedures and develop a financing mechanism aimed at: (i) supporting children who cannot attend mainstream secondary education full-time; (ii) providing services to integrate children with disabilities into mainstream educational institutions; (iii) enabling parents to re-enter the workforce; and (iv) preventing institutionalization through support to family-based care. Services include habilitation, development of social and self-care skills, community-based daycare, and psychological support for children and their parents. 28. PBCs 8, 9, and 10 support a shift from a medical to a biopsychosocial model of disability, introducing a functioning assessment linked to person-centered support and inclusive employment. Building on initial reforms to the medical-social expertise system in Ukraine, PBCs 8 and 9 will support the phased rollout of a disability determination mechanism aligned with the ICF. Implementation will proceed through a pilot in social field, including improved needs assessment and referral mechanisms, clarifying institutional mandates, embedding disability determination, and strengthening cross-institutional data exchange. PBC 9 will be achieved once the law establishing the new disability assessment system is adopted, and the number of individuals assessed reaches 10,000. By the closing date, this number— monitored by the PDI 5 —is expected to reach 50,000 people. The reform aims to expand access to rehabilitation, assistive technologies, and social services. PBC 10 establishes a regulatory framework for individualized employment support, including reasonable accommodation and employment-related social services, to improve employability, earnings, and labor market participation of persons with disabilities. The lessons learned from recent and ongoing operations in Ukraine, along with global experience, have been incorporated into the SPIRIT project design as follows: (a) Operate effectively in environments, focusing on maintaining service continuity while strengthening institutional resilience: SPIRIT aligns with the World Bank Group Strategy for Fragility, Conflict, and Violence (FCV) (2020-2025) and is consistent with the refreshed Strategy for FCV. The project-financed activities aim to preserve Ukraine's development gains and enhance its capacity for future recovery. SPIRIT’s results-based financing, aligned with the GoU’s priorities, drives reforms, even in challenging times. (b) Ground reforms in rigorous analytics: The SPIRIT IPF builds directly on SPIRIT PASA’s country‑specific evidence (e.g., social expenditure reviews, disability assessment, money‑follows‑beneficiary model), demonstrating that investments anchored in robust diagnostics and partner consultation (UNICEF, FCDO) achieve stronger, credible results. (c) Strengthen the humanitarian–development nexus through strategic partnerships: A defining lesson was the partnership model established with UNHCR and UNICEF to align humanitarian assistance with government systems rather than operating parallel delivery mechanisms. This will ensure humanitarian financing flows through strengthened national safety nets, reducing fragmentation while enhancing sustainability. (d) Pilot reforms before nationwide scale‑up. Structured pilots implemented alongside existing systems can mitigate risks and build consensus. The disability assessment pilot will test systems’ performance, assess fiscal impacts, and fine‑tune eligibility criteria, strengthening institutional capacity ahead of national rollout. (e) The ICF represents a global consensus on the standard for describing and measuring health and disability: ICF represents a profound paradigm shift from a purely medical model to a biopsychosocial approach that is both scientifically sound and aligned with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Multiple countries across the EU and beyond have already reformed their disability assessment systems in alignment with ICF principles, citing it as the basis for modernizing disability assessments. III. PROJECT IMPLEMENTATION A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 37. The MoSPFU, the central government agency responsible for developing and implementing social protection policies in Ukraine, will be the implementing agency for the SPIRIT project. The Ministry has significant experience and strong institutional capacity, having prepared and executed six investment lending operations financed by the World Bank. Selecting an agency with a proven track record is a strategic choice for implementing an operation focused on providing immediate social relief by financing 18 social benefits, as well as supporting recovery through a comprehensive reform package. To ensure timely and effective implementation, SPIRIT will, whenever possible, use existing institutional arrangements, platforms, and mechanisms developed or used by the SSNMP and INSPIRE. These mechanisms are trusted by the beneficiaries, accepted by the Bank, and capable of supporting the activities funded by the project. The SPIRIT project adopts preparation and implementation arrangements similar to those used in the ongoing INSPIRE project and the recently completed SSNMP. The Deputy Minister of Social Policy, Family, and Unity will serve as the Project Coordinator, overseeing the overall implementation. The coordinator will lead a PMG to guide the process and make timely decisions. TA and expertise required to implement project activities will be provided by external consultants hired by the MoSPFU, including specialists in procurement, FM, M&E, and E&S safeguards. Additionally, a group of consultants will be established within the FSPPD. This Fund, which is subordinate to the MoSPFU, will coordinate the implementation of community-based microprojects under the overall management of the Project Coordinator. Regional coordinators will be included in the PMG to facilitate the implementation of microprojects at the community level, the establishment of the local Grievance Mechanism (GM) channels, and compliance with the requirements of the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF). To organize procurement effectively and transparently, the Ministry will reestablish a Competitive Bidding Committee (CBC). The CBC will conduct procurement for the project on a collegial basis, ensuring there are no conflicts of interest and that members remain impartial. The Project Coordinator will serve as Chair of the CBC. 39. The FSPPD will coordinate the implementation of community-based microprojects. Local governments, through their social welfare units, will manage microprojects—covering proposal development, planning and budgeting, implementation, and reporting—while the PMG will centrally procure inputs and pay contractors to minimize fiduciary risks. The MoSPFU and FSPPD will convene a Selection Committee including independent experts to evaluate microproject proposals against criteria including: (i) soundness of design and budget; (ii) alignment with service delivery plans; (iii) feasibility of procurement and implementation within the project cycle; (iv) inclusivity and accessibility for vulnerable groups; and (v) capacity to operationalize services, including staffing, recurrent financing. The process will include a public call for proposals, pre‑submission clinics and mentoring, eligibility screening, full technical review, and verification of sustainability commitments. The project’s GM will enable stakeholders to raise concerns at every stage of the selection process. 40. During implementation, the Bank team will provide hands‑on support to help the project achieve its objectives. The team will proactively add value by facilitating sector dialogue, providing capacity-building and policy advice, connecting government counterparts to specialized expertise, and enabling knowledge sharing. It will closely monitor FM, disbursement, and procurement to support implementation of the Procurement Plan and ensure funds are used for their intended purposes. The team will also provide E&S support and oversight, advising on good practices to strengthen the MoSPFU’s E&S management capacity, monitoring safeguards arrangements, and verifying compliance with the ESF and applicable requirements. In addition, the Bank will assess the quality of project reporting and track progress against the results framework, procurement plans, and disbursement schedules, while continuously evaluating overall implementation performance and the adequacy of institutional arrangements. B. Results Monitoring, Evaluation, and Verification Arrangements 41. M&E is central to SPIRIT’s results-based design. The MoSPFU has an established M&E system and reporting arrangements from the SSNMP and INSPIRE projects that will be leveraged to maintain a strong focus on results during SPIRIT implementation. SPIRIT will also put in place credible mechanisms to verify the achievement of PBCs. As PBC performance can be evidenced through budget execution reports and applicable laws and regulations - some of which were developed with the World Bank’s TA - the implementing agency will conduct verification. The World Bank team will then validate all results, consistent with the procedures used under SSNMP and INSPIRE, and as outlined in SPIRIT’s POM. Project monitoring will draw on the MoSPFU’s MIS and FM arrangements for the social safety net payments, which the Official Use Only Page 15 The World Bank Social Protection Project for Inclusion, Resilience, Innovation, and Transformation (SPIRIT) (P513322) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT World Bank assessed as robust and reliable during SSNMP and INSPIRE implementation. Data from the PFU MIS will also be used to track the provision of benefits included in the EEP. Under INSPIRE and SSNMP, the Bank reviewed the architecture of most social safety net programs, including multiple performance audits assessing verification mechanisms for social assistance programs (e.g., application of eligibility criteria) as well as program administration, oversight, and controls. These reviews found adequate transparency and reliable financial and budget execution reporting. Reliable M&E arrangements for community-based microprojects will be critical to their success. The implementing agency will establish these arrangements to assess performance and outcomes, including defining specific indicators, collecting data from participating communities, and regularly reviewing progress. Channel 3: Labor Market Returns to Activation and Inclusion. Integrating income support with employment and social services is expected to generate positive economic impacts at both the household and macroeconomic levels. At the household level, employment transitions the income base of beneficiary households from passive transfers to earned income, reducing long-term benefit dependency and improving well-being. At the macro level, even modest gains in employment rates yield meaningful contributions to output and fiscal revenues — a comparable program in Italy saw at least 19 percent of beneficiaries enter new employment each year. For persons with disabilities, individualized employment support and reasonable accommodation measures are projected to generate additional labor-market returns through two pathways.
