Conditions v The World Bank Bhutan Human Capital Recovery and Resilience Program for Results (P174399) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. The Bhutanese economy exhibited consistent growth and gains in poverty reduction prior to the COVID-19 pandemic. Since the early 1980s, the annual growth of gross domestic product (GDP) averaged 7.5 percent, with 5.5 percent of GDP in 2019, making Bhutan one of the fastest-growing economies globally. From 2007 through 2017, Bhutan reduced poverty by two-thirds, from 36 percent to 12 percent, based on the $3.20/day poverty line, albeit the pace has slowed down in recent years. Much of the growth has been driven by public sector-led hydropower development projects and the export of electricity to neighboring India. 2. However, economic dependence on construction-related hydropower projects and prevalence of subsistence employment in agriculture make Bhutan’s economy highly vulnerable to macroeconomic volatility and climate shocks. Given nascent non-farm private sector in Bhutan, agriculture (including subsistence) accounts for close to 50 percent of employment in Bhutan.1 At the same time, most of Bhutan’s productive infrastructure, fertile agricultural land, and over 70 percent of the settlements are located along the main drainage basins, which puts them at high risk of flooding. Large parts of the population lack access to coping mechanisms needed for fast recovery. (See Annex 8 Climate Co-Benefits.) 3. Bhutan’s economic contraction from the pandemic highlights the need for diversifying the economy and nurturing skilled Bhutanese workers. The unemployment rate has risen almost twofold to 5 percent in 2020 from 2.7 percent in 2019. Activity in the service sector declined as tourist arrivals dried up, leading to significant loss of jobs and livelihoods.2 Industrial activities were severely disrupted due to shortages in critical inputs (including migrant workers) and depressed external demand, especially from India. On the demand side, consumption and investment declined due to domestic containment measures and disruptions in public sector infrastructure projects. High food inflation has also likely eroded the real incomes of many rural poor, which would lead to a 1.8 percentage point increase in poverty by the end of 2021. 4. The Royal Government of Bhutan (RGoB) has made commendable efforts to control the COVID-19 pandemic and protect its population. With the massive vaccination drive3, RGoB has employed several emergency response programs and introduced adjustments to implementing the 12th Five Year Plan (FYP) 2018-2023. The RGoB promptly secured food stocks and provided direct cash transfers to the affected population (Druk Gyalpo’s Relief Kidu), reskilling and reemployment support (the Build Bhutan Program), and support to learning recovery through Education in Emergency COVID-19 Response Plans. The FYP midterm revision coincided with the COVID reality allowed RGoB to reassess its targets and options for achieving its goals in post-COVID-19, expecting to improve the quality of public service delivery, promote economic and environmental sustainability, and increase inclusion to reduce inequalities. 5. As government finances are also impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, the achievement of 12th FYP goals while supporting recovery and resilience will require investment support. Well-implemented programs are expected to strengthen human capital and resilience of both current and future generations, reduce intergenerational poverty, and increase equality of opportunities, thus allowing RGoB to come out stronger post-pandemic. 1 Bhutan Labor Force Survey 2020. 2 Services sector workers in urban areas, including many that directly or indirectly depend on tourism, have experienced job or earning losses (United Nations Development Program. “Rapid socio-economic assessment of COVID-19 on Bhutan’s Tourism Sector.” March 2020). 3 About 72.6 percent of the population were fully vaccinated as of November 2021. Despite significant progress over the past years on human capital, the World Bank’s Human Capital Index 2020 shows that a child born today in Bhutan will be only 48 percent4 as productive in adulthood as they could have been with a complete education and better health care. Furthermore, the pandemic has disrupted the provision of human capital services and resulted in a decline in household welfare, further reducing the human capital investment of the next generations. The combined effect of COVID-19 on education, health, and household welfare could lead to an increase in poverty, stunting, and food insecurity and affect social development indicators such as educational achievement and productivity, threatening hard gains from previous years. 7. Bhutan also continues to face inadequate and unequal access to quality foundational human capital services. Access to and quality of early childhood care and development (ECCD) services remain insufficient and inequitable. Preschool enrollment remains low at only 28 percent5 and a geographic gap remains with 22 percent of urban children having attained primary, ECCD, or daycare, compared with only 13 percent of rural.6 ECCD quality is a concern, with only one third of existing ECCD facilitators having completed the in-service training program. The Programme for International Student Assessment for Development (PISA-D) Report 2018, found significant gaps in students’ performance in higher order skills that are required for successful employment, including digital literacy, which is also critical for lifelong learning, higher paying jobs and for resilience and disaster preparedness. The RGoB also aims to improve learning systems for combating climate change through dedicated climate resilience programs in education. 8. In addition to the unequal access to quality human capital services, the human capital acquired through education and training is also presently underutilized in the job market. Employment in subsistence agriculture continues to be prevalent. A substantial portion of the employed have no educational attainment (42.4 percent) and remain at unpaid family work (30 percent). Gender disparities in employment prevail, with close to 40 percent of employed females in 2020 being family workers in comparison to only around 20 percent for male workers.7 Fewer women enter the labor market, with 65 percent of women vs. 35 percent men in urban areas and 56 percent vs. 44 percent in rural areas recorded as economically inactive. Moreover, women account for only 30 percent of enrollment in technical and vocational training courses, except for traditional trades, which often result in lower wages.8 This signals the need to enhance access to education and skills training for those groups. 9. Accordingly, the issues of quality, equity, and utilization of human capital were at the forefront of the design of the 12th FYP 2018-2023, resulting in clear commitments to address the challenges focusing on the lifecycle approach. The 12th FYP is structured around 17 National Key Results Areas (NKRAs) that are cross-cutting in nature but have specific line ministries responsible for their overall implementation. Specifically, cross-sectoral NKRAs 7 and 11 on “Improving Quality of Education and Skills” and “Productive and Gainful Employment” highlighted RGoB’s commitment to lifecycle approach starting with ECCD, inclusive education irrespective of remoteness, economic disadvantages, and disabilities, and continued to lifelong skilling and gainful and inclusive employment needed for the full realization of human capital potential. The proposed Program would support Bhutan’s national goals of improving human capital outcomes for economic productivity and growth by supporting the implementation of “Improving Quality of Education and Skills” and “Productive and Gainful Employment” NKRAs under the 12th FYP including associated COVID-19 recovery programs. 11. The proposed Program is in line with World Bank Group Country Partnership Strategy (CPF) FY21–24 (Report No. 154927-BT) discussed by the Board on December 18, 2020 and will support two of the strategic focus areas—human capital and resilience. The project will promote investments in human capital and skills for both future and current generations with attention to ensuring utilization of accumulated human capital through employment. Support to recovery of employment after COVID-19 will also improve ability of households to avoid negative coping strategies (such as withdrawing kids from school) and can lead to increased investment in human capital. Strengthening delivery systems both on the education and social protection sides will help make these services more resilient to impacts of natural and economic shocks. Moreover, the project will also contribute to environmental resilience through support to training associated with green jobs and environmental initiatives and integration and strengthening of environmental aspects in education curriculum. 12. The Program for Results (PforR) instrument marks a fundamental shift in the World Bank’s support to the RGoB’s programs and sector strategies, away from input-based, sector-specific activities to an outcome-oriented and cross-sectoral approach by supporting the 12th FYP. This approach is justified given the alignment of the client and World Bank goals, existing institutional capacity, and opportunity to sustainably strengthen client institutions and delivery mechanisms within an existing and well-defined government program. The approach is not without risks given the client’s limited familiarity with the instrument, but with proper implementation and technical support it is expected to lead to further strengthening of the client’s capacity and institutions. II. PROGRAM DESCRIPTION A. Government Program 13. The proposed Program will contribute to equitable and sustainable human capital accumulation and employment in Bhutan by supporting select pillars under the 12th FYP and associated COVID-19 recovery plans. The 12th FYP underwent a mid-term review in May 2021, which - in line with the focus of this PforR - highlighted the need to adjust implementation to reflect reality post COVID-19 pandemic and introduced new programs and plans supported by the PforR. The 12th FYP has a national focus. However, given challenges in access to quality services in rural and remote areas as well as gender disparities, both NKRAs have an additional focus on impact in rural areas and inclusion. NKRA 7 and 11 are directly related to the Program objectives, by supporting education, skills, and employment results areas. MOE and MOLHR are the lead ministries for NKRA 7 and 11, respectively, and both have a strong cross-sectoral focus with the budget allocated across more than one agency. MOE’s outlay is 4 billion BTN or US$60 million under the 12th FYP, and MOLHRs’ outlay is 2.8 billion BTN or US$42 million. These budgets are complemented by funds from donors like the Government of India, ADB’s Skills Training and Education Pathways Upgradation Project in the amount of US$15 million, and Education ICT flagship project in the amount of US$13.4 million. 14. NKRA 7 on “Improving Quality of Education and Skills” aims to achieve excellence in student learning outcomes comparable to leading international standards across all sub-sectors of education. Recognizing the positive Page 3 of 60 The World Bank Bhutan Human Capital Recovery and Resilience Program for Results (P174399) effect of quality ECCD on enrollment, retention and learning outcomes for primary education, RGoB has committed to achieving 50 percent enrollment in ECCD by 2024 and improving ECCD service quality. As the quality of ECCD largely hinges on the quality of the ECCD facilitators, key activities under the 12th FYP include upskilling them and revising the monitoring framework. A second result area is curriculum digitization, to improve equitable enhancement of education quality and ensure that future generations have relevant 21st century skills, which has been set as the sector goals by the Bhutan Education Blueprint 2014-2024. Implementation of both result areas has been hindered by limited capacities, resource scarcity, and education disruption due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Accordingly, MOE is in the process of finalizing the Bhutan Education Roadmap 2021-2030 (BER), which calls for the expansion of and more equitable access to quality education and improving teaching learning practices post COVID-19 pandemic. The latter is evidenced by the roll-out of the New Normal Curriculum in 2021, which involves redefining the learning purpose and promoting more competency- based standards and assessment based on the lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic. 15. To support NKRA 11 on “Productive and Gainful Employment”, MoLHR introduced the Skills Development Plan (SDP) in 2021 to respond to the expanding needs of workers and job seekers affected by COVID-19. NKRA 11 includes various measures to holistically address Bhutan’s employment challenges: improving quality of MOLHR employment support and training, addressing gender disparities over employment and quality of jobs, revision of labor regulations to enhance employment in the private sector, better human resource development planning, and improved coordination with relevant ministries and agencies on skilling and job creation. The SDP aims to provide training support to around 75,000 job seekers whose employment or labor market entry was affected by the pandemic. The SDP aims to: (i) further strengthen the alignment of its training with short and long-term labor market needs, (ii) ensure connection to other necessary support in improving beneficiaries’ employability, including entrepreneurship support; and (iii) increase reach of support to cover women and job seekers in rural areas, ensuring inclusive recovery. 16. The achievement of NKRA 7 and 11 will be assessed based on the following Key Performance Indicators (KPIs): (i) student’s performance in secondary completion exams in core subjects such as English, Maths, Science and Dzongkhag; (ii) improved access to quality and inclusive education including ECCD; (iii) employment of Technical Vocational Education and Training (TVET) graduates after 6 months of training completion; and (iv) changes in the unemployment rate (including youth unemployment). C. PforR Program Scope 22. The proposed Program aims to support a subset of the government’s 12th FYP by strengthening implementation of selected activities that fall primarily under NKRAs 7 and 11 on improved access and quality of education and improved employment outcomes under 12th FYP including programs introduced to mitigate the impacts of COVID-19. Under this umbrella the Program aims to (i) ensure a strong foundation for lifecycle approach to boost Page 5 of 60 The World Bank Bhutan Human Capital Recovery and Resilience Program for Results (P174399) future human capital accumulation; (ii) support economic and human capital recovery post-COVID-19 pandemic, and (iii) increase human capital resilience post-COVID-19 pandemic. 23. The Program boundary is defined by activities/programs under relevant 12th FYP NKRAs implemented through MOE and MOLHR that contribute to: (i) improved child outcomes by strengthening and expanding ECCD services; (ii) accelerated learning recovery and improved access for school-aged children through ICT in education; (iii) increased inclusive skills training to address rural/urban and gender disparities; (iv) improved utilization of accumulated human capital through employment; (v) enhanced resilience of service delivery through program coordination in the area of employment support; and (vi) increased investments in human capital needed for climate resilience. Program boundary is not limited geographically aligning with the nationwide mandate of 12th FYP, though NKRA 7 and 11 have an additional focus on improving outcomes in rural areas and for vulnerable populations. The Program does exclude activities requiring large procurement contracts. Table 1 summarizes the alignment between the government program and PforR. 24. To improve the quality of ECCD programs, the PforR will support the delivery of safe and stimulating ECCD programs and promote learning through age-appropriate pedagogy. The government has articulated a strong commitment to ECCD in the NEP and draft BER. However, many of the resource and implementation arrangements need to be fully defined and therefore, the PforR will support a National ECCD action plan to achieve both the enrollment and quality goals. To improve the quality of ECCD services, the PforR will support upskilling of ECCD facilitators by improving the current training to focus on age-appropriate pedagogy and to be more inclusive and climate sensitive (fostering appreciation and respect for the natural environment). The PforR will also increase the number of ECCD facilitators trained under (i) the initial two-week basic certificate training; and (ii) the three-year in-service ECCD diploma training to cover 90 percent and 50 percent, respectively, of all ECCD facilitators working in government supported provision. Discussions are also ongoing with the MOLHR around aligning training qualifications for those working in private ECCD provision. The Program will also support strengthening the quality assurance system through the revision of existing quality standards for center-based ECCD models, and the development of quality standards for creches. At least 50 percent of focal monitors and MOE program managers will also be trained to support the effective planning, coordination, and monitoring of ECCD services. 27. To improve access to ECCD, the supply of free government ECCD services will be expanded through the Programalization of 30 new integrated ECCD centers targeting the urban poor and 90 new smaller ECCD centers targeting families in rural and remote areas. The integrated ECCD services will build on existing models of ECCD centers but will serve higher numbers of children per center, act as a resource and professional development center for the district and aim to eventually offer creche services for children ages 0 to 2. The smaller ECCD centers are alternative models to target more remote, rural populations and include the community providing the structure to house the ECCD program and parent volunteers leading the services in partnership with ECCD facilitators. The ECCD services will also provide parental education with deliberate efforts to target fathers. The RGoB will also work to address some demand- side constraints by raising awareness about the benefits of ECCD services for children as well as for women’s economic empowerment (and providing information on how to access services and identify quality) through a communications campaign targeted at parents, community leadership and local government officials. The expansion of ECCD services should help address childcare constraints to women’s economic participation and will also create job opportunities, given the vast majority of current ECCD facilitators are women. While there will not be any civil works associated with support to ECCD expansion under the Program, it will support strengthening of energy efficiency measures and promote access to potable water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) services. 28. To inform design and future investments in ECCD, the PforR will support a process evaluation of the ECCD services to identify potential course corrections that may be needed to improve quality and scale up. The Program will also support a longitudinal impact evaluation and baseline data collection. The process evaluation will include assessing implementation fidelity, quality (including classroom practice of ECCD facilitators), and uptake and reported impacts on women’s economic empowerment. The impact evaluation will assess child development outcomes, as well as longer- term outcomes for women’s economic empowerment (and impact on economic outcomes for fathers, where feasible). 29. IV. ASSESSMENT SUMMARY A. Technical (including Program economic evaluation) 52. Strategic Relevance. The Reforms to accelerate recovery from the COVID-19 impacts and build more inclusive and resilient human capital systems are strategically relevant, given that protecting and investing in people is critical to economic growth. As an investment, human capital leads to better-skilled and more productive workers who live and work longer and help drive a dynamic, resilient economy. The World Bank’s 2020 Human Capital Index (HCI) shows there is room for improvement in human capital accumulation. Considering the substantial public expenditures on education and health (6.6. percent and 2.5 percent of GDP, respectively), this unsatisfactory productivity level implies the need to change how investments are made to improve accessibility, quality, demand, supply, and services delivered more efficiently and effectively. 53. The proposed move to building, protecting, and utilizing human capital is also relevant to address key gaps, such as unequal (geographical, gender and socio-economic inequalities) access to quality foundational human capital services and underutilization of acquired human capital due to limited employment opportunities. For example, in 2021 only 28 percent of children ages 3-5 were enrolled in ECCD, but participation in rural areas is close to half of attendance in urban areas. Gender and spatial challenges in employment have been discussed in the Context section. These challenges for equitable human capital accumulation and better employability initiatives are laid out in the 12 th FYP, as well as SDP and BER. There is a case for government intervention to play an essential role in promoting access to education, training, and public employment services to all populations, especially for more vulnerable groups, including women and jobseekers in rural and remote areas. 54. Technical Soundness: The proposed Program would work with RGoB to address some drivers of low human Page 14 of 60 The World Bank Bhutan Human Capital Recovery and Resilience Program for Results (P174399) capital in the country: (a) limited access to certain services for ECCD and ICT competencies, (b) inequalities that lead to segments of the population being left behind, such as adolescent girls and women trying to join the labor market, and (c) high vulnerability to a spectrum of shocks and risks. Consequently, it will support increasing human capital and promote equality of opportunities while enhancing government capacity to improve the quality and resilience of service delivery in education and the employment sector. Specifically, the proposed Program will support increasing access to quality ECCD, enhancing the quality of education system through curriculum digitization and capacity building of teachers, various efforts to mitigate climate change in learning settings, and increase employment outcomes through tailored skills programs to market demands and linkages to employment opportunities, while investing in data and technology, to address the current challenges and improve the cost-effectiveness of existing programs and policies. 55. Gender: Women continue to fare worse in terms of human capital accumulation and utilization in Bhutan. Despite significant gains around equal access to education, girls’ enrollment lags that of boys throughout most of the school years. The disparity becomes apparent at the level of TVET15 and tertiary education completion rates.16 Nearly three times as many men than women complete the former and double as many complete the latter. Similarly, women (including teachers) are less likely to access technology, putting them at a disadvantage in adapting to new teaching methods and changing labor market needs. Once employed, women's outcomes are generally worse than men's in terms of the type of employment, hours, and earnings.17 56. The pattern is in part driven by norms around household responsibilities coupled with limited provision of ECCD services (significantly impacting urban poor and those in remote areas). A significantly higher percentage of women not in employment, education or, training (NEET) cites household duties as a key constraint. Women with children under 6 are much less likely to be employed.18 Furthermore, the analysis of LFS data from 2016-2018 showed that if there was someone else in the household to take care of children, women's labor force participation increased. This does create a case for the expansion of government-provided ECCD. Lower enrollment of women in TVET might be linked to mobility constraints19 and lower expectations around being hired after.20 Furthermore, even when women take up-skill training, they are more likely to enroll in traditional trades instead of ICT or other high-value skills. This might be driven in part by norms among employers but also by limited information on which courses give higher returns. 57. The Program aims to address the constraints above. Increasing evidence shows that access to ECCD services can positively impact women’s economic participation. ECCD services in Bhutan operate for at least 5 hours a day, and some are extended up to 8 hours to align with the needs of working parents. The Program will aim to preserve and expand this modality and strengthen outreach through communication on ECCD benefits (for children and working parents) and ways to access services. The PforR will also support cross-referral between ECCD and skill and employment support programs under MOLHR to connect mothers benefiting from ECCD expansion to labor market insertion support.21 This would be beneficial given that ECCD expansion will target urban poor and women in urban areas have lower participation rates. The Program will also support the impact evaluation of ECCD expansion on women's economic empowerment. Any person or entity debarred or suspended by the Bank will not be awarded a contract under, or otherwise allowed to participate in or benefit from, the Program during the period of such debarment or suspension by the Bank. 68. The conclusion of the FSA is that fiduciary risk is Moderate. However, subject to implementation of the PAP with the aim to mitigate identified fiduciary risks, the overall fiduciary framework is adequate to support Program implementation and to achieve the desired results. C. Environmental and Social 69. In accordance with the World Bank’s Policy/Directive for PforR, the World Bank has conducted an Environmental and Social System Assessment (ESSA) of Bhutan’s existing environmental and social management systems for the higher education and training/skills sector (applicable to MoE and MOLHR). This includes assessment of the national legal, regulatory, and institutional framework used to address potential environmental and social impacts of the PforR. The overarching objective of the ESSA is to assess the adequacy of the RGoB’s system to ensure that the risks and impacts of the Program activities are identified and mitigated, and to strengthen systems and build capacity to deliver the PforR in a sustainable manner. 70. The ESSA analyzed the environmental and social management system for the Program to determine applicability for the six Core Principles outlined in the Policy and ensure consistency with those that apply. The gaps with the Policy, were considered on two levels: (i) the system as written in laws, regulation, procedures and applied in practice; and (ii) the capacity of Program institutions to effectively implement the system as demonstrated by performance thus far. 71. In the present system of RGoB, all environmental and social assessments fall under the Environmental Assessment and Compliance Division (EACD) under the National Environmental Commission (NEC), which is an autonomous agency. The interventions of the Program are unlikely to have any severe/adverse environmental or social impact. The environmental and social risks associated with the Program is LOW and fall under the “Green Category” that does not demand environmental clearance. However, there exist certain gaps between the Implementing Agencies (IAs) and NEC/EACD that could be addressed through employing environmental, social and communication specialists by the IAs for the program to monitor compliances at the field level, strengthening the GRM system, training for capacity building and following COVID-19 protocols by the training institutions and ECCD centers. 72. Based on the analysis, the ESSA identified a number of key actions in order to ensure that the Program interventions are aligned with the relevant Core Principles 1, 3 and 5 for improved E&S due diligence which is appended in the ESSA Annex. 73. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected as a result of a Bank supported PforR, as defined by the applicable policy and procedures, may submit complaints to the existing program grievance redress mechanism or the WB’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly reviewed to address pertinent concerns. Affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the WB’s independent Inspection Panel which determines whether harm occurred, or could occur, because of WB non-compliance with its policies and procedures. Complaints may be submitted at any time after concerns have been brought directly to Page 18 of 60 The World Bank Bhutan Human Capital Recovery and Resilience Program for Results (P174399) the World Bank's attention, and Bank Management has been given an opportunity to respond. For information on how to submit complaints to the World Bank’s corporate GRS, please visit http://www.worldbank.org/GRS. For information on how to submit complaints to the World Bank Inspection Panel, please visit http://www.inspectionpanel.org. 74. Citizen Engagement: The project explicitly seeks to support the engagement of targeted beneficiaries and other stakeholders through comprehensive consultative processes and feedback mechanisms, given that promoting and improving access to essential education and employment services is a vital objective of the Program. V. RISK 75. The overall risk rating for this Program is “moderate”. Among the risk categories, residual risk for political and governance; sector strategies and policies; stakeholder; and fiduciary risks are deemed moderate. Environmental and Social risks are considered low. Macroeconomic risks are deemed substantial due to the economic impacts of COVID-19. Technical design and implementation capacity risks are deemed substantial due to cross-sectoral nature of the Program and the fact that PforR is a new instrument for the client. 76. Macroeconomic risks are substantial. The COVID-19 pandemic presents substantial economic risks. Bhutan, as a land-locked, trade-dependent economy has been impacted by the pandemic-induced global crisis through reduced demand for tourism and non-hydro exports, disruptions to trade, and social-distancing measures to contain the pandemic. The risk of a large-scale domestic outbreak of COVID-19 is reduced due to vaccination but given emerging new variants being a threat, it is not negligible. Shortages of input materials and rising prices pose further macroeconomic risks. The materialization of financial sector contingent liabilities, including from state owned financial institutions, could strain government finances further. To mitigate this risk the Program focused on programs that are aligned with long- term budgetary commitments from RGOB and minimized dependence of results on any other financing. However, with pandemic continuing to affect global economy putting recovery of Bhutanese economy and government revenue predictability at risk, even residual risk for this category is considered substantial. 77. Technical design and Institutional Capacity for Implementation and Sustainability risks are substantial. To achieve impacts on the human capital throughout the lifecycle and resilience the Program requires a cross sectoral approach. Furthermore, lack of familiarity with the new instrument may cause complications and risks delays. To mitigate these risks, the client will prepare the Implementation Guidance Note with clear description of roles and responsibilities around coordination and reporting, DLI verification, Monitoring and Evaluation (M&E), audits, ACGs, and GRM among others. The fiduciary and environmental and social capacity of the client will be further enhanced through stand alone technical assistance on these topics. On the technical side, the Program is well aligned with the ongoing technical assistance provided by the World Bank such as the--Integrated Approach to Employment & Social Protection systems (P170572), Bhutan Human Capital Acceleration PASA (P175646), and Creation of a Functional Labor Market Information System in Bhutan (P170574). Furthermore, the ongoing Public Financial Management (PFM) Multi Donor Fund Financed Strengthening PFM Project (P162196) helps to improve efficiency, transparency and accoutabilty of PFM Systems in Bhutan mainly through strengthening of PFM legal and institutional framework, budget execution and oversight .capacities, internal control and accounting systems. The reforms to accelerate the recovery from the COVID-19 impacts and build more inclusive and resilient human capital systems are strategically relevant for sustainable prosperity and poverty reduction, given that protecting and investing in people is critical to economic growth. The proposed move is also relevant to address key gaps, such as unequal (geographical, gender, and socio-economic inequalities) access to quality foundational human capital services and underutilization of acquired human capital due to limited access to employment opportunities. Furthermore, the disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the importance of having a more resilient system that helps protect the population's welfare and human capital accumulation and supports the skills needed for economic opportunities emerging during the recovery phase. B. Technical Soundness of the Proposed Program Table 3.1 Technical Soundness by Results Area Result Area 1: Expanded access and professionalization of ECCD The proposed Program will support the government in implementing its vision of expanded, equitable access to quality ECCD . The RGoB has made good progress in expanding ECCD services in recent years. However, there are significant gaps in access and quality (i.e., (i) limited access to ECCD in more remote rural areas and for urban poor and (ii) quality of service delivery due to limited capacity of ECCD facilitators and a nascent quality assurance system). To address these challenges the Program will support: (i) development of a National ECCD Plan, operationalization of additional ECCD centers targeting urban poor, and expansion of ECCD service in rural and low population areas; (ii) investments in the capacity of ECCD facilitators with an increased number of facilitators trained under the ECCD diploma and the basic certificate and inclusion of underserved populations by increasing additional ECCD centers, as well as strengthening the quality assurance system. Expansion of quality ECCD is also expected to have positive impacts on women’s economic empowerment. Result Area 2: Digitization of education for improved student outcomes Recognizing the importance of strengthening the equity, inclusiveness, and resilience of Bhutan’s education system, the Program will support the government programs on curriculum digitization, digital learning facilities in schools, teachers’ training to improve their digital pedagogy competencies, and enhanced climate awareness . Digital divide shown in a high ratio of device-to-student (21 and 17 percent in public and private schools, respectively) and limited internet access (38 percent of public school has no internet access, compared to only 5 percent in private school) is one of the biggest challenges in the country. The initiative of digitizing a curriculum, which can greatly improve its accessibility, especially in remote areas, would result in enhanced uptake, thus positively impacting the learning outcomes of all students. Device availability and internet access in schools would also be improved as essential tools for leveraging ICT in educational contexts. To realize the full potential of the digitalized curriculum and other ICT tools, the Program will also support enhancing teachers’ digital pedagogy competency and developing remote learning facilities in schools. Institutionalization of environmental awareness into curriculum would further Program’s contribution to environmental resilience. Result Area 3: Improve Employment and Inclusion Outcomes To achieve the goals of inclusive and gainful employment under NKRA 11, the PforR Program will support (i) inclusion of women and job seekers in rural areas; (ii) enhanced employment orientation of skill training programs; and (iii) strengthened delivery of entrepreneurship support. Women and those in rural areas are less likely to participate in off-farm and high-value sector skilling compared to men and those in urban areas. This results in limited diversification of income in rural areas (vulnerable to climate change) and limited impact of skill training on women’s economic empowerment. To enhance inclusion the program will rely on encouragement to deliver training outside of major urban centers to increase its availability to rural job seekers. SUMMARY OF PROGRAM CLIMATE CO-BENEFITS Country Context: With forest coverage exceeding 70 percent, Bhutan is the first carbon-negative country in the world. Climatic and environmental changes threaten Bhutan’s biodiversity-rich forest landscape, raising concerns for its constitutional mandate and low-carbon development pathway. Climate-related impacts such as drying water sources, changes in wildlife migration, agriculture, and forest cover, and loss of biodiversity can increase ecosystem vulnerability. Sustainable forest management is essential to improving ecosystem resilience and ensuring that Bhutan can continue to be a net carbon-negative economy.23 Flooding is one of the most significant climate-related hazards faced by Bhutan, with most of the country’s agricultural land and infrastructure located along drainage basins that are highly vulnerable to heavy monsoon rains and glacial-lake outbursts. The impact of flooding on human health and livelihoods is expected to grow and could be 4% of GDP by the 2030s. Climate models project a significant increase in the likelihood of heatwaves and droughts. These are likely to impact more severely on communities in Bhutan’s lowlands. Higher temperatures are projected to also contribute to increased snowmelt which could change patterns of river discharge and water availability. Impacts on infrastructure could grow significantly in the second half of the 21st century.24 Most of the major rivers in Bhutan originate from glaciers and are recharged by watershed. Most of the river discharge results from rainfall, supplemented by an estimated 2-12% glacial melt and another 2% from snow melt.” (NDC II, 2021) “Bhutan is a small yet water-abundant country.
