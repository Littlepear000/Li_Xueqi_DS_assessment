Country Context 1. Niger is a low-income, landlocked and fragile country in the Sahel region experiencing a three-fold crisis: security, humanitarian, and more recently health crisis related to the coronavirus disease (COVID-19). The regional insecurity and violent extremist groups threaten stability, fuel pre-existing tensions and cause the displacement of population. Among the estimated 22 million population, growing at about 3.8 percent per year, about 280,000 persons are in high-intensity conflict zones, 3.5 million are in medium-intensity conflict zones, and 6.2 million are in a zone at risk of conflict. The effects of the ongoing civil insecurity continue to affect negatively food security for poorer and displaced households. About 309 security incidents have been reported between January and October 2020, resulting in 968 fatalities according to the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data (ACLED) dashboard. The regions of Diffa, Tillabery, and Tahoua (see map in Annex 2) are most affected by insecurity and hosted an estimated 223,048 displaced people in April 2020, compared to 184,634 people in April 2019. Furthermore, despite progress made in recent years, poverty remains high in Niger, with two in five individuals (40.8 percent) living below the national poverty line in 20181. This corresponds to an absolute number of 9 million poor individuals, most of which (95 percent) resides in rural areas. 2. The COVID-19 pandemic is likely to have severe economic and social consequences in Niger and reverse some of the recent achievements in reducing poverty2. Since its onset in Niger in March 2020, the pandemic has claimed the lives of 162 Nigeriens and infected 4,548 people as of February 2, 20213. The economic slowdown and lower government revenues to finance social programs for its vulnerable citizens have also adversely affected many Nigeriens. The global COVID-19 outbreak is expected to have a significant negative impact on Africa’s economy, and it is expected that growth in Sub-Saharan Africa is predicted to fall to -3.3 percent in 2020.4 The effects of the pandemic-driven global economic downturn and its impact on Niger’s economy are difficult to predict at this stage, but it is likely that impact will be significant and negative. Health care-related spending could rise by as much as 2 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) and measures will be needed to cushion the pandemic’s social and economic fallout. A comprehensive response plan is in place5 with: (i) health measures (mainly prevention and containment); (ii) social protection (food and cash distributions and free utilities); and (iii) business support (predominantly temporary tax relief). 3. Access to natural resources is limited due to climate change impacting significantly the Sahel region, high population increase and non-inclusive territorial development policies.11 Niger has a hot and semi-arid to arid climate and is one of the hottest and driest countries in the world. The country is already experiencing severe droughts and heat waves that result in chronic food insecurity.12 Climate change is expected to aggravate the situation with temperatures projected to increase by 1.6-2.9°C by 2050 and with higher rainfall variability leading to increased occurrence and severity of heavy rainfall events. With elevated risk of extreme droughts, heat waves and floods, Niger’s population is highly vulnerable to climate shocks13. Climate change impacts include water scarcity, reduction in soil moisture, increased erosion and plant damage, crop failure and reduced yields, increased incidence of pests and diseases impacting agriculture and livestock breeding, important sources of livelihood for the local communities.14 Furthermore, heatwaves and flooding also increases risk of infrastructure damage and transport disruptions. Landlocked conditions and high costs of doing business obstruct Niger’s development prospects. Being landlocked and at least 1,000 km from the nearest seaport, combined with low levels of human capital, are formidable obstacles to developing more extensively private sector economic activities. These disadvantages worsen with elevated costs of doing business. Niger’s ranking in the Logistic Performance Index (LPI) dropped from 100th to 157th out of 160 countries between 2016-2018, below the average LPI of Sub-Saharan Africa. Transport costs are high because of insufficient roads maintenance, roadblocks are common, and competition in the trucking sector is limited. 7. The Trans - Saharan corridor, a 4,600 kilometer (km)-long historic trade route, is one of Niger’s main transport axes. It runs in a north-south direction across the Saharan desert from Algiers, Algeria to Lagos, Nigeria and passes through Tamanrasset in Algeria, Agadez and Zinder in Niger, and Kano in Nigeria. This corridor not only connects Niger to the markets of the first and fourth- largest economies in Africa (Nigeria and Algeria), but also plays a critical role in the trade between Niger’s agricultural surplus regions of the South and the deficit regions of the North, with most of the imports into Zinder coming through this corridor from Kano, Nigeria. At the national level, the corridor facilitates both nationwide social and economic connectivity, and trade, which is key to strengthening the social fabric and preventing conflict. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. Road transport is by far the most dominant transport mode in Niger,16 and the National Road No. 11 (RN No.11) is the main transport axis connecting Agadez and Zinder. The country has a total road network length of 20,268 km, of which 10,649 km are national roads, 2,075 km are regional roads, and 6,952 km are unclassified roads. Within the total road network, only 4,655 km are paved roads. The Agadez–Zinder road segment is a 425-km-long section of RN No. 11, one of Niger’s key transport axes and part of the Trans-Saharan corridor. RN No. 11 is a major national trade route through which the northeastern region of Agadez receives agricultural goods coming from the Southern regions of Maradi and Zinder, and through which the southern regions receive livestock from the North. The dominant economic activity along the RN No. 11 axis is the primary sector (agriculture and livestock) followed by small-scale commerce. 9. Decades-long lack of regular maintenance of the Agadez–Zinder section of the RN No.11 have negatively affected the connectivity of the northeastern Agadez region. The paved road section starting from Arlit and passing through Agadez, Zinder and down to the Nigerian border was originally constructed in the mid-1980’s as a two-lane road with a 6-meter-wide paved driving surface and 1-meter-wide usable shoulders on each side. Political instability in the 1990s and a resulting lack of funds allocated for regular maintenance led to substantive deterioration and significantly shortened the life span of this road section. The pavement failed structurally, which means that vehicles could only travel at slow speeds, travel times and transport costs increased substantially, and flash floods, sand dunes movement and encroachment sometimes interrupted traffic. Connectivity along the RN No.11 is expected to deteriorate further without interventions to improve road and drainage infrastructure standards, and without introducing maintenance practices that consider climate change impacts. The relatively narrow road platform, absence of road safety equipment through urban areas and absence of both vertical and horizontal markings also negatively affect road safety. Donor programs helped rehabilitate and upgrade a part of the Agadez–Zinder section of the RN No. 11, leaving only the Tanout-Tiguidit part in urgent need of rehabilitation. In recent years, the Government of Niger (GoN) has been able to mobilize funding from various external and internal sources for the rehabilitation and upgrading of several sections of the RN No. 11 corridor between Agadez and Zinder. The European Regional Development Fund (EDF) has provided funding for the rehabilitation of 200 km of road between Zinder and Agadez, namely for the sections Zinder- Mai Bagari (40 km) where the rehabilitation is fully completed, Mai Bagari-Tanout (98 km) where the rehabilitation works are almost completed, and for Agadez-Tiguidit (62 km) where the rehabilitation is fully completed. The road section between Agadez and Arlit has also been upgraded with funding related to the uranium mines near Arlit. The rough unpaved road section between Arlit and the border with Algeria is currently being upgraded to a paved road with funding from the Arab Fund for International and Social Development and the African Development Bank (AfDB) funds. As a result, the only missing link of the Nigerien section of the Trans-Saharan corridor is the one between Tanout and Tiguidit (226 km) which remains in a deteriorated condition and urgently requires rehabilitation, upgrading and road safety improvements. 11. In Niger, the funding level for road maintenance is still not adequate despite successfully implemented reforms that ensure commercialized management of road assets. A recently established Second Generation Road Maintenance Fund17 collects about US$17 million annually through a fuel levy, tolling fees and fines for overloaded trucks to finance periodic and routine maintenance, and emergency works, for the total network of 20,268 km. These funds barely cover the routine maintenance needs of the network and fall short of covering needed periodic maintenance to extend the life of key infrastructure. A road maintenance agency (Agence de Maitrise d’Ouvrage Déléguée de l’Entretien Routier, AMODER) was established to oversee the execution of the road maintenance program. An independent road maintenance contracts’ audit unit (Cellule d’Audit Courant des Marchés d’Entretien Routier, CACER), anchored at the Ministry of Finance, oversees technical and financial audits to make sure the funds have been used efficiently for the intended purposes. Concept and Characteristics of Output- and Performance-based Road Contracts (OPRC) Output-and Performance-based contracting for roads is designed to ensure that the physical condition of the roads under contract is adequate for the need of road users over the entire period of the contract (which is normally several years) and that the road asset is handed over in good condition at the end of the contract. This type of contract significantly expands the role of the private sector, from the simple execution of works to the design, management and conservation of road assets. During the bidding process for OPRC’s, contractors compete among each other by essentially proposing fixed lump-sum prices for bringing the roads covered by the contract to a certain service level and then maintaining it at that level for a relatively long period. It is important to understand that contractors are not paid directly for “inputs” in the form of physical works (which they will undoubtedly have to carry out), but for achieving specified Service Levels, i.e. the Rehabilitation of the road to pre-defined standards and Maintenance Services for ensuring certain Service Levels on the roads under contract, both representing outputs or outcomes. A monthly lump-sum remuneration paid to the Contractor will cover all physical and non-physical Maintenance Services provided by the Contractor, except for unforeseen Emergency Works which are remunerated separately. The Rehabilitation and Improvement Works which have been explicitly specified by the Employer in the contract are quoted on the basis of measurable output quantities and paid pro rata as performed. In order to be entitled to the monthly payment for Maintenance Services, the Contractor must ensure that the roads under contract comply with the Service Levels which have been specified in the bidding document. It is possible that during some months he will have to carry out a rather large amount of physical works in order to comply with the required Service Levels and very little work during other months. However, his monthly payment remains the same as long as the required Service Levels are complied with. A fundamental feature of the OPRC is that the contractor is responsible for designing and carrying out the works, services and actions he believes are necessary in order to achieve and maintain the Service Levels stated in the contract. If the Service Level is not achieved in any given month, the payment for that month shall be reduced or even suspended. Under the OPRC, the Contractor has a strong financial incentive to be both efficient and effective whenever he undertakes work. In order to maximize profits, he must reduce his activities to the smallest possible volume of intelligently designed interventions, which nevertheless ensure that pre-defined indicators of Service Level are achieved and maintained over time. This type of contract makes it necessary for the Contractor to have a good management capacity. Here, “management” means the capability to define, optimize and carry out on a timely basis the physical interventions which are needed in the short, medium and long term, in order to guarantee that the roads under contract remain above the agreed Service Levels. In other words, within the contract limitations and those required to comply with local legislation, technical and performance specifications and environmental and social regulations, the Contractor is entitled to independently define: (i) what to do; (ii) where to do it; (iii) how to do it; and (iv) when to do it. The role of the Road Administration and of the Employer is to enforce the contract by verifying compliance with the agreed Service Levels and with all applicable legislation and regulations. The project will strengthen the resilience Page 20 of 64 The World Bank ENHANCING NIGER NORTHEASTERN CONNECTIVITY PROJECT (P171793) of populations residing along the corridor against conflict, disease and climate change in the Saharan zone, by improving rural roads and basic socio-economic infrastructure for selected local communities. Moreover, in addition to building of socio-economic infrastructure, the CE activities, targeting pastoralist communities, awareness raising and risk monitoring related to SEA/SH and human trafficking, as well as activities aimed at facilitating the empowerment of women are expected to contribute to preventing escalation of conflict and violence along the Agadez – Zinder road section. The key underlying assumptions are: (i) timely availability of technical studies; (ii) timely deployment of experienced contractors; (iii) high quality of works; (iv) high quality of studies and experienced consultants; (v) availability of experienced NGO to implement SEA/SH mitigation measures; and (vi) strong political commitment, local community participation, and appropriate capacity of the PCU to complete the functions required of such an infrastructure project (e.g. procurement and financial management (FM)). To be conservative, the economic analysis carried out has focused on Component 1 activities, namely road rehabilitation, maintenance and safety improvements, which absorb about 86 percent of the project resources. For this project component there are proven analytical tools and methods and a solid cost-benefit analysis can therefore be undertaken. For Component 2, while economic and social benefits certainly exist, it is not possible to quantify those with any acceptable level of precision and reliability at this stage. 62. The Highway Development and Management Tool (HDM-4) was used to carry out the analysis, which simulates life cycle conditions and costs, and provides economic decision criteria for road construction and maintenance activities. The project’s main development impact is improved connectivity, which is linked to the ability of citizens to physically move at a reasonable cost and speed. These factors are captured in the Component 1 cost-benefit analysis which entails the combination of (i) the rehabilitation of the IDA-financed part of corridor road, between Tanout and Tiguidit of 226 km and (ii) the performance-based management and maintenance of the entire 425 km corridor road between Zinder and Agadez. The economic analysis also considered road safety related costs identified using the Road Safety Screening and Appraisal Tool (RSSAT), as presented in Section C below, and GHG emissions costs (without and with the project). 63. The results of the cost-benefit analysis show a Net Present Value (NPV) of US$103.76 million and an economic internal rate of return (EIRR) of 16.6 percent. The sensitivity analysis carried out as part of the economic analysis simulated (i) an increase in construction costs of 20 percent, resulting in an EIRR of 14.2 percent; (ii) lower expected project benefits, by 20 percent, resulting in an EIRR of 13.7 percent; and (iii) a combined increase in construction costs of 20 percent and lower expected benefits by 20 percent, resulting in an EIRR of only 9.4 percent, which is nevertheless still higher than the adopted 6 percent discount rate. 64. The GHG accounting analysis indicates that the total gross Carbon Dioxide (CO2) emissions over the 20-year evaluation period under the without-project scenario are estimated at 0.649 million tons and under the with-project scenario at 0.911 million tons resulting in a net increase of CO2 emissions with the project of 0.261 million tons, or 13,075 tons per year. The increase in CO2 emissions is attributed to the increase in travel speeds with the project, which in turn is due to the improved road conditions created by the project. The proposed project will apply OPRCs with an eight-year duration, which include the following scope of works and services: (i) The initial full rehabilitation of the 226 km Tanout-Tiguidit road section; (ii) Road Asset Management and Maintenance Services for the entire 425 km corridor road between Zinder and Agadez; (iii) the application of a Single Bituminous Surface Treatment for the entire road of 425 km in years 7 and 8 of the contract, which should ensure that the pavement will remain good for several years after the end of the project. The contracts will also have provisions for Emergency works which may be needed to repair damage from unforeseeable events (climate-related or other). 66. The MoE has hired an international engineering consulting firm with extensive experience in Niger to carry out a detailed design for the rehabilitation of the Tanout – Tiguidit road section. The typical cross-section is in line with the standard adopted by West African Economic and Monetary Union (Union Economique et Monétaire Ouest Africaine, UEMOA) for this class of road, and has also been applied on the other sections of the corridor road for which the rehabilitation was funded by other financing institutions. This design, which will be proposed to the bidders for the OPRC contracts, has the following main characteristics: the rehabilitated road platform will be 10.2 meters wide, with two lanes of 3.6 meters width and sealed shoulders of 1.5 m width. This means that the road will be widened by about 1.2 meters on each side, remaining nevertheless well within its existing right-of-way. Wherever the road crosses villages or small towns, the roadbed will be further widened to allow for safe pedestrian sidewalks and vehicle parking along the road. The riding surface shall be executed as Double Bituminous Surface Treatment (DBST) which has shown good results in Niger’s climatic and traffic conditions. The rehabilitation works also include the strengthening of the road’s base course, much improved horizontal and vertical signaling and many other road safety improvements. These include measures to reduce speed in populated areas and to increase the distance between opposing traffic elsewhere. 67. An assessment of the conditions conducive to the success of OPRCs in Niger was carried out as part of the technical feasibility study. It showed that the capacity of local and international road contractors and engineering consultants present in Niger is sufficient for this type of contracts which require good technical, engineering and management capacities that go beyond those normally needed for the execution of traditional road works contracts. The project activities also include technical support for the MoE which is in charge of managing the overall project implementation. During the project preparation period, the World Bank team has provided substantial technical support to the client in the specific area of OPRC. 68. The OPRC contracting method will address the issue of inadequate incentives for contractors under traditional works contracts to ensure sustainability. In particular, it will ensure quality during construction by making the contractor accountable for road service quality through long-term contracts, in this case eight years. It also ensures the day-to-day management and maintenance of the road during that period. Contractors are not paid directly for “inputs” or physical works, but for achieving specified Service Levels, i.e. the rehabilitation of the road to pre-defined technical and quality standards, the maintenance service of ensuring predefined Service Levels on the roads under contract, and specific improvements such as road safety enhancements and measures to increase climate resilience, all representing outputs or outcomes. A monthly lump-sum remuneration paid to the Contractor covers all physical and non-physical maintenance services provided by the Contractor, except for unforeseen emergency works, which are remunerated separately. The OPRC contracts will also include pavement strengthening in year 7 and 8 of the contract, which should ensure that the road would resist the traffic load and remain in good condition for about another eight years after the end of the project. 69. The choice of long-term (eight-year) OPRCs was made to break the vicious cycle often observed in many countries, namely: first, the expensive reconstruction of roads, followed by absence of adequate maintenance, leading to premature road deterioration and then again the need for reconstruction, long before the expected lifespan of the Page 26 of 64 The World Bank ENHANCING NIGER NORTHEASTERN CONNECTIVITY PROJECT (P171793) road is achieved. The management and maintenance services which are a key part of the OPRCs will not only cover the road sections to be rehabilitated under the project, but also several other sections along the road between Agadez and Zinder which have either recently been rehabilitated (or where rehabilitation is ongoing) under traditional works contracts which do not include maintenance arrangements. 70. Alternatives were considered for OPRC contracts of longer duration than eight years, but rejected. From a purely technical perspective, the ideal contract duration should be 15 years, which is the expected lifespan of the selected DBST pavement design. However, the 15-year option was rejected for several reasons: (i) The World Bank would not be able to fully fund a 15-year contract duration. Consideration was given to funding the first 8 years of a 15-year contract, with the Road Fund or the GoN committing to pay the remaining seven-year period, but it was found that this would introduce too much uncertainty and risk concerning the actual availability of funding for the last seven years. This risk would appear as too high for contractors who may not be willing to submit bids at reasonable prices. (ii) In terms of Contracting Strategy, it was recognized that Niger is clearly a high-risk FCV country, not only in terms of security, but also in terms of availability of Government funds to pay contractors. Qualified international contractors are typically only willing to participate in bidding for large construction contracts if they know that the contracts are fully funded by external sources. In addition, the cost of obtaining and maintaining a Performance Security with a commercial bank is high for Contractors in any case, but it is even higher for contracts taking place in high-risk countries such as Niger. The cost is proportional to the length of a contract. While this is still a manageable issue for an 8-year contract, it would become prohibitive for a 15-year contract, especially if the funding by the external donor ends after 8 years. 71. A contract duration of less than eight years was also considered but rejected, because it would not allow holding the contractor sufficiently accountable for the longevity of the road. Road failures that are due to low-quality road construction (or rehabilitation) works normally occur four to seven years after the completion of the construction/rehabilitation works. If the OPRC contracts were shorter than eight years, the likelihood of premature pavement deterioration or failure due to shoddy construction appearing during the contract period is low. Therefore, one of the main benefits of OPRC contracts, of holding the contractor accountable for premature road deterioration, would just not be present for contracts with a duration of only five, six or seven years. Eight years is thus deemed to be the minimum appropriate contract duration for paved roads. In the past there have been OPRC contracts with shorter (5 or 6 years) duration, imposed mostly due to the unwillingness of financing institutions to support longer projects, but there is now a widespread consensus that such shorter contracts are sub-optimal for paved roads and do not make use of the full potential benefit of this contracting method.33 C. The historical rainfall data dating back to 1983 and the rainfall forecast factoring climate change shows that the number of days of rain during a given rainy season will decrease but the amount of rain would increase, increasing the risk of flash floods. 75. The climate vulnerability assessment also identified measures to enhance climate resilience which have been included in the project. These include: (i) the improvement of engineering design (especially of drainage structures) and materials (see Components 1 and 2 for more details); (ii) the inclusion of continuous maintenance in the project (see Components 1 and 2 for more details); and (iii) inclusion of provisions for the contingent emergency component (see Component 4 for more details). 76. Climate change adaptation measures included under Component 1. To enhance resilience to extreme temperatures and floods, several measures have been introduced in the engineering design and materials selection of road works. To strengthen the hydraulic design of the road drainage structures, culverts with larger cross-sections will replace the existing corrugated metal pipes. Certain locations will need to consider the issue of the gradient of the road and the need to place additional culverts. The corrugated metal pipes were found to be unsuitable, not only because of their small diameter to absorb the water run-off, but also because of the difficulty of cleaning accumulated siltage. Finally, the choice of the Double Bituminous Surface Treatment for the pavement will help to withstand extreme temperatures better, compared to Asphalt Concrete pavement. Extreme heat often leads to deformation and premature deterioration of Asphalt Concrete pavement, especially on roads with heavy trucks circulating. The good availability at reasonable cost of suitable materials for producing crushed stone and gravel of adequate quality contributed to this choice. To enhance resilience of roads to movement of sand dunes, control measures will be deployed to ensure all season- connectivity along the corridor. The higher frequency of sandstorms and sand drifts will increase the movement of sand dunes, resulting in higher risks for road blockages and clogging of drainage structures. The measures included in the planned road rehabilitation works is the installation of sand drift fences and vegetation to protect the road platform. Sand drift fences are used to catch sand before it reaches the road. The fence is installed perpendicular to the wind direction and sand deposition takes place due to sudden drop in the airstream pressure around the fence. Vegetation increases the surface roughness of the sand sheet or sand dune and thus reduces sand movement. This solution is feasible with local plants that can withstand very harsh conditions such as low precipitation and extreme temperatures. 78. (I) Environment 103. The Environmental Risk Rating is Substantial. This project will support the rehabilitation and upgrading of the 226 km road segment from Tanout to Tiguidit, and the long-term maintenance during eight years of the entire road between Page 34 of 64 The World Bank ENHANCING NIGER NORTHEASTERN CONNECTIVITY PROJECT (P171793) Zinder and Agadez (425 km). This road is a crucial lifeline for the delivery of services and movement of people and goods. Extreme temperatures, high winds, flash floods and sandstorms in the region impact this road. The project aims to make this road section climate resilient, which will have an overall positive environmental impact. However, the planned civil works are likely to be associated with temporary adverse environmental risks and impacts that will need to be managed to meet the requirements of the Environmental and Social Standards under the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF). These risks and potential negative impacts are related to: (i) resource efficiency and pollution prevention and management (sourcing of materials for civil works, water and energy use, GHG emissions, dust, noise, potential contamination of water sources due to spills and run-off of petroleum products, infiltration into storage areas and improper disposal of fuels; (ii) occupational and community health and safety (OCHS); (iii) potential loss of vegetation and biodiversity during civil works and preparation of sites, quarries areas for extraction of materials and higher demand of fuelwood by workers; and (iv) potential impact of civil works and excavations on cultural heritage. 104. The Borrower prepared an ESMF that covers all project components except the rehabilitation of the Tanout- Tiguidit road section which is covered by an ESIA and ESMP. The ESIA and ESMF include environmental health and safety requirements to be incorporated in the Contractor’s ESMP for civil works and rehabilitation of infrastructure. Relevant procedures relating to cultural heritage for “chance finds” have been annexed to both the ESMF and the ESIA as they are applicable to all project contractors. The EDF provided funding for the rehabilitation of 200 km of road between Zinder and Agadez. Since the project will support the operation and maintenance of this road, the Borrower will prepare an environmental and social assessment and audit for the operation and maintenance of the road segments that were not rehabilitated under the World Bank-funded project. A Supervision Consultant firm will be hired to monitor compliance with environmental and social requirements related to the OPRC implementation . This consulting firm, with a dedicated specialized team member for environmental and social issues, will be responsible for verifying and enforcing all contractual requirements, during the entire contract duration of 8 years. (ii) Social and Security 105. The social risk of the project is rated High. This project road is in a fairly remote part of the country and is part of a corridor that on one hand contributes to improving connectivity between Nigeria and northern Niger, but on the other is also used for a significant volume of human, drug and arms trafficking. It is a major transportation route for long haul freight trucking that provides important goods and access to markets for the population along the road but the improvement of the road may contribute to an increase in SEA/SH, banditry, illegal smuggling including human trafficking and threats to the local communities. In addition, project activities such as the rehabilitation of the Tanout-Tiguidit road section may potentially generate some economic displacement which could also affect women. As the road is in a fairly remote area of the country, the adequate management of these risks and adequate mitigation may be difficult and this could potentially contribute to social conflict and/or loss of public support for the project. The situation is further compounded by the fact that on-site implementation support by the World Bank team to verify compliance and help the PCU to verify screening and manage risks is also likely to be restricted. 106. The rehabilitation of the 226 km Tanout-Tiguidit section of the existing road may have negative effects on the health, safety and security of the neighboring communities. Experience indicates that the influx of workers into a project area can lead to adverse social impacts (SEA/H, sexually transmitted and communicable diseases), especially in rural areas. To manage these risks, the potential social issues are first identified and evaluated in the ESIA, and then the targeted actions are spelt out in the CHSP, LMP and the ESMP. The ESMP includes labor-related clauses that clearly define the contractor’s responsibility regarding SEA/SH, community health and safety including human security and labor management. SEA and SH. Component 3 of the project focuses on the prevention of SEA/SH in the project areas. A 2015 national survey in Niger revealed that women are three times more likely to be survivors of some form of physical violence than men, especially in a rural and poor zone such as the project area. The civil works under the project involve large-scale constructions activities that require the influx of male workers near communities with low absorption capacity and scarce social services, and where lack of economic opportunity prevails, compounded by social norms approving unequal power gendered relationships. The civil works are spread across a wide geographic area, difficult to supervise, with sites near school routes that women and girls use for their daily activities. The project will seek to minimize risks of GBV/SEA/SH and will include the following key measures in contracts as part of the procurement process of the civil works: (a) mandatory signature of the CoC (including SEA/SH) required by every contractor staff (both managers and workers) that clearly defines SEA/SH and outlines sanctions for breaching the CoC; (b) mandatory and repeated training and awareness raising regarding the obligations stated in the CoC; and (c) on a case-by-case basis, agreeing to cooperate with law enforcement agencies in investigating complaints. 109. The project will explore opportunities to work with existing local specialized NGOs and partners such as the UNFPA that can provide technical assistance and monitoring to support the implementation of the action plan. Moreover, an NGO specialized in GBV will be recruited prior to the start of civil works to provide prevention activities such as: mapping of GBV service providers in areas of implementation, conducting an awareness campaign, trainings to workers and communities; referral and care support to survivors; and monitoring of effectiveness of mitigation measures. The referral of survivors will rely on the existing service provision pathways established at the national level through the National Strategy for the Prevention and Response to GBV and with support from UNFPA. Based on the project’s approach, a SEA/SH survivor will be able to address a complaint to the GBV GRM and will be referred to the appropriate services such as medical, psychosocial and legal services. The specialized NGO and relevant government representatives working on the project will provide SEA/SH awareness training and relevant capacity building activities to the implementing agency in order to provide them with the skills and knowledge on how to incorporate gender into the project cycle, including integration of prevention and response to SEA/SH in the anticipate rural road rehabilitation and maintenance works. The PMRC-PCU will recruit by project effectiveness a SEA/SH specialist to support project implementation, and a SEA/SH action plan will be finalized and approved by project effectiveness. 110. The results of the national survey on the causes and determinants of GBV, conducted by the Ministry of Population for the Promotion of Women and Child Protection and UNFPA Niger in the eight regions of Niger revealed that women are three times more likely to be survivors of some form of physical violence than men are. They are 2.7 times more likely to be survivors of some form of sexual violence than men are. The risks of experiencing some form of psychological or economic abuse are respectively 1.02 times and 1.4 times higher for women than for men. Women's risk of experiencing any form of cultural violence is 14 times that of men38. 3. GBV risks from labor influx in project areas. The project is in a rural and poor zone where GBV is common. The civil works under the project involve a major quantity of constructions that require influx of male workers near communities in a low absorption capacity with scarce social services and where lack of economic opportunity prevails, compounded by social norms approving unequal power gendered relationships. The civil works are spread in wide areas, difficult to supervise, with sites near school routes that women and girls use for their daily activities. Niger has a high rate of child marriage. Seventy-six percent of girls in Niger are married before their 18th birthday and 28 percent are married before the age of 15. According to United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund (UNICEF), Niger has the highest prevalence rate of child marriage in the world and the 14th highest absolute number of child brides – 676,00039. In this regard, important programs are in progress, in particular, the "Illimin" teenage initiative, which aims to reduce the rate of child marriage and to delay teenage pregnancies. Niger also has a good practice of reporting and protecting survivors of GBV through the “safe space” and the “school of husbands” initiatives established by UNFPA at the village level. The “safe space” shelters for survivors of GBV, protects them against retaliation and helps the survivor get the needed medical and legal services. The “school of husbands” are associations of husbands created to raise awareness among other men and husbands against GBV and change their behaviors against women. As such, 320 Safe Spaces across the country were set up hosting 32,000 adolescent girls in 2017. 6. Overall, however, Niger has a score of 40 out of a maximum of 100 on protecting women from violence against a score of 46 for Africa and 54 for the world40. There is no law on SH in the workplace. These structures intervene as much in the prevention as in the psychological, medical, social, economic and legal care of the survivors. The actors in the public sectors are the justice system, the police, the technical services of the ministry in charge of women empowerment, the communes and the health services. These actors are present in all regions and are the legitimate enforcers of the legal texts related to GBV. The weakness of the public-sector actors is the inability of health infrastructures to offer quality service to survivors, lack of training and inadequate laws to respond to GBV. 8. The civil society organizations, including national NGOs, are very active in the prevention and response to GBV. The ministry of women empowerment has created a consultation platform of all actors in the fight against GBV to create synergies in actions and to ensure an effective impact. The well-known NGOs that are represented in most of the regions are: SOS Women and Children Survivors of Domestic Violence (SOS Femmes et Enfants Survivants de la Violence Domestique), Association of Women Lawyers (Association des Femmes Juristes), DIMOL and CONIFPRAT. Contractor’s responsibilities. The project will seek to minimize risks of GBV/SEA/SH and will include the following key measures in contracts as part of the procurement process of the civil works: (a) mandatory signature of the CoC (including SEA/SH) required by every contractor staff (both managers and workers) that clearly defines SEA/SH and outlines sanctions for breaching the CoC; (b) mandatory and repeated training and awareness raising regarding the obligations stated in the CoC; (c) and on a case-by-case basis, agreeing to cooperate with law enforcement agencies in investigating complaints. The contractor’s ESMP (C-ESMP) will be required to describe GBV and SEA/SH risks and include an action plan (including a response and accountability framework), requirements toward mitigation of environmental, social, health and safety (ESHS) risks, and inclusion of appropriate related mitigation measures. Bidding documents will require the Contractor not to undertake any works, including mobilization, until all measures are in place for addressing ESHS risks. 11. Working with GBV service providers. Given the anticipated difficulty in reaching women/girls in the remote project and community areas, the project will explore opportunities to work with existing local specialized NGOs and partners such as the UNFPA that can provide technical assistance and monitoring to support the implementation of the action plan. Moreover, an NGO specialized in GBV will be recruited to provide the following prevention activities such as: mapping of GBV service providers in areas of implementation, trainings to workers and communities; referral and care support to survivors; and monitoring of effectiveness of mitigation measures. 12. Referral of survivors will be based on the established national GBV response framework. The response will rely on the existing service provision pathways established at the national level through the National Strategy for the Prevention and Response to GBV and with support from UNFPA. Based on the project’s approach, a GBV survivor will be able to address a complaint to the GBV GRM and will be referred to the appropriate services such as medical, psychosocial and legal services. 13. A GBV specialist will be hired in the PCU with the capacity to support project implementation. 14. The project will ensure that these community structures are not reinforcing gender norms and solving cases under an approach that is not survivor centered. Mixed migration channels, smuggling, and traffickig (of people, drugs, and weapons) play a significant role in the informal economy. According to the IOM’s 2020 World Migration Report, Niger is an important country of transit, as well as a major smuggling hub for migrants seeking to travel from West Africa to Southern Europe (see Figure 4.1)42. The city of Agadez, located 60km north of Tiguidit, is a hotspot for human smuggling routes that link Mali and Nigeria to Libya and Algeria – and by extension to Europe. According to research funded by the European Union, about 400 people per week join migrant convoys traveling between Agadez and Dirkou, down from 2,000 in 2016.43 This is partly the result of the Government’s efforts to combat illegal migration through measures such as the criminalization of human smuggling through the 2015 Law No. 36. The UNODC is supporting Niger to develop more effective responses to trafficking and smuggling through its GLO.ACT Project.44 However, research has also shown that in addition to drugs, people, and weapons, the contraband also involves essential goods necessary for survival of poor and vulnerable communities.45 COVID-19 will likely worsen the situation of vulnerability and aggravate the impact of informality. Page 62 of 64 The World Bank ENHANCING NIGER NORTHEASTERN CONNECTIVITY PROJECT (P171793) ANNEX 5: Impact of the COVID-19 in Niger and Government’s Response 1. The COVID-19 pandemic has significantly impacted the development trajectory of an economy suffering from persistent security challenges and cumulative crises. The health impact of COVID-19 in Niger has not been as severe as expected given relatively low number of cases - now that the numbers total 4,458 confirmed cases and 162 deaths. The pandemic, however, has adversely impacted the Nigerien economy. Growth is expected to decline to 0.5 percent in 2020 from 5.8 percent in 2019, and total revenues are set to decline by 10 percent while spending increases by 1.6 percent of GDP, of which 0.8 percent of GDP is directly attributable to fighting the pandemic. The final review of the three-year Extended Credit Facility (ECF) program was approved by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) Executive Board on Oct. 26, 2020 and negotiations for a new ECF are planned once the new Government is sworn in around April 2021. 2. The pandemic and related economic shutdown and border closures reversed recent achievements in reducing poverty. Results from macro-micro simulations suggest that the poverty rate in Niger would increase by about 1.6 percentage points in 2020, and 370,000 additional people are projected to fall into extreme poverty in 2020. Beyond the monetary dimension, the COVID-19 crisis is also expected to have lasting effects on the non- monetary dimensions of well-being, such as education, health, and food security. 3. The Government was swift to announce containment measures and prepare an emergency response plan to curb the impact of the pandemic. At the onset of the pandemic, and the first confirmed COVID-19 case in Niger on March 19, 2020, the Government took quick measures to contain the spread of the disease, including closing airports, borders, banning travel to and from Niamey, closing schools and recreational areas, and instituting social-distancing measures. Subsequently, the Government presented its COVID-19 Preparation and Response Plan to development partners on March 27, 2020. The Plan estimated costs of about US$2.2 billion (CFAF 1.296 billion) to prepare for and respond to the sanitary, social, and economic impacts of the pandemic. WBG Response and Adjustment to the Country Program 4. Remaining within the broad parameters of the Niger CPF, the WBG program was adjusted to support the GoN’s response to the COVID-19 crisis. These adjustments align with the priorities of the WBG Approach Paper “Saving Lives, Scaling-up Impact and Getting Back on Track”. The World Bank rapidly deployed and re- purposed IDA resources in the FY20-21 pipeline and active portfolio and leveraged trust fund resources to support the country’s COVID-19 Preparation and Response Plan. More specifically the following adjustments to the program were introduced: • Saving lives: mobilizing US$7.8 million under the Regional Epidemic and Diseases Surveillance in West Africa (REDISSE) to support initial health sector activities in partnership with UNICEF; preparing a US$13.9-million COVID-19 Emergency Support Project approved on April 15, 2020; and mobilizing US$5.5 million from the Global Pandemic Fund to strengthen the health system, laboratories, community engagement and training for health workers (in partnership with WHO). • Saving livelihoods and protecting the poor: The World Bank revised reform measures under the Fostering Inclusive Growth Development Policy Operation (DPO2) (P173113) to ensure social protection for 30,000 households and mitigate the impact of other measures on basic services (US$250 million, approved on August 6, 2020). The World Bank reallocated US$46 million under the Adaptive Safety Nets 2 Project to scale up cash Page 63 of 64 The World Bank ENHANCING NIGER NORTHEASTERN CONNECTIVITY PROJECT (P171793) transfers to poor and vulnerable households in urban and rural areas; and provide support for weather-related shocks. The program is implemented in partnership with WFP and UNICEF among others. • Under the Climate Smart Agriculture Project (P153420), US$20 million was reallocated to support the agriculture campaign and mitigate food insecurity, including to finance critical inputs for farmers, livestock, irrigation and sustainable land management. • Additional resources were made available under other projects in agriculture, education, water and sanitation, electricity services, public administration and local development to maintain critical public services and support the recovery. 5. In December 2020, the GoN requested World Bank support to help finance their COVID-19 vaccination program. The government’s goal is to vaccinate 47.78 percent of the population (11 million persons) this year. The total estimated cost is US$136 million towards which the Government has included US$7.4 million in the 2021 budget, leaving a gap of US$128.6 million. The World Bank will provide support to the immediate financing needs of the government’s Covid-19 vaccination program by mobilizing some US$5 million from the ongoing Population and Health Support Project (P147638) and US$2.5 million from the COVID-19 Emergency Response Project (P173846). Additional US$28 million is being mobilized from IDA financing through an additional financing to the Emergency Response Project (P173846) which is expected to be delivered in fourth quarter of FY21. This World Bank financing will supplement support from COVAX facility, which will cover vaccine for 20 percent of the population. 6. The World Bank is adapting its portfolio supervision efforts to ensure timely implementation and results despite COVID-19 disruptions. COVID-19 restrictions have compounded access challenges in remote or conflict- affected zones due to security issues. World Bank teams have adapted supervision approaches, utilizing technology and alternative means to continue interface with the clients and monitoring of project activities. Project implementation units have also strengthened and adapted their telecommuting and teleconferencing equipment and facilities to promote social distancing in offices and to work virtually where possible. Selectivity, Complementarity, Partnerships 7. The World Bank’s response is closely coordinated with the IMF, UN, AfDB and other development partners. The World Bank has closely coordinated its health and social protection response with UN specialized agencies. In addition, the World Bank and UNDP jointly financed the government’s e-governance efforts to connect ministers and key ministry staff and thereby, support the virtual public administration. Further coordination with the United Nations and other partners is being pursued and complementarity is being explored to effectively and efficiently support Government’s efforts to purchase and successfully deploy the COVID-19 vaccines in Niger.
