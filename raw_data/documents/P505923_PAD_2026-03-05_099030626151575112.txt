STRATEGIC CONTEXT 1. The Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (SCBFE) Program is an ambitious and transformational commitment to create jobs through sustainable management of the Congo Basin Forests (CBF). The world’s second- largest tropical forest system yields significant local, national, and global benefits, absorbing around six times more carbon annually than the Amazon.1 The Program’s dual focus on jobs and sustainability is especially important in CBF countries (Cameroon, Central African Republic (CAR), Republic of Congo (RoC), Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Equatorial Guinea2, and Gabon) given the potential of their natural resources and growing young population. This Multi-Phase Programmatic Approach (MPA) proposes an indicative IDA and IBRD financing envelope of US$1.02 billion equivalent. Phase 1 is expected to create 220,000 jobs and reduce annual greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions on average by 17.6 million tCO2e. A. Regional Context 2. The Congo Basin region is characterized by low economic growth, extreme poverty, and high fragility. Gross domestic product (GDP) growth in the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (Communauté Économique et Monétaire de l'Afrique Centrale, CEMAC)3 region, decelerated to 1.8 percent in 2023, down from 3.1 percent in 2022.4 Nearly 13 percent of the world’s extreme poor live in the CBF countries,5 with the majority of extreme poor located around forested landscapes, where they depend heavily on subsistence agriculture and forest resources, with limited access to basic services and economic opportunities. Ongoing conflicts and insecurity in the region continue to displace thousands and exacerbate disputes over control of natural resources.6 3. The region is missing opportunities to leverage natural resources for economic and structural transformation, a critical prerequisite for job creation. The contribution of the forest sector to GDP (ranging from 4 to 6 percent in CBF countries in 2021 7) has stagnated, largely due to a focus on exporting logs rather than developing local wood processing. Unreported extraction and fragmented domestic markets undermine price discovery, traceability, and reliability of legal supply for downstream processors. 4. Concurrently, job-intensive domestic and export activities are stagnant, and most businesses are informal and characterized by low-productivity.8 The need for more and better jobs is exacerbated by high population growth rates and increases in working-age adults. Estimates project that the population of Central African countries will increase by a third by 2035 and double by 20509. The CBF cover about 200 million hectares (ha), an area slightly larger than Indonesia or Mexico, and account for between 35-90 percent of the total national land areas in each of the six CBF countries. The CBF is the only tropical forest system that still serves as a global carbon sink.12 These forests sustain an estimated 40 million people in nearby urban centers and directly provide livelihoods for an estimated 60 million people who live in or near the forest areas, including local communities and Indigenous Peoples, comprising at least 150 different ethnic groups.13 Critically, about 100 million ha (or about half of the CBF), are not under active management14 and are mostly unallocated forests that have potential for sustainable management. About 54 million ha of the CBF are under commercial forest concessions, while Protected Areas of high conservation value constitute 30 million ha. 6. Forest loss is rising in the CBF, driven by subsistence-scale fuelwood and charcoal production, mining, small- scale agriculture,15 and informal extraction of timber, particularly in unallocated or unmanaged forest areas.16 DRC recorded the highest rise in forest loss among CBF countries, from 300,000 ha in 2015 to approximately 600,000 ha in 2024.17 Studies show18 that nearly half (48 percent) of CBF wood production takes place informally, mainly from these unallocated forests. For example, the informal charcoal value chain19 is valued at millions of dollars and contributes thousands of jobs. However, high levels of informality raise due diligence costs for investors and undermine creditworthiness. Unmanaged, informal logging also leads to social problems such as conflicts and labor abuse. 7. Community co-management in unallocated forests could improve sustainability and expand economic opportunities for communities. This approach has been effective in other tropical regions, such as in Latin America and Asia, when combined with clear tenure security and institutional and technical support.20 CBF countries have robust forest laws, and many have recently updated their national forest laws with provisions for community forest management; but Box 1: Forest Laws in CBF Countries Cameroon, CAR, and RoC (Phase 1 of the MPA) have been developing comprehensive and robust forest laws, but implementation is a challenge. Cameroon’s 2024 Forest and Wildlife Law, CAR’s 2008 Forest Code, and RoC’s 2020 Forest Code all embed Indigenous Peoples’ and local communities’ rights into forest governance. CAR is in the process of updating its Forest Code. CAR received technical assistance from the World Bank to develop a stronger focus on participatory management and rights of local communities and indigenous peoples, better definition of the forest domain, classification procedures and permits. Cameroon’s law integrates customary and usage rights, guarantees stakeholder p articipation, and establishes mechanisms for community forests and protected areas. CAR’s current Forest Code secures customary rights, requires FPIC for relocations, and supports community forestry through management agreements and technical assistance. Despite being the source of 80 percent of Africa’s wood exports, CBF countries account for only half a percent of the global wood value chain21. Development of the wood value chains in the region is also limited by the lack of robust timber legality verification systems, skilled labor, and accessible financing. Only 24 percent of forest concessions in the RoC have third- party certification for legality/sustainability. Community forest institutions and SMEs face high fixed costs for legality verification, limiting their access to premium international markets.23 National information systems like Cameroon’s SIGIF 2.0 (Système Informatique de Gestion des Informations Forestières) are being developed with support from the European Union’s (EU) Forest Law Enforcement, Governance, and Trade (FLEGT) Action Plan and the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) of the United Kingdom. SIGIF 2.0 issues automatic barcodes across the timber value chain but lacks full compliance with EU Timber Regulations (EUTR). Most countries in the region lack specialized vocational institutions aligned with modern industry needs, while existing training centers are often under-resourced. Banks view the forest sector as high-risk, limiting operators’ ability to invest in processing.24 10. To fully and sustainably unlock the potential of CBF for driving economic growth, creating jobs, and reducing poverty, it is essential to implement targeted interventions across both upstream and downstream segments of the wood value-chain. This approach should include: (i) supporting the effective implementation of regulations and procedures, with a particular focus on empowering local and community institutions by strengthening their capacity to oversee forest management, protected areas management, and participate in decision-making processes. By building the capabilities of these local institutions, communities can take an active role in ensuring the legality and sustainability of wood production and biodiversity conservation which helps foster inclusive growth and job creation at the local level; (ii) facilitating the formalization of informal wood-based enterprises to promote sustainability, compliance, and access to finance and markets which helps firms to grow; and (iii) strengthening wood value chains by forging stronger connections among operators, integrating SMEs into processing and product development, and broadening access to both domestic and regional markets. The jobs agenda is central to these activities, creating employment opportunities both upstream— in sustainable wood production managed by empowered local and community institutions—and downstream, in the wood processing industry where SMEs and larger firms collaborate for product innovation and market expansion. IFC is expected to play a pivotal role by providing advisory services to promote best practices in wood processing industry, facilitating access to infrastructure and finance for SMEs, and supporting market linkages that enable growth and job creation throughout the value chain. D. Multiphase Programmatic Approach 13. Rationale for a horizontal MPA: A horizontal MPA is suitable based on the following factors: (a) Addressing common regional challenges, including through learning and policy harmonization: The CBF countries share a common forest biome and similar forest management systems, and they face comparable technical and policy challenges in advancing their forest economies. Regional institutions such as CEMAC and COMIFAC, with mandates to convene member countries on regional trade as well as forest and biodiversity policies, serve as important anchors for harmonizing wood trade and tariff regimes, guiding industry development, and advancing forest governance and biodiversity protection. While countries are progressing at different paces, each country offers valuable lessons. A horizontal MPA provides a platform for countries to build on each other’s experiences and pursue a coordinated, mutually reinforcing approach to strengthening forest economies and conserving their shared natural heritage. At the same time, the MPA will provide a platform to leverage institutions such as CEMAC and COMIFAC for regional policy harmonization, learning, and stakeholder engagement. (b) Covering cross-border externalities: (i) Climate mitigation and adaptation and ecosystem services. The CBF absorbs around 600 million tons of carbon annually, and the sustainable management of these forests and protected areas will maintain their global mitigation and adaptation function, as well as their essential role in the water cycle in the Sahel. (ii) Reduced deforestation and forest degradation and increased assurance of sustainability of tropical wood and non-wood products entering regional and international markets. This will be achieved through improved wood traceability and certification as well as forest regeneration and conservation of biodiversity. (iii) Meeting the sustainability and green industrialization aims of the African Continental Free Trade Area Agreement (AfCFTA) and its Africa Green Industrialization Initiative (AGII). Program Framework and Components 14. CBF countries share similar forest biomes and encounter common challenges in management, capacity, and resources, particularly regarding: (i) ensuring a legal supply of wood for both domestic industries and export markets; (ii) developing forest resources for, and in partnership with, local communities and enhancing conservation of critical biodiversity; and (iii) advancing the local wood processing industry. The Program is designed to address these constraints through three interconnected components. Together, these components work synergistically to overcome key barriers and promote sustainable forest management, community development, and industry growth in CBF countries. • Component 1 – Policies and Institutions: This component focuses on updating and strengthening forest regulations, as well as enhancing information systems to improve verification of wood legality within CBF countries. • Component 2 – Forest Resources Development: This component supports production of sustainable biomass and wood by promoting community and locally managed forests and enhanced protected area management. • Component 3 – Industry and Enterprises: This component aims to boost private sector access to finance and improve infrastructure to support the growth of domestic SMEs in wood processing. 15. The Program adopts a unified One World Bank Group approach, strategically sequencing public investments supported by the World Bank, IFC advisory and investment services as well as MIGA guarantees, to unlock private capital throughout the wood value chain. As progress is made in establishing wood legality and sustainability—and as key sectoral reforms create new investment opportunities – IFC and MIGA will leverage existing pipelines and portfolios to scale up financing within the sector. Notably, IFC has a US$600 million pipeline in the Congo Basin focused on enabling infrastructure such as roads and energy, which can directly address critical logistical barriers to private investment in wood processing. MIGA is active in the Republic of Congo, offering proposed risk coverage for the asc Impact Forestry Project – Congo (Project ID: 15345 with expected Board approval in FY26Q4). Close coordination will be ensured so that the Program remains responsive to and informed by private sector needs, using IDA financing to target and unlock emerging investment opportunities in the region. It will also align with other development partner programs in CBF (see paragraph 39). 16. The MPA is organized into three overlapping phases, all aligned with the Program’s Development Objective: (a) Phase 1 will launch national investment projects in Cameroon, CAR, and RoC. In addition, it will provide targeted support to CEMAC and COMIFAC, aiming to enhance their capacities in forest management and conservation, including efforts toward policy harmonization across the region. Phase 1 countries have similar challenges of high dependence on export of minimally processed wood and wood traceability systems still under development. As first movers, Phase 1 countries will have the opportunity to develop their traceability systems and domestic wood processing sector and benefit from the MPA Learning Agenda, with opportunities to learn from the experience of Gabon. This sequencing helps reduce the sector development gap between the CBF countries. (b) Gabon, which has already made substantial progress addressing some sector challenges, will join the Program in the second phase, with activities potentially centered on advancing wood Official Use Only Page 5 The World Bank Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (P505923) – Phase 1 processing industries through the establishment of a second Special Economic Zone (SEZ). This phase may also feature investments to mitigate human-wildlife conflict and promote community-based forest management. (c) DRC, as the largest among CBF countries, has unique needs which will be addressed in Phase 3. The focus could be on expanding efforts in managing forest landscapes and fostering development of domestic wood SMEs to satisfy local market needs. 17. Each phase will finance planned activities across all three components concurrently, targeting the weakest links in the wood value chain. Activities within each component will be strategically arranged to ensure that regulations are being implemented and capacity is adequate to support upcoming investments. Component 1, which centers on forest regulations and traceability systems, will be prioritized, as its progress is fundamental for sustainable forest development under Component 2 and enabling SME financing under Component 3. In Component 2, technical assistance (TA) for preparing management plans and selecting sites will be completed in accordance with national laws and World Bank requirements before any forestry investments are made. 18. For Component 3, the sequencing is as follows: (a) offering TA to SMEs to help integrate informal businesses into the formal system, and providing TA to financial institutions; (b) upgrading training centers, developing curricula, and assisting existing SMEs in accessing legal wood through contracts with logging companies; (c) providing access to finance to SMEs, conditional on compliance with the wood legality verification systems established in Component 1; and (d) launching investments in SME service parks after a comprehensive preliminary process—including site selection, feasibility studies, and relevant environmental and social assessments—to ensure informed decision-making and effective risk management. IFC could potentially offer advisory services during stages (a) and (b) and later provide investments as the enabling environment for private sector participation improves. Additionally, MIGA’s asc Impact Forestry Project – Congo can help ensure a steady supply of legal wood to SMEs through contracts established under (b). Lessons Learned 40. Community managed forests are effective in generating commercial success and jobs in addition to enabling their sustainable management.31 The MPA builds on decades of World Bank investments in community co-management and wood industry in Latin America, East Asia, South Asia and Africa, which demonstrate that communities can not only record financial success and decrease deforestation but also lower poverty rates and increase jobs when compared to communities who do not manage their forests. These results were more pronounced when investments continued over a longer period and were accompanied by legal and policy reforms. An average rate of return ranging from 20-81 percent has been reported for community forest enterprises in Brazil, Mexico, Guatemala, and Cameroon.32 41. Certification advances the job creation agenda, while addressing illegal logging. Certification schemes, whether legality verification, FSC, or national standards, shift market demand toward formal operators, enabling SMEs (and community forests) to access higher-value domestic and export markets. To comply, firms must upgrade processes, invest in traceability, and professionalize operations, which create new employment in milling, drying, and related services. Certified firms can also gain access to facilities and collective equipment in the Project-supported SME service parks and are also more likely to attract finance and investment, allowing them to expand production and hire additional workers. 42. Active engagement with a wide range of stakeholders is essential for the success of forestry operations. Forests are local, national, and global public goods where the interests of various actors often do not converge. To enable success on the ground, it is essential to (i) convene all key stakeholders; and (ii) have a pro-active and open communication with civil society, Indigenous Peoples, development partners, and the private sector. The Program will ensure extensive stakeholder engagement through the Regional Stakeholder Platform managed by COMIFAC and organize campaigns to raise awareness and share information among all the participating countries. 43. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis 49. The Project is grounded in a "whole-of-sector" theory of change that addresses the CBF’s most binding constraints: weak governance and legality systems that undermine sustainable forest management; underdeveloped forest landscapes that constrain the supply of legal, sustainably produced timber and non-timber forest products (NTFPs); and an undercapitalized domestic wood processing industry that fails to generate the jobs and economic value that the region's exceptional forest wealth should support. The Project addresses these constraints through an integrated upstream to downstream architecture, sequenced to build from the institutional to the productive to the commercial. Component 1 establishes the enabling environment through operationalization of forest codes, modernization of national forest information, legality, and traceability systems and improved forest inventories. These systems are sequenced ahead of largescale private sector support, ensuring that downstream investments under Component 3 rely on verified, legal, and sustainable wood supply. Component 2 applies technically sound approaches to forest resource development, combining plantations, assisted natural regeneration, community and communal forest management, and improved protected area management, supported by site selection criteria, management plans, and exclusion lists to manage environmental and social risks. Component 3 strengthens technical and financial viability of wood-based SMEs through targeted access to finance instruments, SME service parks, skills development, and conditional matching grants linked to legality compliance. The project design leverages proven international experience in community forestry, strengthening the stewardship of IPLCs in natural resource management, certification, and cluster based industrial development. Climate resilient and biodiversity positive practices are incorporated, and strong safeguard frameworks, third-party monitoring, and grievance redress mechanisms are embedded. Together, these features ensure technical feasibility, risk mitigation and sustained impact across governance, forest productivity, and value addition. Phase 1 of the MPA, in the three countries of Cameroon, CAR and RoC, is economically viable using standard efficiency parameters. The investment costs for the three Phase 1 countries of the MPA are US$394.83 million over 8 years (2026-2033). The benefit-cost analysis (BCA) yields a net present value (NPV) of US$773.00 million without taking into consideration GHG emissions reduction benefits, and an economic internal rate of return (EIRR) of 24 percent. The NPV rises to US$14.20 billion with an EIRR of 66 percent using the high social cost of carbon (SCC) and in a scenario using the low SCC, the incremental Project-linked economic benefits amount to US$7.56 billion, with an EIRR of 54 percent.36 The BCA estimates just three economic benefits – increased processed wood exports, more direct and indirect jobs in the timber and wood industry, and GHG emission reductions – out of all measurable benefits. Other measurable benefits that are not included in the present analysis include incremental forest ecosystem services (e.g., watershed services regulation, pollination, improved micro-climate, biodiversity conservation, and soil erosion reduction) from protecting existing forests from deforestation and from Project-afforested areas;37 and additional income from producing greater quantities of better quality NTFPs for sale in national markets. Other less-tangible incremental benefits not measured in the current analysis include increased awareness, capacity, and empowerment of government forest staff and forest- dependent communities (especially women and youth), reduced land degradation and poverty, and greater resilience to climate change. Thus, the BCA is likely underestimating the economic viability of the Project. 51. Phase 1 is also financially viable. In addition to the economic analysis – which does not include taxes or subsidies but includes social benefits like GHG emissions reduction – a financial analysis was carried out, using market prices (inclusive of taxes and subsidies) for inputs and outputs, but not including social welfare benefits. This financial analysis shows that the Project is profitable for private Project beneficiaries, with an NPV of US$1.10 billion and a financial internal rate of return (FIRR) of 28 percent. These are conservative estimates as the BCA assumes that (i) costs are equally distributed over the 8-year Project lifetime (2026-2033); (ii) benefits start only after Project completion in 2033; (iii) benefits flow over a 20-year post-Project period (2034-2053); and (iv) the social rate of discount is 9 percent. D. Environmental and Social 67. The environmental and social risks are rated as High. These risks are viewed at two levels: the Project-specific risks, which are relatively contained and technical in nature, and the contextual and sector-wide risks, which are more complex, interlinked, and influenced by external drivers beyond the Project’s direct control. While the former can be addressed through well-established mitigation measures, the implementation context in the Congo Basin significantly elevates overall risk due to its ecological sensitivity, biodiversity, carbon value, and systemic risks in the wood sector including illegal logging, deforestation, weak governance, fragility and conflict-affected (FCV) conditions, and limited oversight. The most significant risk is that Project-supported wood processing enterprises could inadvertently increase demand for illegally harvested timber, which may be further exacerbated by weak traceability, while transboundary pressures such as wildlife trafficking, uneven regulatory enforcement, and artisanal mining could exacerbate deforestation and conflict. Amplified by weak institutional capacity and the FCV context of several participating countries, these systemic, multi-layered, and interlinked risks—ranging from localized impacts to broader cumulative governance and ecosystem threats—justify a high environmental and social risk classification for the MPA. Environmental risks associated with Component 2 activities include soil and water contamination, habitat disturbance, vegetation loss, erosion, waste generation, and air pollution. This is based on the public sector’s low capacity and lack of resources which limit effective governance, policymaking, and investments. Private investment is held back due to unclear or overlapping tenure issues and policy challenges. The interests of multiple stakeholders – government, private sector, IPLCs, and international NGOs – often do not converge, which contributes to the challenges. The regional context of limited access to timely information and poor infrastructure compound these challenges. 73. The overall Project risk for Phase 1 is rated High based on the High Environmental and Social and Stakeholder risks and a range of other risks (including Political and Governance, Macroeconomic, Sector Strategies and Policies, Technical design, Institutional capacity, fiduciary risks) are rated Substantial. 74. Political and Governance risks are Substantial. Risks arise from potential political instability, divergence of political interests and Project objectives, resistance to increased transparency, wider access to information, accountability and oversight arising from the deployment of wood legality verification systems and greater involvement of private sector and non-state actors in Project activities. Mitigation measures embedded in design include greater stakeholder engagement and collaboration with development partners in the sector and proactive communication. In addition to consultative processes, Project manuals will include detailed criteria for selection of beneficiaries and Project locations to mitigate risk of undue political interference in implementation. Other mitigation measures will include third- party monitoring during implementation, in-person and e-GRMs in countries and at the regional level, an independent panel for oversight of implementation. To address security risks in CAR, the Government of CAR has prepared and will implement a Security Management Plan. 75. Macroeconomic risk is Substantial. Key risks could include suspension of Project activities due to wider macro- fiscal instability, insufficient counterpart financing of salaries for Project implementing agencies and for expenditures such as forest law enforcement, not covered by the Project. Project countries may continue to allow export of unprocessed logs to counter fiscal challenges, which may affect private sector investment in wood processing facilities, impacting the long-term potential for growth of wood value chains. The World Bank’s ongoing dialogue with the countries on macroeconomic stability and budget support operations, including structural reforms contribute to mitigating this risk. To mitigate exposure to macro-fiscal volatility, the Project design initially prioritizes activities with limited recurrent Page 24 The World Bank Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (P505923) – Phase 1 fiscal implications, particularly community forestry, and plantation establishment. Investment sequencing allows core public-good functions (forest governance, legality, traceability, and community support) to proceed before access to finance and credit lines which need greater macroeconomic stability are initiated. 76. Sector Strategies and Policies risks are Substantial. Developing and implementing regulations under the forest codes, strengthening community forest management and ensuring traceability of timber for domestic markets are among the key areas which will need significant political will and action within the government and cooperation with multiple stakeholders. The Project will address these issues through coordination with development partners already active in the sector (EU, UK FCDO, GIZ). In addition to national investment activities directed towards improving policy and governance environments, the Regional Component will establish regular dialogues and provide training to private sector operators and the public sector in collaboration with partners such as ITTO, on legality compliance, sustainability and social aspects to address concerns regarding governance. 77. Technical Design risk is Substantial. Design risks could include potential for unverified wood being processed by Project-financed SMEs, low capacity among community institutions vested with forest management responsibilities, lack of uptake of formalization incentives by informal operators, low professionalism in the SME sector and the risk that the financial sector may not respond to Project incentives to offer long-term finance to SMEs. To mitigate these risks, activities in the three components will be sequenced. For example, SMEs will receive financing after ascertaining the functionality of wood traceability systems and the SMEs’ own compliance systems. A communications campaign will be launched to highlight the benefits of legal and verified wood to communities and entrepreneurs, and on the simplification of small-scale wood extraction procedures. Promotion of lesser-known commercial timber species will be supported to allow SMEs access to lower-cost alternatives to high-priced timber, thereby reducing the incentives to over-harvest. Community institutions will receive technical services to obtain approvals for forest management plans before commencing wood extraction. Financing for SME service parks will be phased to start small, demonstrate their viability, and expand based on initial successes, thereby ensuring sustainable growth and development in the wood value chain. Technical assistance will be offered with IFC support to strengthen banks’ capacity to lend in the wood value chains. Vocational centers will be established or strengthened to enhance SME technical and managerial skills. The duration of the Project allows for sustained capacity building of institutions and course correction during implementation. 78. Institutional Capacity for Implementation risks are Substantial. Some of the challenges include lack of inter- institutional coordination, insufficient resources and lengthy and bureaucratic procedures which limit collaboration between ministries. The Project will have oversight by inter-ministerial Technical Committees to overcome some of these challenges. The PIUs in Ministries of Forests will include staff and experts from the Ministries of SMEs to strengthen collaboration during implementation. Institutional assessments carried out during preparation will also inform development of targeted training programs for forest rangers, eco-guards, and community facilitators with training on gender, social inclusion, and conflict-sensitive approaches. This risk will also be mitigated by high-level oversight of the national projects through inter-ministerial steering committees and a Regional Steering Committee of Ministers of Economy to be convened by CEMAC. 79. Multiple stakeholders and unequal power dynamics raise stakeholders' risk. Stakeholders in this case could be categorized as ‘winners’ and ‘losers’ or as (i) high influence/high interest groups who will have decision-making power and could influence Project decisions and outcomes; (ii) low influence/high interest groups who could be impacted by the Project but do not have the power to influence the decisions; and, (iii) high influence/low interest groups who could have greater influence but may not be interested in the Project. The first group—comprising government officials, influential NGOs acting as service providers, and senior executives of large timber enterprises—presents particular risks as their interests may include maintaining benefits from the existing status quo (e.g., illegal logging, transport, and trade), and who can exert undue influence over Project investments, or steer resource allocation for personal gain. To mitigate these risks, the Project will ensure transparent and publicly disclosed criteria and engage in continuous stakeholder dialogue. Independent audits and third-party verification mechanisms are built into the Project along with strong grievance redress systems and third-party screening of site selection to avoid conflicts. 82. The low influence/high interest group would include Project beneficiaries such as informal SMEs, communities, Indigenous Peoples and vulnerable groups whose livelihoods are dependent on communal or state forest resources and those lacking secure land tenure and who may be at a disadvantage due to the power dynamic with government officials. The Project will deploy participatory planning and FPIC and ensure that these groups have equitable access to Project-financed livelihood options, training or entrepreneurial support. SMEs and informal enterprises will be targeted for training, matching grants and formalization supported by clear eligibility criteria and safeguards against elite capture. Each country will develop solutions to simplify the administrative processes and reduce the costs of permits for small- scale wood logging and registration licenses. The Project will also support artisanal entrepreneurs and others working with illegal timber (such as transport workers, charcoal makers, etc.) with technical training, and linkages with registered legal wood suppliers and other options to join formal operations. The Project will also promote diversification of commercial timber species and create market incentives for legal and sustainably sourced wood to offset higher costs for price conscious consumers. PIUs will be sensitized on dealing with this group of vulnerable stakeholders, including continuous feedback, adaptive management, and strong GRM mechanisms to help address risks. High influence/low interest groups, such as development partners, media and civil society, will be targeted with strategic communications, coalition building and advocacy to raise awareness and reduce misinformation and increase transparency about the Project. 83. Stakeholder risks will also be addressed through the Learning Agenda and the Regional Stakeholder Engagement Platform, which will facilitate a better understanding of the challenges faced by different actors, provide communication channels, and improve cooperation. This Platform will facilitate targeted communications and dialogue between specific groups of stakeholders such as large forest companies and small wood processors. To mitigate stakeholder risks and turn potential ‘losers’ into ‘winners’ the Project will: (i) deploy a robust communications and information campaign and organize regular information and feedback sharing sessions through the Independent Advisory Panel which will be a part of the Regional Stakeholder Platform to inform and engage all stakeholders; (ii) pilot and scale appropriate modern IT equipment and associated products and services (including satellite connectivity, digital MRV). And free, offline-capable mobile data collection tools (KoBo Toolbox) will be used to offset these shortcomings when working with remote communities; and (iii) in addition, the PIUs in each country will adhere to the actions agreed in the ESCPs and ensure that GRMs are fully functional. Box 1.1. NTFPs: The Potential for a New Bioeconomy in Cameroon In Cameroon the combined economic value derived from both marketed and consumed NTFPs is estimated at 32.3 billion XAF (US $64.7 million), representing about 0.2 percent of the national GDP. While a large number of NTFPs are consumed domestically and within the region as food and medicine, there is potential to develop the value chains for oils used in cosmetic and pharmaceutical industries within the country and in export markets. The sector remains largely informal and unregulated, limiting its contribution to sustainable development and public revenue. The Project will support priority value chain development of three to four high potential products and explore options for sustainable production through agroforestry. Products that could be explored would include honey and bush mango (Irvingia gabonensis) as food and seed oil in the East, South, and South-West, Mbalaka (Pentaclethra macrophylla) seed oil for pharmaceuticals in the South, Djansang (Ricinodendron heudelotii) seeds in the East and South-West, which are highly popular in traditional foods in Central and West Africa, and bamboo for construction and handicrafts in the Central, South, and South-West parts of the country. The Project will also develop a digital application for managing statistical data on NTFPs. The proposed platform will enable efficient data collection, analysis, monitoring, and dissemination, improving transparency and decision-making. LOCs and PCGs are most effective when there are macroeconomic stability and no large fiscal deficit to affect financial markets. The CEMAC regional economy lost momentum in 2023, according to the International Monetary Fund (IMF). “The external position weakened, with the current account shifting to a deficit and foreign reserve accumulation slowing. While inflation continued to ease, it remained in the 4 percent to 6 percent range for CEMAC countries in 2024. Available data indicate a deterioration in the underlying fiscal positions of many countries.” There are mixed signals from macroeconomic performance, with foreign asset reserves targets not met in 2024, and debt vulnerability increasing in some cases. 4. NGIF will apply risk-based guarantee pricing, ranging from 2 percent for financial institutions with non-performing loans below 4 percent, to 2.5 percent for FIs with NPLs up to 12 percent. A stop-loss mechanism also protects the fund: if the claim rate of an FIF exceeds 12 percent of its outstanding loan portfolio covered by the guarantee, the issuance of new guarantees would be suspended and the payment of existing guarantees halted until the rate falls below the threshold. The guarantee coverage is higher for women entrepreneurs, youth and other marginalized businesses (70 percent versus 60 percent for general SMEs). The fund will apply a prudent leverage ratio of 2 in the first three years. This approach limits exposure while the fund strengthens its operational track record, builds a sound guarantee system for the loan portfolio, and refines its engagement with PFIs. The fund will not charge a commitment fee, eliminating additional costs for PFIs and encouraging the full use of the guarantee. Investment income is projected at 7 percent, based on the average return on public securities issued by the Bank of Central African States (Banque des États de l’Afrique Centrale, BEAC) in January 2025, which helps support the fund's operations and long-term viability. Training is integrated to support both PFIs and SMEs.
