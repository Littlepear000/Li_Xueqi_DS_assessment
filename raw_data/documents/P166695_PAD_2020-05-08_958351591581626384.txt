STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Three years into the country’s political transition, the Government of The Gambia (GoTG) has taken important steps to set the country on a new development path. Following the 2017 Parliamentary elections and local elections held in April 2018, the GoTG initiated reforms directly responsive to citizens’ high expectations for quick improvements in service delivery. The GoTG has launched the implementation of economic, public financial management (PFM) and state-owned enterprise (SOE) reforms. The GoTG has allowed for a freer press, re-joined the Commonwealth, and has re-built relations with Senegal and with international financial institutions. The Cabinet of the GoTG, issued a White Paper in October 2019 endorsing the findings and recommendations of the Jammeh Commission of Inquiry set up to investigate the financial activities of public bodies, businesses and offices connected to the former President Jammeh1. The nationwide consultative Truth, Reconciliation and Reparations Commission conducted public hearings since January 2019, providing a platform for reconciliation within communities splintered by atrocities committed by the former regime. Meanwhile, a new Constitution was drafted by the Constitution Review Commission, submitted to the President and published in March 2020. The draft Constitution clearly defines Presidential term limits and powers and includes provisions to strengthen accountability to the National Assembly. The Constitution will be taken for a referendum prior to being presented to the National Assembly for approval by end of 2020. Secondary legislation is being reinforced to limit interference in areas of economic management. 2. Poverty incidence remains high and is associated with low endowment in human capital and assets. In 2015, 10.1 percent of the population lived below the international poverty line of US$1.9 (2011 PPP) per day per capita. Using the national poverty line, the proportion of the population living in poverty remained unchanged between 2010 and 2015, at about 48 percent due to lack of growth in per capita income. In absolute terms, the poverty headcount grew from 790,000 in 2010 to 930,000 in 2015. The population, 60 percent of which is under 25 years of age, is concentrated around urban and peri-urban centers where poverty is lower due to higher economic activity from commerce, public administration, and service sectors and remittances. Economic volatility, drought, and unemployment drive youth migration abroad. The Gambia scored 0.4 on the Human Capital Index, 2018, on par with the regional average. While The Gambia scored above the regional average on survival rates, stunting, and school enrollment, it scored slightly lower than the regional average on learning outcomes. 3. Important progress has been made in restoring macroeconomic stability and reigniting growth. Following strong performance under the 2019 International Monetary Fund (IMF) Staff-Monitored Program (SMP), with a sharp reduction in the fiscal deficit, and debt relief from key plurilateral and bilateral creditors, The Gambia was able to exit from debt distress paving the way for an Enhanced Credit Facility (ECF) approved by the IMF Board on March 23, 2020. The fiscal deficit was reduced from 6.2 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2018 to 2.6 percent of GDP in 2019, supported by increased tax revenues and strong donor inflows. Growth has remained robust at around 6 percent despite the fiscal adjustment and external shocks, including in the tourism sector. International reserves have been brought closer to prudential levels, interest rates have eased, and inflation has remained stable. 4. The unfolding global coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic is expected to have severe socioeconomic consequences and slow efforts to consolidate public finances and enhance debt sustainability as well as the pace of poverty reduction. The tourism sector, which represents roughly 10 percent of GDP and drives overall economic performance, will be affected by travel restrictions in Europe - The Gambia’s largest market. This will drag down tourism-related services and industries. Weakened remittances will delay construction activities given the importance of investment in the sector by the diaspora. Agricultural commodity exports may experience a price decline given subdued demand in the Eurozone, but on the upside, lower oil prices will improve terms of trade as The Gambia is a net oil importer. Trade and supply disruptions will also delay project implementation. 1 The commission has discovered that the former president withdrew at least US$45 million from the Central Bank for personal use. Sectoral and Institutional Context Weak tax policies and low capacity in tax administration constrain Domestic Revenue Mobilization (DRM) 5. Significant improvement in DRM is required to help manage the debt situation and to finance basic state functions, investments and services. The Gambia’s tax-to-GDP-ratio of 10.3 percent in 2018 was well below the sub-region average of 17 percent. A recent review of tax policy in The Gambia (2019 Public Expenditure Review – PER) by the World Bank estimated the structural tax gap to be between 5 and 7 percentage points of GDP. 6. Increase of the tax-to-GDP ratio is constrained by limited administrative capacity. Weaknesses in tax and customs administration compromise the GoTG’s ability to achieve revenue potential and translate policy reform into revenue streams. A 2018 assessment of the Tax Administration Diagnostic Assessment Tool (TADAT) identified several weaknesses, including: an unreliable taxpayer registration base, and weak core tax administrative systems, processes and procedures, including basic compliance. The current tax administration system does not support data analytics and the unreliability of basic taxpayer information limits a data driven approach to tax compliance. The Gambia Revenue Authority (GRA) must divert resources to manage system failures and lacks a clear overview of tax filing and payment performance, essential to effective compliance management at both the macro and case level. Tax and customs are administered using separate systems. Comparison and verification of transactions as reported in each system does not take place systematically. Meanwhile, current systems do not allow for systematic use of third-party information to detect inaccurate tax reporting. 7. Improving DRM is contingent on improved tax policies as well as strengthened capacity for tax policy design and oversight. The Gambia is struggling with flawed tax policy design, resulting for instance in too many tax exemptions. Substantial revenue potential is foregone due to ad-hoc negotiation of tax incentives, often led by line ministries and not always involving the Ministry of Finance and Economic Affairs (MoFEA) or the GRA staff. With respect to building strategic and operational capacity within MoFEA, the following functions must be strengthened: (i) revenue collection establishment and implementation; (ii) revenue forecasting and associated reform formulation, including assessment of revenue impact, efficiency (such as potential distortions and impact on the investment climate of proposed policies), and equity/distributional implications of reform; and (iii) oversight through close monitoring of drivers of revenue performance. 8. Implementing the strategic plan to address TADAT findings is expected to increase the tax-to-GDP ratio by 1.5 to 2 percent. A strategic plan to address the TADAT findings was prepared with IMF support. It contains a set of short- and medium-term actions to strengthen and modernize the basic functions and capacity of the GRA, including major investments to improve its Information and Technology (IT) systems. Experience in other developing economies suggests that moving revenue administration into the digital frontier can lead to a tax-to- GDP increase of around 1.5 to 2 percentage points (IMF 2018). These gains will derive from improved operational systems and related tax administration effectiveness. SOEs: High fiscal risk and burden, underperformance, weak oversight and corporate governance 9. SOEs are a source of high fiscal risk due to weak operational and financial performance; SOEs were the source of a major debt crisis in 2014. Since their establishment in the 1970s, SOEs’ operational and financial Page 8 of 60 The World Bank Gambia Fiscal Management Development Project (P166695) performance has been suboptimal despite several reforms implemented between the 1980s and early 2000s. Following the closure of The Gambia Divestiture Agency in 2009, SOEs’ operational and financial performance has not been monitored. Consequently, the SOE sector has markedly deteriorated, resulting in rising fiscal pressures and increasing SOE debt guarantees. In 2014, SOEs were unable to service their debts, and the GoTG had to meet SOE external financing requirements equivalent to 5 percent of GDP. SOE arrears also reached unsustainable levels, with tax arrears reaching 4.3 percent of GDP in 2014. The National Water and Electricity Company (NAWEC), the mobile operator The Gambia Telecommunications Cellular Company (GAMCEL), the fixed line operator The Gambia Telecommunications Company (GAMTEL), and The Gambia Groundnut Corporation (GGC), in particular, experienced financial challenges that have created serious macroeconomic and fiscal implications. 10. Due to their mismanagement and lack of financial discipline under the former regime, SOEs are large debtors of the GoTG. SOE resources amounting to almost 4 percent of GDP2 were misused and diverted by the previous regime from mid-2014 to 2017, thus contributing to the country’s economic fragility. A key indicator of the lack of financial discipline and management of SOE finances and SOE/Government relations is the recurring and persistent problem of ‘cross arrears.’ The most recent assessment carried out by the Department of Public Private Partnerships and Public Enterprises (DPPP&PE) in January 2018 revealed that, netting out ‘cross arrears’, the total owed by SOEs (as of January 2018) was US$17.6 million. NAWEC is the largest SOE debtor of the GoTG, owing US$53.6 million, almost 5.8 percent of GDP. Other net debtors include The Gambia Civil Aviation Authority (US$16.1 million), GAMCEL (US$14.4 million), The Gambia International Airlines (GIA) (US$1.7 million), and The Gambia Radio and Television Services (GRTS) (US$0.2 million). 11. A fragmented SOE oversight function weakens fiscal risk management and corporate governance. SOE oversight involves multiple public stakeholders with overlapping, uncoordinated roles and unclear channels of communication. Political interference in the management of SOEs is frequent. SOE Boards of Directors are perceived as merely symbolic entities; frequently incapable of fulfilling their strategic oversight role. Board members are rarely selected based on clearly defined policies or qualification criteria, and are frequently dismissed, constraining continuity and institutional memory. While SOEs are required to annually submit their audited financial statements to the National Audit Office (NAO) and the National Assembly, findings of special purpose audits3 carried out by Ernst & Young revealed accountability mechanisms to be inadequate. The Public Enterprise Committee (PEC) established within the National Assembly is required to play an extensive controlling role in collaboration with the NAO. However, the nature of information to be disclosed, whether financial or operational, is unclear and the practice appears to be imbalanced. There is a backlog in the submission of activity reports and financial statements. Institutional capacity in terms of data collection and analysis is also weak. 12. Delays in reforming GAMCEL and GAMTEL impede sector development and contribute to overall SOE underperformance, constraining growth. 13 SOEs in The Gambia are operating in essential sectors of the economy, including electricity, water, telecommunication and agriculture. Weak performance of SOEs is a significant constraint to growth. For example, the two telecom SOEs, GAMCEL and GAMTEL, have not been able to adapt to the increasingly dynamic broadband market. GAMTEL’s inadequate management of essential fiber optic backbone infrastructure is hampering growth and the transition to a full-fledged broadband market. While mobile phone penetration (voice and data) is currently around 140 percent4, mobile broadband penetration (data only) is estimated to be only around 38 percent5. GAMTEL and GAMCEL telecommunications companies are two of the SOEs that pose the most urgent risk to the State from a contingent liability perspective. Due to their weak performance and significant external debt, GAMTEL and GAMCEL follow only NAWEC in terms of financial losses incurred since 2010. GAMTEL had the second largest unbudgeted fiscal spending in 2016. Since 2015, both companies have experienced negative growth in every revenue category. In 2016, GAMTEL’s revenue was around US$2.2 million and GAMCEL’s around US$7.7 million, accounting for 15 percent and 6.5 percent respectively of total SOE sector revenues. The GoTG must service around US$58 million of telecom-related loans over the next two decades. With debt financing from the Islamic Development Bank and China EximBank, the GoTG has funded Economic Community of West Africa Network (ECOWAN8) and National Broadband Network (NBN9) investments as well as some e-government infrastructure such as a data center. The debt servicing schedules require an annual repayment of US$5 million on average in 2018-38. The Government is considering the establishment of a public-private partnership (PPP) to manage the essential fiber infrastructure whereby it could explore options to off-shoulder part of the external debt. In addition to this debt, GAMCEL’s financial statements showed commercial debt totaling US$21 million for end-2018. Together (but mostly GAMCEL), their tax arrears make up over 50 percent of total SOE tax arrears and they are the only telecom companies with tax arrears10. 14. The two companies, operating in different segments of the telecommunications market, face distinct challenges. Both operators have revenues per employee significantly lower than competitors. The mobile operator, GAMCEL, operates a sub-optimal 2G network and a 3G network with limited coverage, and has steadily been losing market share to its competitors in recent years. In 2017 GAMCEL’s share of the market stood at 7 percent (by subscriber count) and 9 percent (by revenue). Its market share has been declining since the ACE submarine cable launch in 2012, indicating that the company has not been able to transition from basic voice service provider to a 3G/4G mobile broadband service provider. GAMTEL has a larger (52 percent) market share (by revenue) in its core business as a retail fixed services provider, but this may partly be due to its preferential access to the wholesale fiber network and exclusivity over fixed-line voice telephony. Its cost structure is higher than the cost structures of its competitors, causing GAMTEL to incur losses while its smaller competitors accrue profits. For example, 50 percent of GAMTEL and GAMCEL’s revenue is directed to covering its payroll. In 2017, GAMTEL’s losses amounted to US$5.4 million, and GAMCEL’s to US$2.9 million. In 2017, GAMCEL had net liabilities totaling US$6 million. GAMCEL’s share of market revenue declined from 41 percent in 2012 to 9 percent in 2017. GAMCEL is insolvent and would require an estimated US$15-20 million to upgrade its mobile network to catch-up with other mobile operators. 15. Inefficiency and limited transparency in public procurement, lack of e-government services and gender gap 17. The Gambia’s procurement system and its overall transparency and accountability mechanisms require strengthening. The 2015-2016 Gambia Public Procurement Agency (GPPA) annual report identified several challenges: (i) limited competitive procurement (restricted tenders and single sourcing represented 37 percent and 28 percent respectively of total value of tenders in 2016); (ii) noncompliance with the public procurement framework; and (iii) insufficient transparency (award of tenders is not published). The control and regulation functions could be further segregated to promote a more transparent and competitive procurement system . A shortage of qualified procurement specialists and the absence of an integrated electronic procurement system also create inefficiencies and lead to high transaction costs. 18. The GoTG intends to leverage digital technologies to improve service delivery. Currently, the use of online banking services and e-commerce services is nascent. Meanwhile, an e-Government strategy is being developed by the Ministry of Information and Communication Infrastructures (MoICI). Addressing Cyber Security12, data protection and data privacy issues will be critical to implementation of a Digital Government Strategy. In terms of Cyber Security, various efforts need to be pooled together in a more cohesive strategy that helps to govern the safe use of digital technologies by the Government and citizens. Introducing e-government services for citizens will require end-to-end security measures certified by third parties, to build citizen trust. 19. Women are underrepresented in corporate/SOE leadership positions despite constituting over half of the country’s population. The 2018 Global Gender Gap Analysis Index13 found that women in The Gambia have reached 68 percent parity overall, leaving a 32 percent gap mainly across four areas: economic participation and opportunity, educational attainment, health and survival, and political empowerment. Leadership gap is the widest gap with only 18 percent of ministerial positions and 34 percent of managerial positions held by women. The relatively low participation of women in leadership and in community and high-level decision making is to an extent due to lower educational attainment, skills and experience, but this itself is a result of social, cultural and economic barriers that inhibit women’s role within The Gambia’s traditionally patriarchal society. C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 20. The proposed Fiscal Management Development Project (FMDP) is consistent and aligned with the strategic objectives of the Government’s National Development Plan (NDP). II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Development Objective PDO Statement 24. The Project Development Objective is to increase fiscal space and improve effectiveness of public resources management. PDO Level Indicators 25. The PDO-level results indicators are as follows: • PDO Indicator 1: Value Added Tax (VAT) Gross compliance ratio. • PDO Indicator 2: Aggregate annual SOEs report prepared and published by the centralized oversight agency (SOE Commission). • PDO Indicator 3: Share of competitive biddings for procuring organizations using electronic Government Procurement (e-GP). B. Project Components 26. The proposed five-year operation financed by IDA grant seeks to improve the Government’s effectiveness in fiscal management for better public service delivery. This will be achieved by: (a) enhancing the GRA’s compliance management, revenue risk analysis, and enforcement capacity, for broadening the tax base and improving voluntary compliance; (b) introducing electronic Government procurement (e-GP); and (c) improving Page 12 of 60 The World Bank Gambia Fiscal Management Development Project (P166695) financial and operational performance of SOEs and reducing the telecom-related fiscal burden. The operation is built around two interlinked components that seek to address core challenges currently hampering fiscal management and service delivery. Component 1 aims to increase fiscal space for public services and implementing reforms of The Gambia procurement system for more efficient and better-quality procurement. Component 2 provides incentives for achieving results in cost-cutting measures for fiscal risk management and performance monitoring of SOEs, as well as technical assistance (TA) to reinforce the Government’s oversight and public accountability of the SOE sector and critical reforms to enhance financial viability of SOEs in the telecommunications sector. Component 1: Strengthening revenue collection and public procurement processes (US$17 million equivalent) 27. This component seeks to increase fiscal space, the efficiency of public expenditure and support business continuity in this COVID-19 pandemic. Support will be provided to MoFEA to strengthen capacity for tax policy formulation and implementation. GRA will be supported to improve its performance on revenue collection, tax compliance and taxpayer services. GPPA will also be supported to promote increased competition in public procurement through the implementation and adoption of electronic Government Procurement. Online tax services and e-procurement will prepare to support the business continuity in future situations similar to this COVID-19 pandemic. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 62. Strong and sustained counterpart commitment (underpinned by a clear strategy) is a pre-requisite to translating reforms into implementation but can prove insufficient in the absence of reinforcing incentive structures. Experience in preparing both DPFs and investment lending highlights the importance of political commitment and having reform champions to ensure successful and sustainable reforms. For example, implementation of the ongoing The Gambia: Integrated Financial Management Project (IFMIS, P117275 slowed down for a period partly because of a lack of ownership and technical capacity. Reforms intended by that project were also constrained by absence of a real strategy to guide implementation, and by a lack of adequate incentives. The FMDP has taken these lessons into account by focusing on areas with clear reform traction (and strategies) and by working closely with reform champions during project design, providing a clear framework that will be sustained moving forward into project implementation. Prospects for timely and successful reform implementation will be further buttressed through the performance-based element of the Project, providing the incentive structure for reform implementation. 63. Counterpart capacity is central to reform implementation in fragile settings, especially where negotiations with the private sector and around establishing PPP arrangements are involved. Previous DPF and IPF experiences highlight the importance of capacity building in fragile institutional environments, especially within highly specialized and nascent areas. In particular, the design of the FMDP, which includes targeted focus on capability building and TA, reflects lessons learned from previous attempts at partial privatization of GAMTEL. These efforts resulted in a highly underperforming SOE, due largely to an inability to structure and negotiate a balanced deal in the absence of support from specialized TA. In this regard, FMDP is providing resources for technical and legal advice to help guide and support the GoTG in undertaking these transactions moving forward. 64. Flexible design of PPP may mitigate resistance of the Government to divest and help to adapt to changing market conditions. PPPs in the telecom sector, often led by private consortia, have attracted significant private capital, and have been completed relatively faster. These have tended to be more successful in structuring partnerships between the public and the private sectors where equity ownership of both parties is involved, including through the use of SPVs that entrust operation to the private sector. Nonetheless, embedding flexibility in the PPP design would allow for changes in shareholdings, for easy on-boarding of new investors, and access to infrastructure capacity. This would help to adequately assuage Government’s concerns about equity ownership and fair competition. Clear definition of regulatory conditions can further help to avert issues around established telecom operators not being willing to pay again as ‘shareholders of a new operator’, and initial investors preventing newcomers from directly accessing the ACE capacity, etc. The design of the project has considered these lessons learned. The project also includes a technical assistance component that will support the GoTG to develop transparent governance frameworks and conclude robust transaction agreements, including clear rules of engagement. Effective implementation of the new systems, procedures developed under the FMPD will remain critical to sustaining fiscal discipline and efficient management of public resources. Experience from previous reforms in The Gambia indicates that risks, in terms of loss of momentum and reversal of overall gains, are inherent in medium and long-term structural and institutional reforms. These risks are more so significant in light of the presidential elections planned for the end of 2021. However, against the backdrop of recent external and domestic shocks, The Gambian authorities have acknowledged the importance of planned institutional changes to achieve the GoTG’s broader macroeconomic stabilization and sustainability goals. The Gambia’s need to prioritize structural fiscal management reforms is heightened by its status as a low-income country without access to international capital markets. In this regard, planned reforms are expected to help encourage capital flows by reducing perceived and inherent international investor risks 72. The SOE legal and institutional framework is being revised to incorporate key principles of good corporate governance and help the Government make the evolution sustainable and irreversible beyond the transition period. The SOE Act is being updated to include a clear definition of SOEs (including business purpose). The SOE Act will outline a complete corporate regime for SOEs that sets a clear distinction between shareholders and board/management powers (and guarantees managerial autonomy, free from political interference), and that separates ownership functions from managerial functions. The SOE Act will set clear reporting, disclosure and performance monitoring mechanisms. In addition, the institutional framework will be reinforced to organize SOEs as a share corporation with ownership rights centralized to simplify decision-making and communication, and to make the oversight function more effective. Projected improvement in revenue collection is expected to cumulatively reach 2.5 percent of GDP over ten years – overall the net present value of cash flows is expected to reach US$26 million over this period. Against this background, the projected improvements in revenue collection are considered achievable and realistic relative to achievements in comparable jurisdictions. The DRM component will support the effective implementation of the fourth corporate strategic plan of GRA for the period 2020-2024. The overall risk for achieving the PDO is high given the limited implementation capacity in the public sector and the overall governance fragility. The key risks and proposed mitigation measures are discussed below. 96. Political and governance risk is high. While political and governance risks in The Gambia are high, the new Government has made progress with the introduction of a fiscal stabilization program and reform measures that enhance transparency and accountability in public sector procedures and management. The Government commitment to finalize the SOEs reforms in the telecommunications sector will need to be sustained, given difficult decisions on restructuring of GAMTEL/GAMCEL based on the market test that could conclude on private sector interest for only some of the telecom SOEs’ assets. In addition, the COVID 19 pandemic may delay the restructuring process. Political interference remains a substantial risk to the success of this operation. To mitigate this risk, the World Bank assisted the authorities in developing a communication strategy and plan to clearly explain, to the public and all stakeholders, the rationale, objectives, and scope of the planned restructurings. 97. Macroeconomic risk is high. The Gambia has gone through a difficult and prolonged process of establishing a positive track record on macroeconomic performance and securing financial support and debt relief needed to address its unsustainable debt. While the economic recovery strengthened further in 2019, external factors could still negatively impact the macroeconomic path and derail reforms. A prolonged global contraction (L-shaped recovery, for instance) particularly in Europe and a widespread domestic COVID-19 outbreak could have a severe impact on the economy and would delay the V-shaped recovery envisaged for The Gambia in 2021. It may also Page 27 of 60 The World Bank Gambia Fiscal Management Development Project (P166695) increase pressure on external and fiscal balances over the medium-term. DRM is highly sensitive to economic activity and any shocks are likely to limit DRM efforts which could have an adverse implication for the project’s development objectives. Macroeconomic imbalances could further reduce the risk appetite of potential investors and delay SOE related reforms including privatization of telecom SOEs. This risk is mitigated by the macroeconomic reform program undertaken by the Government with support from development partners, including the World Bank financed DPF series and a IMF program. 98. Sectoral strategies and policies risk is substantial. Policy reform has been limited by the limited capacity of actors to commit and their readiness to cooperate and coordinate actions to achieve socially desirable goals. The public sector lacks human resources and an effective administration which are imperative for the delivery of public services. Commitment challenges also reflect the weak capacity of the administration and volatility in announcing and implementing reforms. Coordination challenges include fragmentation of governance leading to silo effects, and lack of coherence. Finally, the ongoing constitutional review process may impact the enactment of the proposed SOE Act into law and delay the implementation of some activities. This risk is mitigated by the SPDD which is coordinating the decision-making process and monitoring the implementation progress of the selected priority reforms under the Office of the President. In addition, broad consultations with policy makers and key stakeholders are being conducted during the design stage of programs and policies and have become the norm. 99. Technical design risk is substantial. The Project involves wide-ranging reforms covering different entities with different mandates. In addition, several new business and telecom/ICT related risks are introduced into the environment. Lack of substantive involvement of key stakeholders in the implementation of the design may undermine the Government’s ability to complete the implementation on time. To mitigate this risk, the Project Steering Committee will meet regularly to ensure that implementation issues are timely discussed, and consensus is built around options to resolve emergent bottlenecks and challenges. 100. Institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability risk is substantial. Significant personnel and organizational structure changes are also associated with reengineering or redesigning business processes. It is challenging to embrace a tightly integrated environment when different business processes have existed among business units for so long. The level of user acceptance of the system has a significant influence on its success. To mitigate this risk, a change management strategy will be developed, and its implementation will help alleviate uncertainties associated with structural changes. Considerable training will be included as part of project activities to support users to be more diligent and efficient in the performance of their day-to-day duties. An operational management unit will be established within GRA comprising of a project manager and tax experts to ensure adequate vendor management. A Quality Assurance firm will be recruited to strengthen the quality of systems and proper customization prior to the purchase of the tax system. 101. Fiduciary risk is substantial given limited institutional capacity and non-compliance with rules and regulations. Limited competition for international procurement activities due to the current COVID-19 sanitary situation. GoTG import restrictions in place for goods/service providers/consultants/contractors from certain countries; constraints in institutional and implementing capacity in borrowing countries (quarantines). This risk is mitigated by the recruitment of a dedicated Financial Management Officer and a dedicated Procurement Officer in the DPPP&PE and the GPPA’s oversight of procurement activities. Furthermore, the Internal Audit Directorate of MoEFA will ensure internal audit functions for the project. The implementation and adoption of electronic Government Procurement, financed by the project, will contribute to increased competition and transparency in public procurement. Finally, it is expected that with implementation of project activities, overall institutional capacity will be gradually strengthened, and thus contribute to mitigating the fiduciary risks. In addition, given the emergency context with COVID-19, the World Bank will adapt the implementation support to the current sanitary sanitation by establishing virtual communication channel with project implementation teams to keep engaged on issues impacting performance, compliance and financial reporting. In addition, specific procurement approach such Page 28 of 60 The World Bank Gambia Fiscal Management Development Project (P166695) as limited/restricted approach will be used with the support of the World Bank in facilitating access to certain procurement needs including electronic submission for identified activities. 102. Stakeholder risk is substantial due to difficulties to coordinate a large number of counterparts with expanded activities to line ministries and agencies. The lack of ownership by stakeholders and limited involvement of line ministries in the implementation of the project may undermine the ability of the Government to complete the implementation on time. To mitigate this risk, a Project Steering Committee (PSC) will be established and chaired by the PS of MoFEA and include representatives from key stakeholders to provide strategic guidance and oversight of the project and ensure robust stakeholder coordination. . The proposed five-year operation financed by IDA grant seeks to improve the Government’s effectiveness in fiscal management and service delivery. This will be achieved by: (a) enhancing GRA’s compliance management, revenue risk analysis, and enforcement capacity, for broadening the tax base and improving voluntary compliance; (b) introducing electronic Government procurement (e-GP) and leveraging on technology to promote transparency and public expenditure efficiency; and (c) improving financial and operational performance of SOEs; through two interlinked components, the project seeks to address core challenges currently hampering fiscal management and service delivery. Component 1 aims to increase fiscal space and public procurement efficiency. Component 2 provides incentives for achieving cost-cutting results in the area of fiscal risk management and performance monitoring of SOEs. It also provides technical assistance (TA) to reinforce the Government’s oversight and public accountability of the SOE sector and critical reforms to enhance financial viability of SOEs in the telecommunications sector. Component 1: Strengthening revenue collection and public procurement processes (US$17 million equivalent) 2. This component seeks to increase fiscal space, public expenditure efficiency and business continuity in this COVID pandemic. Support will be provided to MoFEA to strengthen capacity for tax policy formulation and its implementation. GRA will be supported to improve its performance on revenue collection, tax compliance and taxpayer services. GPPA will also be supported to promote increased competition in public procurement through the implementation and adoption of e-GP. Online tax services and e-procurement will support the business continuity in this COVID pandemic. Sub-component 1.1: Strengthening the Gambia Revenue Authority’s tax administration processes and digital transformation (US$12 million equivalent) 3. Objective: This sub-component aims to enhance the GRA’s operational effectiveness and achieve digital transformation, including through widespread adoption of electronic filing (e-filing) and electronic payments (e- payments) to improve taxpayer services and transparency. It will also support comprehensive tax compliance risk management processes based on international good practices. 4. Status: The GRA’s current operational effectiveness limits the GoTG’s capacity to generate revenues from several sources. The current tax administration system does not support data analytics and the unreliability of basic taxpayer information limits a data driven approach to tax compliance. GRA must currently divert resources to manage system failures and lacks a clear overview of tax filing and payment performance that is essential to effective compliance management at both macro and case level. Tax and customs are administered using separate systems. Comparison and verification of transactions as reported in each system does not take place systematically. It is also not currently possible to make systematic use of third-party information to detect inaccurate tax reporting. Key institutional capabilities, such as the comprehensive use of standard operating procedures and change control processes do not exist. The governance of the compliance activity is weak and there is no process for using the results of compliance activities to inform the design of future programs. The status of Gambia Tax Management System (GAMTAXNET) within GRA is uncertain and the scale of improvement required is overwhelming. While it is possible to stabilize the system in the short-term, a new system needs to be procured and implemented to allow for a significant improvement in the GRA’s operational effectiveness. In addition, core Page 39 of 60 The World Bank Gambia Fiscal Management Development Project (P166695) functionality on filing and payments’ compliance needs to be strengthened and e-filing and e-payments implemented. 5. Activities to be financed: The sub-component will support: (i) a Business Process Reengineering (BPR) that covers all the GRA’s processes including study tours, and provision of TA in the preparation of International Competition Bid (ICB) documents; (ii) the selection and implementation of a new Integrated Tax Management System (ITAS) using a Commercial Off-The Shelf (COTS) package. This will include the parametrization and customization of the COTS to the needs of GRA using a turnkey solution17 as an implementation strategy, and Data warehouse and Business Intelligence Systems including Mobile payment and filing option for micro and small taxpayers; (iii) Advisory services and Quality Assurance for effective implementation of the system; (iv) Training and capacity building to ensure adoption of the system by both GRA officers and taxpayers; (v) TA to develop and implement Information Technology (IT) governance policies, standards and procedures to ensure that adequate IT service management and exploitation are in place (IT Service Management and Transition, IT change management, etc.); (vi) TA on integration/interfacing of the new ITAS system with existing revenue and PFM systems such as ASYCUDA World, IFMIS and other relevant applications for data analytics and matching; and (vii) support on strategic compliance, risk management, and specific training on sector and compliance audit techniques. TA will be financed to ensure the sustenance of improvements to processes and ways of working resulting from the system development delivered by the BPR and system implementation. This TA will help embed management structures and disciplines to maintain the integrity of standard operating procedures. This element of the project will also address the current weaknesses in the governance of compliance activities including audits. TA will also help the GRA develop compliance campaigns focused on key sectors, such as the hotel and tourism sector and the liberal professions. A separate campaign will be supported to increase registration of businesses entitled to use the informal sector tax scheme. 6. Contribution to COVID-19 response: The digital transformation related activities that will be supported under the project will contribute to build more resilience into revenue/tax administration functions which are key to maintain the government effectiveness in this COVID 19 pandemic. After the first phase of emergency response, The GoTG will be focusing on the recovery phase and will in due course be considering public administration reforms to make their response and service delivery more resilient. This would require a fundamental shift away from face to face interactions towards more efficient (and less susceptible to corruption) models of government operations, service delivery and interactions with citizens, which include options for digitization of services to citizens and businesses. Digitizing tax administration processes through the implementation of an Integrated Tax Administration System (ITAS) along with online services such as e-Declaration, e-Filing and e-payments for citizens and businesses will facilitate the recovery phase and help the government to maintain key services. 7. Expected outcomes: As a result of administrative and policy changes, the Value-Added Tax (VAT) gross compliance ratio is expected to increase from 26 percent to 40 percent. The GRA will also deploy 15 percent of its operational staff for field audits to improve compliance management. The new tax administration system (once effective) will deliver online 24/7 services to taxpayers and allow for e-filing, e-payment and mobile filing and payment. 17 single contract for both hardware, software and implementation services. Objective: This sub-component aims to strengthen tax policy formulation and the overall policy regime for revenue collection. The project will support the establishment and operationalization of an effective policy unit at the MoFEA. 9. Status: A recent review of tax policy in The Gambia (2019 PER) by the World Bank confirmed that (i) strategic capacity to establish and implement revenue mobilization targets at the MoEFA is limited; (ii) basic capacity, including tools and data, is low for common tasks such as revenue forecasting, and preparation of specific tax reform options, including assessment of revenue impact, efficiency (such as distortion with regard to the impact on the investment climate), and equity/distributional implications of reform; and (iii) monitoring of drivers behind (low) revenue performance is irregular and insufficient. Furthermore, the coordination of policy across government institutions needs to be strengthened. In the context of the current negotiations of tax treaties and new hydrocarbon projects, limited coordination and insufficient analytical underpinning of tax policy measures carries an elevated risk of substantial revenue losses. 10. Activities to be financed: The sub-component will support (i) the development of tools and techniques and oversight on all tax sources, and the implementation of core tasks within the tax policy unit, such as revenue forecasting, revenue impact assessments, distributional analysis, etc. ; and (ii) analytical review of specific tax policy challenges that will aim to assist the tax policy unit to determine priorities and develop steps to implement policy reforms. Specific outputs that will be supported include, (amongst others), a review of fiscal incentive policy. This would cover both direct and indirect tax instruments, with a view to consolidating all tax instruments in the tax code and meeting transparency and consolidation targets formulated by the GoTG as part of the wider policy dialogue with the World Bank. Moreover, TA in developing sector tax policy initiatives, including on tourism will be financed. 11. Expected outcomes: A Tax Expenditure Policy will be adopted to rationalize tax incentives. By the end of the project, the Tax Policy Development Unit is expected to possess requisite technical capacity to lead the policy formulation process effectively and to have developed the analytical underpinnings necessary to develop policy priorities and provide specific recommendations to strengthen the national tax policy framework and to effectively monitor the GRA’s performance. Sub-component 2.1: Fiscal Risk Assessment and Performance Monitoring (US$6 million equivalent) 22. Objective: This sub-component aims to support the operationalization of an active and informed oversight entity for SOEs. An adequate institutional framework is required to establish a Centralized SOE Oversight Agency that will serve to exercise the State ownership rights and manage the relationship with SOEs. While several models exist, a centralized entity, wherein various stakeholders may be represented is deemed the best fit to avoid fragmentation of powers and responsibilities. 23. Status: The special audit of seven SOEs revealed that Government interference through executive directives continues. SOEs are influenced by requests for funding or sponsorship for activities unrelated to their business, through instructions to, for example, appoint individuals and recruit surplus personnel and disband the entire Board. It is important to establish robust mechanisms to monitor SOEs performance because of their role in the economy including in the delivery of essential public services and to avoid public financial liabilities. To that end, the GoTG is committed to establish a strong autonomous Centralized SOE Oversight Agency to improve SOE performance monitoring. Performance monitoring is a key ownership function of the State as the owner, that can facilitate both financial and non-financial improvements. A strong performance-monitoring regime will set objectives and targets that provide clarity to SOE boards and management on the expectations of the Government. Where non-commercial goals are set by the Government, those should also be disclosed, and compensation measures should be provided. There is a need for clear goals, accompanied by measurement and accountability for results. There is also a need for the establishment of a framework in which the SOE board can adopt and execute implementation strategies with an appropriate degree of autonomy, while providing shareholders with sufficient assurance that SOEs will be held accountable for their performance. 24. Contribution to COVID-19 response: An assessment of fiscal implications and costs related to COVID 19 and an emergency contingency plan and policies for SOEs will be supported under the project to build more resilience into fiscal management and oversight functions of SOEs which are key to maintain the government effectiveness in this COVID 19 pandemic. 25. Activities to be financed: This sub-component will support: (i) the definition of an ownership policy that outlines the objectives of State ownership, as well as expectations for individual companies in terms of their performance and adherence to general corporate governance principles; (ii) the introduction of a performance based management through supporting the preparation of guidelines, and the preparation of strategic plans and performance contracts for selected SOEs; and (iii) the preparation of comprehensive and analytical annual reports on the entire SOE portfolio by the central unit of SOE, including analysis of their financial and operational performance, and contributions to the economy through payment of taxes and dividends. The overall residual risk rating is substantial. Procurement arrangements The Gambia public procurement institutional set-up 24. The Gambia Public Procurement is characterized by many layers and key actors that involve the Presidency, the Cabinet, MoFEA, GPPA, the Complaints Review Board (CRB), the National Audit office (NAO) and the Procuring organizations (POs), and the private sector bidders. 25. The legislative and regulatory framework provides for oversight functions to be carried out by GPPA, and the CRB. 26. The Gambia Public Procurement Authority, apart from its regulatory role, performs both the control and assurance function for all contracts; through ex-ante and post reviews of the procurement proceedings; including an adjudicatory role by virtue of its membership to the Complaints Review Board. 27. The Complaints Review Board as provided for in the Act is to promote and uphold fairness in the public procurement system through judicious and impartial adjudication of matters arising from disputed procurement proceedings. The CRB resolves disputes arising from bidders who claim to have suffered or risk suffering, loss or damage due to the breach of a duty imposed on a Procuring Organization by the Procurement Law. CRB is already established as an autonomous body composed of seven members plus secretariat to provide administrative services. Its membership includes, among others; the PS MoFEA, Director of Procurement Cadre and GPPA. Procurement under the Project 28.
