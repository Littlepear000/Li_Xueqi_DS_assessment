STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Thailand has made remarkable progress in social and economic development and transitioned from low-income to upper-middle-income status in less than a generation. Thailand moved from a low-income, agrarian economy in the 1970s and 80s to one that is modern, industrialized, and export-driven. But growth decelerated to an average of 2.6 percent annually since 2010 (expected 2.0 percent in 2025), with sluggish investment, a stalling shift away from low- productivity agriculture, and deepening demographic challenges including a rapidly aging population. Thailand is a country where high inequality is a key constraint to sustainable and inclusive growth. Thailand ranked as the 13th most unequal out of 63 countries1. Urbanization has been heavily focused on Bangkok - one of the world’s most mono-centric cities - and contributed to this inequality. Rural areas, especially in the north, northeast and south (the location of the proposed project), have significantly higher vulnerability but also offer opportunities for inclusive growth. Thailand’s next development phase will require new drivers of growth and job creation that unlock competitiveness, build a future- economy ready workforce, and invest in high-quality and resilient infrastructure. 2. Over the last decade, the World Bank engagement with Thailand has been anchored in strong analytics and technical assistance, and the Bank is now in a position, after a hiatus of 15 years, to partner with Thailand on an International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD) program. The World Bank Group (WBG) has been Thailand’s partner of choice for analytics, with technical assistance and Reimbursable Advisory Services. The International Finance Corporation (IFC) has a US$1.58 billion investment program. The Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA) does not have current exposure. Over the past two years, the Bank has engaged with Thailand to reopen a lending program to support Thailand’s next phase of development. The WBG’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF) FY2019-2022 (Report No. 125234-TH) was extended in January 2023 for an additional two years, through June 30, 2024, following the Performance and Learning Review (Report No. 170788-TH). The project will support CPF Objective 3—Strengthening the quality of infrastructure investments—by improving transport connectivity and enhancing climate resilience for access to Lanta Island and across Songkhla Lake. Discussions are underway for a new CPF. This year, for the first time, Thailand included the Bank in its formal development financing process, resulting in a firm and selective pipeline in resilient transport, flood protection and water management, and low carbon cities. The proposed project would be the first lending operation as part of said pipeline. 3. Thailand's tourism sector is an economic driver contributing about 20 percent of Gross Domestic Product, providing 4.3 million jobs and more than 11 percent of total employment. Thailand’s beaches and islands are among the most sought-after travel destinations in the world. More than 35.5 million tourists visited Thailand in 2024, spending more than THB 157 billion (US$4.7 billion), of which 42 percent was spent in the Southern region of Thailand. Songkhla and Krabi provinces are among the top five tourist destinations in the South. The bridges will serve as enabling conditions towards enhanced eco-tourism and local area development. This could potentially stimulate Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) in the area for further revenue generating activities. During project implementation, discussions will continue to facilitate growth of SMEs to boost tourism opportunities to support job creation in the local economy. 4. Transport connectivity and mobility are proven catalysts for tourism and local economic development, jobs, and livelihood. It makes destinations more accessible by reducing travel time and costs. This increased mobility stimulates local economies and drives job creation—especially in rural and underserved areas —where tourism can become a vital engine of sustainable growth. The proposed Lanta and Songkhla Lake Bridges, which would reduce the travel distance and associated costs by approximately 90 percent, can attract an additional 4 percent more tourists (approximately 120,000 tourists) per year. Lower transport costs through two bridges will also enable the addition of approximately 25,000 jobs around the area, equivalent to a 9 1 World Bank (2023). Thailand's diverse natural resources and significant biodiversity assets are a critical part of its tourist attractions . In addition to the country’s most celebrated species, including the Indochinese tiger and the Asian elephant, the Southern region boasts a wide array of both terrestrial and marine flagship species, rich ecosystems and natural landscapes. Rapid economic development and increased human activities in the Southern region have intensified pressure on natural resources, calling for a more balanced management of development and conservation tradeoffs. 6. Thailand is susceptible to many natural hazards, including floods, tsunamis, storms, droughts, landslides, and forest fires. Droughts, storms, and floods present the greatest risks, threatening sustained economic growth. The frequency of natural disasters has increased over time. Resilient infrastructure and all-season accessibility will be crucial in ensuring that economic activities can be sustained and the well-being of the local population improved. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 7. Thailand’s Transport Development Strategy 2018-2037 serves as the country’s comprehensive plan for the transport sector, outlining key objectives and priorities. There are three guiding pillars: (i) green and safe transport, (ii) transport efficiency, and (iii) inclusive transport. The first phase of the strategy (2018-2022) focused on addressing critical transport issues, missing links, and bottlenecks for the main transport corridors as well as for lower income groups. The second phase (2023-2027) continues the efforts of Phase 1 with greater emphasis on transport safety, logistics development with a focus on economic corridors, urban public transport in large cities, rail transport development, transit-oriented development (TOD) and improving access. The third and fourth phases continue to ensure accessibility, urban transport, TOD, energy efficiency of transport services and their improved quality through innovation and technology. Enhancing transport and logistics connectivity has also been a development priority both at national and provincial levels. Thailand’s long-term National Strategy (2018-37) emphasizes transport infrastructure and logistics development as key development pillars to support regional trade, urban development, tourism, and agricultural production. 8. The Department of Rural Roads’ (DRR) development strategy, including the proposed bridges for Lanta Islands and Songkhla Lake, aims to resolve transport bottlenecks, improve last-mile connections and resilient accessibility, and support tourism and economic development of the Southern region. The poverty rate in the Southern region is around 9.4 percent, compared to the national average of 4.9 percent in 2024. Phatthalung province’s poverty rate is 15.7 percent - among the top 10 provinces with the highest poverty rate in Thailand. The Songkhla Lake Bridge (7km) aims to address a missing transport link around Songkhla Lake. The proposed bridge will shorten the travel distance from 80km to 8km – connecting the two sides of the lake. This will directly benefit approximately 308,000 people in Phatthalung and Songkhla provinces on both sides of the lake. In addition, the bridge will bring efficiency gains to the transport network in the area, including economic centers such as Hat Yai City (with a population of over 400,000) and Songkhla Deep Sea Port (which handles approximately 1.8 million tons of cargo annually). The Lanta Bridge will connect the Lanta Noi and Lanta Yai islands to Krabi province’s mainland via a 2-km bridge over the sea. The bridge – as the last-mile connectivity – will provide improved and resilient transport accessibility for the local population, tourism activities, waste management, and utilities for the islands. The island is currently served by limited and inefficient ferry operation, which is the only means of public access to the island. This project represents a forward-looking approach to sustainable territorial development, leveraging strategic infrastructure to unlock the region’s economic potential. By constructing two key bridges that connect high-potential tourism zones to the national network, the investment reduces transport costs, enhances connectivity, and serves as a catalyst for inclusive, job-rich, and sustainable territorial development. Figure 1 shows the location of both bridges. 9. Songkhla Lake is the largest natural freshwater body in the country, and home to a unique ecosystem. The lake is recognized as a Key Biodiversity Area of international significance by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN), and as an Important Bird Area by Birdlife International. The lake supports economic activities related to artisanal fisheries, an important source of income for the local economy – a registry of fishers and fishing groups to date includes over 6,000 fishers and Page 2 The World Bank Thailand Resilient Transport Connectivity and Irrawaddy Dolphin Conservation Project PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT (P509460) 116 groups in the whole Songkhla Lake, with more yet to be registered. In addition, the upper part of the lake – called Thale Luang – hosts an isolated sub-population of Irrawaddy dolphins (Orcaella brevirostris). Irrawaddy dolphins are found in marine, brackish, and freshwater habitats in Southeast Asia. The species as a whole is considered endangered in every habitat throughout its range,2,3 with the freshwater populations considered to be at greatest risk.4 The Thale Luang sub-population in Thailand has followed a similar trend as other isolated, freshwater groups: the sub-population sharply declined from more than 100 dolphins in the 1990s to an estimated 14-20 in 2022, and is thought to have continued to decline since.5 Fishing gear entanglement has been identified as the largest cause of mortality.6 The sub-population has been classified as Critically Endangered by IUCN. 10. The construction and operation of the Songkhla Lake Bridge are not expected to generate significant impacts in the Irrawaddy dolphin sub-population in Thale Luang, given the distance from the bridge alignment to the dolphin core zone and the mitigation measures that will be implemented. The steep decline of the sub-population over the past decades, however, suggests that, in the absence of urgent intervention, this sub-population may no longer be viable in the near future. The proposed bridge construction provides an opportunity to support the Thai Government, regional institutions and local communities in their aspiration to better manage and conserve the Thale Luang ecosystem and its Irrawaddy dolphin population. The World Bank’s convening power serves as a catalyst for convening stakeholders and global expertise (see box 1), as well as mobilizing essential grant resources for biodiversity conservation from international donors. A 2023 Expert Roundtable consultation7 confirmed the momentum and interest by public and non-government stakeholders to drive conservation efforts of the Irrawaddy dolphin in Thale Luang and confirmed that bridge construction-related impacts can be adequately managed. Recommendations by the Roundtable for long-term dolphin conservation included better understanding mortality and conservation threats, eliminating fishing-gear entanglement and other bycatch related threats, and implementing mitigation measures to counter and ringfence potential impacts from bridge construction.8 The Expert Roundtable acknowledged the scarcity of high-quality dolphin population information in Thale Luang and stressed the need to adapt conservation strategies and better tailor responses as more information becomes available. The Thai Government has implemented several conservation efforts targeting Irrawaddy dolphins and their surrounding ecosystems around Thale Luang in the past. Key initiatives included a buy-back program for giant catfish fishing nets in 2014 and the promotion of fishing community conservation efforts by the Department of Fisheries (DOF), which coincided with reduced dolphin mortality. Coordination and implementation of these activities, however, has been challenging due to complex and fragmented responsibilities from the Department of Marine and Coastal Resources (DMCR), the Department of Natural Parks, Wildlife and Plant Conservation (DNP), and DOF, together with limited budgets and data scarcity.9 12. The Government is intensifying efforts to address the situation and remedy past shortcomings. To overcome fragmentation and coordination challenges, a comprehensive, long-term strategy for the conservation of Irrawaddy dolphins in Thale Luang was prepared and approved in 2023. Jointly developed by DMCR, DNP and DOF, it sets out short-, medium-, and long-term measures to minimize threats to the dolphins and their habitat, promote alternative livelihoods, advance biological and ecological research, and strengthen community engagement and outreach. An action plan for the initial five years of implementation –the Conservation Plan for Irrawaddy Dolphins in Songkhla Lake 2024-2028, or “Dolphin Conservation Plan” (DCP) – has been prepared and was endorsed by Cabinet in February 2025. The endorsement includes approval of up to US$8.3 million in national financing for DCP implementation, thus addressing past budget constraints. The DCP is expected to be implemented through close collaboration among DMCR, DNP, DOF, DRR, and the Office of Natural Resources and Office of Natural Resources and Environmental Policy and Planning (ONEP). Further and to help address coordination challenges, these agencies signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) in November 2023 to facilitate the process and provide a framework for interagency coordination during implementation. Lastly, another important development is the recent approval by DNP of new zoning regulations for permitted fishing gear inside Thale Luang. When effectively enforced, the new zoning is expected to significantly reduce dolphin entanglement risks from fishing gear. 13. The bridge would replace the ferry service, which is currently the only mode of transportation to Lanta Islands from the mainland and vice versa. The ferry’s limitation is the small pier, which results in long queues during the peak season (October-April). The bridge will also address the demand for reliable and consistent emergency transportation to mainland medical services which cannot be met by the ferry service during off-hours (approximately 10:00 PM – 6:00 AM), and during low tide or hazardous wind conditions. Each lane is 3.75m wide, separated by a painted median (1m width) and road shoulders on Page 12 The World Bank Thailand Resilient Transport Connectivity and Irrawaddy Dolphin Conservation Project PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT (P509460) each side (2.5m width) giving a total width of 13.5 m. The Lanta Bridge includes three bends to pass around the small Koh Pling Island. The bridge crosses the Khlong Chong Laad (110m width) close to the Koh Klang side and a broad shallow area between Koh Pling and the Koh Lanta Noi side. The bridge design will comprise an extradosed bridge (prestressed box girder and cable- stayed bridge) over the Khlong Chong Laad and a balanced cantilever bridge (combination of supported structure and continuous structure) over the shallow sea. The construction of the Lanta Bridge requires a period of three years. 43. The extradosed bridge upper structure will be cast in situ with a prestressed box girder to hold the cable suspending the superstructure to the pylon. The Balanced Cantilever Bridge will extend 875m with 13 piers. The height of the bridge clearance is 3 to 8 m. The piers will be based on a single column with a 2.5-meter depth and 6.2-meter width. The approach and end bridge pier structures will consist of precast box girders that require four bored piles each. Fifteen approach piers will be built on the Koh Klang Sub-district to support 280m of the bridge. Eight piers will be built on the Koh Lanta Noi side supporting 140m of the bridge. The bridge reflective road studs will be embedded into the road surface at 12-meter intervals to clearly designate the traffic lanes. U-turn points will be incorporated on each side of the bridge with sufficient size to allow all types of single unit trucks to turn. 44. The bridge is designed to the following standards: (i) American Association of State Highway and Transport Officials (AASHTO) Guide Specification for LRFD (Load and Resistance Factor Design) Seismic Bridge Design 2nd Edition, 2011; (ii) Standards of the Engineering Institute of Thailand; and (iii) Department of Public Works and Town & Country Planning (DPT) Standard 1301/1302- 61 on the Design of Buildings to withstand Seismic Waves. 45. The Songkhla Lake Bridge will provide all-season connectivity across Songkhla Lake, which cuts the travel distance from 80km to 8km. The Songkhla Lake Bridge will serve as a landmark and is designed as an extradosed bridge structure. The bridge comprises two main structures, namely the connecting (box girder) bridge and the central main (cable-stayed) bridge with a higher vertical clearance of 18m above the high tide to accommodate the navigation of large vessels. The connecting bridge is based on a precast segmental box-girder bridge design. The length of the bridge between each pier structure is 40m. The substructure of the bridge is comprised of large-bored piles, bridge pier structures, and cast-in-place concrete pillars. The construction of the beam of the superstructure is done using a launching girder system in a span-by-span method. The main bridge will be constructed in the middle of Songkhla Lake with a length of 80m + 140m + 80m. The superstructure of the bridge uses a box-shaped prestressed concrete beam with stay cables installed from the bridge girder to the bridge pylon. The lower structure of the main bridge is comprised of large-bored piles, bridge pier structures, and cast-in-place concrete pillars. The Songkhla Lake Bridge incorporates two gentle curves with radius of 1,000m and 1,500m on the Phatthalung and Songkhla sides respectively. 46. The bridge drainage is designed to be Pipe Flow system to address pollution incidents, where drainage flows through a channel on the bridge floor (bridge gully) into a manhole before draining into the lake below. There will be specific protocols in case oil or dangerous chemical leaks occur on the bridge. If a leak occurs through the gully drain channel, it will flow into the manhole at the pier under the bridge and collected for disposal at an appropriate hazardous waste disposal site. 47. The construction of Songkhla Lake Bridge requires an estimated period of three years. The construction of the main span of the bridge and the connecting parts will occur simultaneously and take approximately 30 months and 36 months respectively. The construction of the connecting parts of the bridge takes place at every 10 bridge pier structures, moving closer to the main span of the bridge from both sides. The construction of each bridge pier structure takes approximately 115 to 120 days. Sediment curtains with a size of 330m x 55m will be installed at the main span of the bridge, covering the underwater structure. Sediment curtains will be installed from the start of constructing the temporary platforms to the completion of bridge footing work, which will take approximately eight months. Sediment curtains will be reinstalled for dismantling of the temporary construction platform, which will take approximately one month. Sediment curtains with a size of 34m x 25m will also be installed at connecting bridges covering the underwater structure. There are two structures of the connecting part of the bridge: (i) the substructure is comprised of concrete piles, concrete bridge pier structures and concrete pillars which require construction in Songkhla Lake; and (ii) the superstructure uses a box-shaped prestressed concrete beam; installation of the beam is done after the construction of the substructure is completed. A launching girder is used and can be installed from the completed substructure that progresses from the edges towards the center of the bridge. Construction of the bridge will involve boring, vibratory or impact pilings that cause underwater noise. An assessment of the impact of underwater noise, using a precautionary approach, reveals that underwater noise from project activities could exceed the assessment thresholds within a limited zone (of up to 300m away from construction) during piling activities. The core area occupied by Irrawaddy dolphins is 8 to 10km away from the bridge construction site, however, given the uncertainty about their year-round ranging patterns, the project will implement precautionary mitigation measures to minimize the risk of project- generated noise impacting the dolphin sub-population (these include observer platforms, acoustic detection, and others). The construction method allows the free flow of water currents and will not impact the movement of fish or other food sources on which the dolphins depend. The oceanography and erosion analysis found there would be no significant change to water flow, and sediment dispersion would be mostly localized, although silt curtains will be installed to mitigate this impact. 49. Activities during the operation phase of the bridge will have a reduced intensity compared to the construction phase. The bridge pier structures will not affect the flow of water currents, cause erosion or affect the benthic fauna or other components of the food chain. The noise levels that may be generated during operation should fall below the assessment criteria within 100 m, and dolphins are expected to become accustomed to this noise should they be present in the vicinity. The negative impact to the Irrawaddy dolphins, thus, is expected to be of low significance based on a precautionary assessment. 50. The project promotes integration of efforts from all stakeholders towards Irrawaddy dolphin conservation. Current challenges are the result of limited funding, fragmentation of responsibilities and insufficient coordination among relevant agencies responsible for their protection, technical limitations, and knowledge gaps. The long-term strategy for the conservation of Irrawaddy dolphins in Thale Luang, developed by the Government, provides the forward look and the commitment needed to address these challenges, whereas the DCP presents an action-oriented plan for implementation over the first five years. Given the critical status of the sub-population and the growing risk that it becomes non-viable, the DCP takes the most expeditious approach to its conservation, by focusing on minimizing threats to the dolphins and their habitat, promoting alternative livelihoods to both benefit communities and to limit further habitat distortions, closing key knowledge gaps for adaptive management, and analyzing potential wider, systemic impacts on the ecosystem. Assessment of consistency with country’s climate strategies: a. The project is consistent with Thailand’s 3rd Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC 3.0), the National Adaptation Plan (NAP) and Long-Term Strategy (LTS). The NDC 3.0 targets include reducing Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emission from the transport sector by 22.6 MtCO2 equivalent by 2035 from the 2019 baseline. The project contributes to fuel use efficiency through the construction of two bridges that lower travel time, distance, and improve efficiency, while providing essential last- mile access functions to critical utilities, such as electricity, water, fast internet, and solid waste management. This is in line with Thailand’s LTS, which identifies transport as contributing sector to emissions reduction through cleaner and more efficient use of fuels and technologies. On adaptation, Thailand’s NAP and LTS include efforts to enhance capacity of local communities to cope and respond to climate change impacts, and to sustainably manage natural resources. The project directly contributes to these efforts through Irrawaddy dolphin conservation and ecosystem management, as well as through provision of various capacity building activities to build resilience to risks from climate hazards. The project directly contributes to implementing the recommendations from the Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) for Thailand on prioritizing investment to reduce vulnerability of communities and infrastructure to flooding risks. 56. Assessment and reduction of climate change mitigation risks: a. Component 1: The construction of Lanta Bridge and Songkhla Lake Bridge are comparable to the construction of a secondary road section and are low risk. Lanta Bridge will serve an essential last-mile access function to connect the rural Lanta Islands to Krabi province’s mainland. Songkhla Lake Bridge will be connected by secondary roads in the Khao Chaison District of Phatthalung Province and in the Krasae Sin District of Songkhla Province. Both bridges, once constructed, will share the infrastructure with and not create any barriers to non-motorized transport (NMT), long distance public transport, nor the transition to electric vehicles in the future, and present low risk in terms of carbon lock-in and transition risks. Importantly, the existing ferry service between Lanta Islands and the mainland is not a substitute to the proposed Lanta Bridge because: (i) it cannot meet the connectivity demand for critical emergency situations such as carrying medical ambulance during off-hours, low tides, or under strong wind conditions on the sea, and (ii) in addition to transport connectivity, the proposed Lanta Bridge will also connect the Lanta Islands with other critical utilities, such as waste, electricity, water, and fiber optic internet. Given that Songkhla Lake is a protected area, there are no other technically feasible and economically viable lower carbon alternatives to the Songkhla Lake Bridge, including inland waterway transport. The Songkhla Lake Bridge will serve as an alternative route to an existing bridge over the Songkhla Lake and shorten the travel distance from 80km to 8km between two sides of the lake using the existing bridge. Consequently, the traveling volume between the two sides of the lake is expected to reduce from approximately 147 million vehicle-km per year on average without the bridge to about 15 million vehicle-km with the proposed bridge. This results in a net GHG emission reduction of 0.46 million tCO2e over the economic lifetime of the bridge. Therefore, the traffic volume using the bridges and associated emissions are expected to be moderate and non-material to Thailand’s low- carbon pathway. Further, both bridges are located far from cities (70-80km away) and will be an integral part of the existing secondary road network, which does not lead to any risk of urban sprawl. Lastly, the World Bank provided technical assistance to Thailand’s electric mobility adoption in 2022, focusing on analyzing the potential impact of E-Mobility Transition to Thailand’s automobile industry and policy recommendations to facilitate the industrial readiness. Assessment and reduction of climate change adaptation risks: a. Component 1: The inherent physical climate risks exposed to the proposed Lanta Bridge and Songkhla Lake Bridge are considered moderate. The Lanta Bridge will be located over the sea between the Lanta Islands and the mainland of Krabi province. The physical climate risk applicable is mainly high tide surge from sea level raise and tsunami, and strong wind from typhoon. Although the likelihood of occurrence for tsunami and typhoon is low (the record spanning 61 years from 1951 to 2011 shows only one typhoon passed over Thailand and one tsunami incident in the Koh Lanta District where the project bridge is located), the potential damaging impact could be severe, therefore the overall risk is rated moderate. The Songkhla Lake Bridge will be over the largest lake in Thailand, the Songkhla Lake, which occupies a very large area over 1,040km2. Given the large area occupied by the lake, heavy precipitations from extreme climate events will lead to very little changes to the water level and velocity of the lake. Therefore, the inherent physical risk of flood-induced bridge scour is low. However, given its location over a massive open area above the lake, the Songkhla Lake Bridge is exposed to extreme wind risks from typhoons, which threatens the structural stability of the bridge. Both bridges are exposed to heat wave risks, which have becoming increasingly frequent in Thailand. In 2023 and 2024, heatwaves stroke Thailand consecutively between April and May with the highest temperature reaching a record- breaking level of 52 degrees Celsius. Heatwave poses significant risks to infrastructure including bridges due to structural expansion and material melting. The Lanta Bridge and the Songkhla Lake Bridge are designed with core climate resilience features through (i) bridge structure that is adaptive to identified climate risks; and (ii) adoption of alternative climate resilient materials for construction. In order to adapt to potential risks from high tide surge and tsunami wave from the sea, the structural design of the 2-km Lanta Bridge uses a combination of balance cantilever bridge (1km over the shallow sea) and an extradosed bridge (main bridge over the sea, 440 m), and an approach bridge (on land, 340 m). The use of extradosed bridge enables the height of the Lanta Bridge over the sea to reach 16.50m and thereby significantly reduces the risk exposure to tide surge and tsunami waves. In comparison, if only the balance cantilever bridge structure is used for the entire Lanta Bridge, the height of the bridge over the sea will only be 11.50 m. To mitigate the risk of seawater corrosion with increasing sea level and tide velocity, the Lanta Bridge will be constructed using a resilient material named Marine Concrete, which enables the bridge to withstand Chloride and Sulfate components of sea water that are dangerous for reinforced steel and concrete. Permanent steel casings will additionally act as a prevention from seawater corrosion. Non-metal road studs will also be used to prevent seawater corrosion. For the Songkhla Lake Bridge, the structure of the bridge uses specific reinforcement design to enhance the bridge’s resilience against extreme wind velocity by following the AASHTO LRFD Bridge Design Specifications. Using this design specification leads to an overall higher resilience level of the bridge. In particular, the applied specifications ensure that the Songkhla Lake Bridge’s structural components and reinforcement will withstand a uniform wind pressure of 2.4 kN/m2 acting on the bridge deck. This pressure (force) corresponds to a wind speed of 161km/h (100 mph), higher than the recorded wind speed of ‘Typhoon Gay’, which was about 120km/h. In addition to structural resilience, the Songkhla Lake Bridge will also use high-performance to enhance fatigue resistance under high wind loads and additional expansion joints to adapt to heatwaves. Based on the approved EIAs, individual potential environmental impacts and risks are rated largely low to moderate, and are readily avoided or mitigated by the selection of bridge alignment (minimizing/avoiding impacts on mangrove, coral and seagrass), construction methods (bridge pile drilling rather than impact driving), and applying mitigation and monitoring measures prescribed in the environmental and social management plans (ESMPs). Under Component 2, there is a risk that efforts at saving the sub-population of Irrawaddy dolphins are misinformed or ineffective, or that the release of certain fish species for restocking the fishery could increase the use of illegal fishing gear that increase the risk of dolphin entanglement. There is also a risk that dolphin conservation measures could reduce access to fishing livelihoods leading to resentment of the dolphins by fishing communities. The high dolphin-related risks arise primarily from the extremely small size of the sub-population, which provides only a minimal safety margin – if human-caused mortalities continue, the sub-population may soon become non-viable. The social risk rating is Substantial largely due to access restrictions and possible displacement of fishers from Component 1 and 2 activities in the Songkhla bridge. The survey revealed three sections within the ROW, from Phatthalung to the Songkhla side, consisting of fish-aggregating devices (FADs) namely, six rows of fish/shrimp traps (totaling 58 traps) and 32 single traps owned by fishers. As estimated by DNP, which conducts regular patrols in the area, these traps are owned by about 8-10 fishers. Other than those using FADs, another affected group are fishers using nets to catch fish.
