The Republic of Yemen is currently in the middle of a severe humanitarian crisis. The country has been in a complex civil war since 2014, struggling due to a fall in global prices, weak public infrastructure, a limited ability to cope with extreme climate events, and the strains of COVID‐19. Yemen also battles mass outbreaks of preventable diseases such as cholera, diphtheria, measles, and dengue fever. Yemen’s death rate is currently five times the global average,1 and the country has been designated a fragility, conflict, and violence (FCV) country by the World Bank. 2. Authority in Yemen is fragmented, with parallel institutions operating in government and opposition‐held areas. Aden remains the interim capital, with the internationally recognized Government of Yemen (GoY), while Sana’a is controlled by the De Facto Authorities (DFA). 3. Yemen’s seven years of continuous armed conflict since 2014 have caused massive damage and prompted a major humanitarian crisis. Over 80 percent of Yemen’s 31 million population remain in need of assistance. Two million children are acutely malnourished. There is a continued outbreak of diseases, and close to 18 million people lack access to safe water and sanitation. In recent years, the situation has been compounded by massive flooding and worsening displacement, with over 4 million Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) as of early 2022. Changes in the humanitarian situation have corresponded to various conflict‐related shocks in recent years, including large swings in violence, changes in governance from the capture of territory, intermittent restrictions on transportation, natural disasters, widespread disease outbreaks, and currency crises. 4. The ongoing conflict has severe socioeconomic consequences for the country. Socioeconomic conditions are deteriorating rapidly, affected by declining remittances, trade disruptions, severe fuel supply shortages, and the disrupted and declining humanitarian operations. “The onset of the conflict led to a sharp drop in economic activity. In 2018–19, as conflict activity stabilized, oil production and exports picked up, thereby supporting a recovery. However, that recovery proved fleeting, and the economy’s downward trajectory resumed when the conflict intensified in 2020 with a contraction of 8.5 percent, followed by a further contraction in 2021, projected at 2 percent.”2 Non‐oil economic activities were affected by the COVID‐19‐induced slowdown in trade, dual taxation systems in the north and south, scarcity of inputs, and commodity price hikes. The war in Ukraine is further worsening the already desperate socioeconomic situation, as discussed below. 5. Overall macroeconomic risks remain significant. “While a modest rebound in the growth rate of the gross domestic product (GDP) is expected in 2022, Yemen’s economic prospects heavily depend on the evolution of the conflict and overall security conditions on the ground. On the downside, episodes of hostilities coupled with persistently high import prices could further undermine conditions for the private sector. Yemen’s fiscal budget and foreign exchange earnings over the last three decades used to be heavily dependent on oil and gas revenues; the decline in production due to the crisis has severely affected the government’s budget. Nearly 75 percent of central government fiscal revenues and 90 percent of export receipts used to come from the oil sector, which contributed to volatility in public finances, inflationary pressures, competitiveness of the non‐oil sectors, and external balances.4 Oil revenues accounted for 30 percent of total government revenues (excluding foreign assistance) in 2020 and an estimated 44 percent in 2021.5 Oil production commenced in the mid‐1980s, peaked in 2000 at around 440,000 barrels per day, then began to wane from 2000 to 2010 as existing oil fields began to deplete — with that production decline exacerbated by disruptions arising from the civil conflict from 2014 to 2020. Oil production dropped to 125,000 barrels per day by 2014, reached a low of 18,000 barrels per day in 2016, before rising slowly to 65,000 barrels per day in 2020.6 The collapse in oil and gas production has therefore severely affected the government’s budget and Yemen’s external balances. 7. While the evolution of the war in Ukraine remains rapid and highly uncertain, it is estimated to have a broadly negative impact on the Yemen economy, through rising oil and food prices. While the war in Ukraine offers an opportunity for Yemen to increase its export revenues from crude oil sales — due to the spike in international oil prices to over $100/barrel in the first half of 2022— this would likely be offset by the sharp rise in costs of imported refined petroleum products. The increase in other commodity prices, including food items like wheat, due to the war in Ukraine will add to the severity of the problems in Yemen.7 Imported foods account for over 80 percent of Yemen’s food needs8, making it highly vulnerable to the spikes in global food prices, especially wheat. Ukraine and Russia are significant exporters of grains and agricultural fertilizers, so Yemen faces sharply higher prices for both.9 Those negative impacts from the war in Ukraine will exacerbate the already dire food security situation that Yemen was facing in 2020 and 2021, which arose from a combination of several large shocks including the COVID‐19 pandemic, a significant decline in humanitarian assistance, a currency crisis, rising global food prices, difficulties in importing fuel, and increasing conflict.10 8. The effects of climate change have exacerbated the disaster vulnerability of the Republic of Yemen. The Notre Dame Global Adaptation Initiative (ND‐GAIN) for 2019 ranks Yemen as the 22nd most climate change vulnerable country in the world and the 13th least‐ready country to adapt, with its readiness score significantly affected by the ongoing conflict.12 While the overall projected precipitation trends for the next 30 years differ among climate models, Yemen’s rainfall patterns have shown increasing extremes. Overall, temperatures in Yemen have increased by 1.8⁰C in the past 50 years. With a wide range of regional variations, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change forecasts average temperature increases of 2.3⁰C by 2060, with heat waves lasting longer.13 Greater rainfall variability in the future could increase drought periods, diminishing water supplies more rapidly. Similarly, increased temperatures could lead to higher evapotranspiration rates, further slowing the replenishment of water sources.14 The rural population and especially farmers depending on rain‐fed agriculture are hardest hit by climate impacts, leading to overextraction of groundwater resources. The rate of groundwater extraction currently exceeds a sustainable rate at which water can be replenished, depleting groundwater sources at a rate of 1 to 7 m per year. At this rate, Yemen’s groundwater reserves will likely be depleted within 20 to 30 years regardless of climate change.15 10. Disruption to energy access, transport, and water have had far‐reaching direct and indirect impacts on households. Nightlight satellite imagery shows that major electricity disruption has left three‐ quarters of the country in the dark. The electricity grid and distribution networks have been extensively damaged by the conflict, leaving millions of households and businesses without access to public electricity, with diesel generators and solar PV only available to a few. Damage to critical services facilities—health clinics, water and sanitation infrastructure, and schools—has resulted in increased deaths and illnesses due to disease and starvation. International trade and mobility are seriously hindered due to the extensive damage to key airports and ports. Connective roads and bridges across the north of the country have been severely affected by the conflict, disrupting flows of trade and humanitarian aid. Telecommunications infrastructure has also been damaged during the conflict, cutting people off from communications within the country and internationally. Nearly 70 percent16 of Yemen’s 31 million population17 lives in rural and peri‐urban areas where the level of poverty is higher than in urban areas and is increasing. More than 12 million of the population in rural and peri‐urban areas are poor and food insecure, while more than 5 million are on the verge of starvation. GDP per capita has more than halved since 201418, reflecting the deteriorating situation. During the 1980s, expatriate Yemenis transferred substantial remittances from Saudi Arabia, UAE, and other countries, investing them in vehicles, new houses, and water wells with diesel‐operated pumps, and turned their rain‐fed farm fields into irrigated fields of qat and citrus. By the 1990s, rural living conditions began to deteriorate because of the civil war and external shocks. In the early 2000s, as farming incomes diminished, the main source of income for most rural households was men’s urban labor, resulting in less earnings. Poverty worsened. By 2020, meager household incomes in rural Yemen had mostly disappeared, falling victim to Yemen’s ongoing conflict, devastating droughts and floods, and the global COVID‐19 pandemic. Self‐sufficiency in basic staples was a distant memory. Rural people became as dependent on food imports as urban dwellers, with one notable difference — the higher added cost of transportation to remote locations. The situation is expected to worsen as a result of rising costs of food imports and a reduction in remittances caused by the war in Ukraine. 12. The devastation and destruction of war has not spared the rural and peri‐urban electricity sector. The few rural and peri‐urban areas that were supplied power from the grid prior to the conflict have either had the infrastructure destroyed or cannot be supplied due to insufficient grid‐connected generation capacity. Without access to electricity, health clinics have not been able to operate after sunset, store medicines, or power medical equipment; water wells have not been able to pump clean drinking water; farmers have not been able to irrigate crops and/or they have been using polluting fuel‐ powered generators to pump water for irrigation; and educational institutions have struggled to operate effectively, all contributing to the humanitarian disaster disproportionately affecting Yemen’s poorest and most vulnerable rural and peri‐urban population. 13. The World Bank19 and other international organizations have helped provide solar energy solutions for rural and peri‐urban health clinics, schools, and water facilities and have encouraged the development of a private sector‐driven market for renewable, off‐grid electricity. The support has not been enough. The need for targeted interventions to improve electricity access to rural and peri‐urban households and critical services facilities continues to be huge. More attention and assistance needs be paid to restoring and improving off‐grid power supply in rural and peri‐urban areas if the worst effects of the humanitarian crisis are to be stemmed. The energy sector in Yemen comprises two main components: (a) oil and natural gas production and (b) electricity production, transmission, and distribution. The two are intertwined because oil and gas sector revenues provide most of the government revenues required to subsidize the electricity sector and fuel supply drives electricity production. 15. Yemen’s oil and natural gas sector remains in decline. Yemen’s economy was transformed by the discovery and production of oil. Yemen’s natural oil and gas proven reserves are 3 billion barrels and 9.4 trillion cubic feet, respectively. However, oil production in Yemen has seen a massive decline — due to the absence of investment, the natural decline of operating fields, kidnappings of oil workers, perceptions of unattractive production sharing agreements (PSAs), and frequent attacks on oil and gas infrastructure. The oil production that still occurs is in the governates of Shabwa, Marib, and Hadhramaut.20,21 Oil from those areas is exported from the Bir Ali terminal (Mukalla) and Ash Shihr terminal (east of Mukalla) (see Figure 1), with the earnings from those exports being an important part of the IRG’s revenues, along with international remittances, foreign aid, and import duties and taxes. 16. Yemen’s natural gas production, like oil production, has suffered the consequences of persistent attacks throughout the conflict. The country’s largest natural gas processing and export facility, Yemen Liquified Natural Gas (LNG), which commenced operations in 2009, halted operations for six months in 2014 and then permanently in the first half of 2015 (when the current conflict reached the oil and gas producing governorates) and has remained idle since. The LNG liquefaction plant at Balhaf (see Figure 1) is under care and maintenance by its shareholders. The war in Ukraine and resulting scale‐down of European imports of oil and gas from Russia offers an opportunity for increased LNG export revenues if production could be scaled up. 17. Yemen’s state‐owned Public Electricity Corporation (PEC) is the enterprise that oversees electricity generation, transmission, and distribution in the county. Other sector participants include: the General Authority for Rural Electrification (GARE), which is responsible for electrifying specific rural areas outside the main and secondary cities; private generators that produce electricity for own use or to sell to the PEC; private captive power produced for government buildings; and self‐generation by consumers. 18. The PEC struggles to meet the demand of its existing customers, most of which are urban, and there are no indications that the crises the utility faces will be resolved soon or that rural households and institutions will be connected to the grid any time soon. Electricity sector activities are governed by Electricity Law No.1 of 2009. The law stipulated several positive and ambitious measures to reform the sector and improve sector performance, such as unbundling the PEC into three corporate entities—one each for generation, transmission, and distribution—and creating an independent regulator to control sector activities. Private sector participation in the electricity sector began in 2006 and was limited, by law, to electricity generation activities. The PEC purchased energy from private producers, based on short‐term contracts, and supplied them with the fuel needed for generation. The role of the private sector was mainly to back up the PEC in bridging the electricity supply gap. A law allowing public‐private partnerships, and the sale of privately produced power to end use consumers, was drafted before the war but has not been approved. At present, there is no formal legal basis for the direct sale of privately produced electricity to end use consumers, even though such sales are taking place in Yemen during the conflict. Yemen’s public electricity system, supplying mostly cities and urban areas, was already suffering from numerous problems before the latest civil conflict erupted in 2015. Many of the PEC’s power generation stations are old and in a poor state of repair, having had little or no maintenance or rehabilitation for many years. In addition, almost the entire generation fleet is operated at great cost on diesel or heavy fuel oil. Historically, the electricity tariffs levied on consumers, while extremely low, have been far below the cost of production — which led to large subsidies having to be paid by the Government, absorbing a large share of the national budget. The unsustainable nature of Yemen’s power sector operations also means that Yemen’s electricity transmission and distribution grid infrastructure was run‐ down, inefficient, and unreliable before the conflict. Although many technical studies were conducted before the war on ways to upgrade Yemen’s generation, transmission, and distribution systems, few of the needed investments took place. 21. The ongoing conflict in Yemen has significantly worsened an already inadequate electricity supply. The provision of public electricity service has essentially collapsed. Now after seven years into the conflict, only about 12 percent of the population relies on public electricity. Five out of ten cities assessed in the latest update of the Yemen Dynamic Needs Assessment were found to lack access to public electricity services, including major cities such as Aden, Sana’a, and Hodeida. Even in cities where critical power infrastructure remains intact, assets often sit idle due to fuel shortages. Inadequate electricity infrastructure has contributed to aggravating humanitarian conditions throughout the country, a decrease in productivity, a rapid deterioration of the business environment, and a reduction in GDP. 23 22. Restoring electricity supply in Yemen is vital to restoring other essential services—especially water pumping, water treatment, sanitation services, and hospital and health care services. Impacts on those dependent on reliable electricity have been devastating. In the absence of functioning public utilities, power supply is becoming a binding constraint for critical services such as health facilities, water supply and sanitation, food supply and cold chain, banking services, and agriculture.24 Because the state can no longer provide fuels in sufficient quantity at subsidized rates and the PEC has difficulties collecting bills, the PEC is in severe financial distress and losing money on every unit (kWh) supplied. Solar power has been the main source of electricity supply for basic needs since the outbreak of the conflict for many Yemenis and represents a major opportunity to address some of the most immediate impacts of the developmental crisis and restore livelihoods. In one of the few positive stories that emerged from the conflict, the lack of public electricity supply and limited fuel availability for diesel generators has been the growth of a booming industry for small‐ to medium‐scale solar systems. Based on a market assessment conducted in 2019,27 it is estimated that the market penetration of PV systems is already at around 75 percent of households in several major urban areas, including Sana’a. According to some estimates, including a recent representative phone survey, some 50 percent of Yemenis use solar for lighting or appliances. Estimates suggest that more than US$200 million has been invested per year in the residential off‐grid solar sector since the outbreak of the conflict. Beyond households, significant opportunities exist to use solar power to help restore critical parts of public service, agriculture, and commerce — including by installing solar systems for use in schools, clinics and health care centers, water cooperatives, and irrigation pumps —to help alleviate the most immediate and dangerous impacts of the unfolding developmental and humanitarian crisis. The use of solar power will contribute towards addressing climate vulnerabilities. Yemen regularly suffers from drought and requires electricity for ground water extraction. In addition to climate change mitigation, the use of solar power will therefore enable the replacement of more expensive and less reliable fossil fueled power generation plant used to pump water for human consumption and agriculture. Yemen’s authorities have struggled for decades to expand energy access through the expansion of the national grid, and recurring conflicts have repeatedly set back whatever small progress was being made. (b) Off‐grid solar market assessment. Participating MFIs have also expressed interest in better understanding the market opportunity for off‐grid solar PUE. The larger off‐grid solar systems capable of powering PUE appliances are sold at higher prices, more in line with the typical loan sizes offered by MFIs. The most relevant appliance categories for Yemen include cooling; cold storage; ice‐making; drying; agro‐processing (milling, husking, threshing, hulling, grating, and pressing); and livestock (poultry lighting, egg incubation, and milking machines). Many of these appliance categories will reduce drudgery for women, while some, such as electric sewing machines, will increase productivity and incomes in sectors that mainly employ women. The project will undertake a market assessment to provide a comprehensive analysis of: (i) the current PUE market and the key stakeholders active in the market; (ii) the potential market; (iii) the main market barriers; and (iv) the institutional and regulatory framework for renewable energy, clarifying the role of renewables, and the need for legislations to promote renewable energy and incentives and measures to facilitate the access of financing schemes for renewable power for PUE. It will make recommendations on how market barriers might be overcome. Data collected and analysis provided will be carried out in a sex‐disaggregated way. (c) Mini‐grid market assessment. Mini‐grids run by private operators, primarily using diesel generators, are in operation in parts of Yemen with high population density and elevated levels of economic activity that rely on the availability of electricity (such as ice‐making for cold storage of fish). However, there is currently no legal or regulatory framework for these businesses —neither as independent producers and distributors, nor as public‐private partnerships. The assessment will therefore advise on the needed sectoral legal, policy, and regulatory reforms and evaluate the current market and the potential market; identifying the main barriers to establishing a mini‐grid market and how these might be overcome. On the technical side, the assessment will take full account of plans for grid extension and the work of other stakeholders in this area before seeking to identify a specific geographical area where a potential project might be developed to test a model for supporting electricity service delivery through mini‐grids and analyze mini‐grid feasibility in more detail for that location. The assessment will also take stock of international best practices and innovative solutions regarding support to the regulated and unregulated mini‐grid sectors in other FCV or humanitarian settings, such as Gaza and Somalia. The proposed project design incorporates lessons learned during the preparation and implementation of RY‐EEAP on the World Bank’s experience in conflict‐affected countries and is informed by ongoing engagements in Yemen, one of which is the success of working through UN partners. The World Bank can achieve results in FCV situations, where the Government lacks capacity, by instituting direct third‐party implementation arrangements. The World Bank has demonstrated an increasing ability to work effectively through UN partners in complex FCV environments and has successfully collaborated with UN agencies, including RY‐EEAP. 74. Another lesson learned from the ongoing RY‐EEAP stresses the need for flexibility even after the areas of intervention have been agreed. Access to areas that have been agreed can change without notice due to renewed conflict or imposition of access restrictions. In such cases, the project design’s flexibility in terms of targeted size of systems and beneficiaries (including household businesses for productive use), allows resources to be reallocated to other areas and activities that do not face such restrictions. 75. As reflected in the project’s design, building large‐scale energy infrastructure in Yemen through the existing sector institutions is challenging. This is a lesson learned from a review of the WBG’s past lending Page 32 of 65 The World Bank Yemen Emergency Electricity Access Project-Phase II (RY-EEAP-II) (P178347) activities in Yemen’s energy sector.38 The review found that the main public institutions in the energy sector did not have adequate institutional capacity to implement large investments with multiple contracts on time even before the conflict, despite significant donor financing accompanied by TA in the form of engineering consultants hired to support decisions and procurement processes. The current conflict has led to further weakening of the sector institutions, fragmentation of institutional authority, and deteriorated capacity. Therefore, the review concluded that the World Bank should prioritize distributed energy solutions and seek alternative models for procurement and project management when financing energy infrastructure investments in Yemen. Nonetheless, the PEC and the power grid will be required to play a vital role in the development of a sustainable power sector for socioeconomic recovery post the conflict. The project therefore includes TA to support addressing broader power sector issues. 76. Subsidies need to be appropriately targeted to the poor and those in remote and inaccessible areas to address affordability, equity, and inclusion. This lesson comes from the 2015 evaluation by the Independent Evaluation Group (IEG) of the WBG’s assistance for energy access.39 IEG’s 2013 assessment of the WBG’s assistance to low‐income fragile and conflict‐affected states40 concludes that country assistance has lacked the flexibility to respond to conflict risks that have materialized. In response, the design of the project is flexible to account for changes in the delivery capacity of the implementation agencies and security risks due to conflict. III. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis (if applicable) (i) Technical Analysis 91. Distributed solar energy systems provided through the private sector represent an environmentally sustainable and technically resilient electricity access solution for countries that suffer protracted fragility and conflict. Due to their decentralized nature, off‐grid solar is more resilient to situations of fragility and conflict. Small plug and play systems, which are modular, portable, and require no installation, are particularly suited for situations of uncertainty and instability. In countries where public utilities and government entities can no longer ensure equitable access of electricity to the population, the private sector can be significantly more resilient and effective, providing solar systems as a product rather than a service. As fuel‐independent systems, solar systems circumvent the long and burdensome supply chain to bring fuel for generators and result in fewer adverse environmental and health impacts. Finally, solar systems are significantly cheaper than fuel‐based generators with costs having dropped significantly (80 percent plus) over the past five years. 92. The installation of solar PV systems in areas not connected to the grid is a ‘no regrets’ action. In a no‐conflict situation, areas remote from the grid in Yemen would not be given priority to connect to the grid, especially with an insolvent public supplier such as PEC. Off‐grid supplies, such as solar PV and other renewables, would be of lower cost, and solar PV would be the least‐cost solution for Tier 1 supply where Page 36 of 65 The World Bank Yemen Emergency Electricity Access Project-Phase II (RY-EEAP-II) (P178347) pico‐solar meets demand. Moreover, as the national grid or mini‐grids develop(s), the sub‐component 1.2 and 1.3 systems can be integrated with the networks. 93. Historical concerns of poor‐quality solar products have been addressed globally, but in Yemen, quality systems are not yet widely available. Solar systems sold in Yemen are of poor designs that prioritize more affordable, low quality, and easily available components over higher‐quality alternatives. In addition, since middle‐to‐low‐income households have severe affordability and access to finance challenges, they opt for cheaper, low‐quality systems that match their ability to pay, but that will break down after a few months of operation. The project will continue to work to improve technical standards in the market and ensure that systems under the project meet the minimum quality standards of Lighting Global and are certified by VeraSol. (ii) Economic Analysis 94. The adoption of quality solar products is estimated to result in a net economic benefit for all groups of beneficiaries. The main impact of the project is the increased access to electricity through distributed solar energy systems, a substitute for dirtier and more expensive alternatives. The economic internal rate of return (IRR) for each of the project beneficiaries is conservatively quantified based on avoided costs of fossil fuel alternatives over the lifetime of each solar system. These avoided costs include both the direct costs—for example, of kerosene lamps or diesel generation—and the avoided social costs of carbon emissions. 95. Conservatively estimated economic IRRs range from 10–25 percent for health clinics and schools to 83–98 percent for rural and peri‐urban households. It should be noted that the deployment of solar will have benefits beyond fuel savings and GHG emissions, including improved air quality and benefits due to the restoration of critical services, which would further increase the IRR but are not captured in this cost‐ benefit analysis. 96. The investment case for solar deteriorates with shorter product lifetimes, showing the value of introducing higher‐quality products in the market. Although high‐quality, small‐scale household solar products have much higher up‐front costs, they generate greater benefits due to their longer lifetimes. Even without any capital subsidy, a high‐quality solar product (15 W) financed over two years with a lifetime of five years realizes a higher internal rate of return compared to a cheaper low‐quality product with a one‐year lifetime. Providing a grant only further improves the investment case. Even when considering a scenario in which beneficiaries will have access to subsidized fossil fuel, investments in high‐ quality solar systems can break even financially in the first year. This illustrates the potential benefits from providing beneficiaries with access to quality products and finance to overcome the barrier of high upfront cost. (iii) Gender 97. The proposed project is an emergency operation processed under IPF Policy and Directive paragraph 12 ‘Projects in Situations of Urgent Need or Capacity Constraints’. It uses UNOPS as the non‐ sovereign recipient of IDA funds and alternative implementation agency on an exceptional basis under the FMFA between the World Bank and UN agencies. The FMFA provides for the use of the UN’s Financial Regulations by offering flexibility in the application of World Bank fiduciary policies through the application of alternative assurance mechanisms. UNOPS will follow its own procurement procedures as alternate procurement arrangements (APA) allowed by the World Bank’s Procurement Framework Policy Section III F. 2. The project will be implemented by UNOPS through direct implementation and with local entities. UNOPS will (a) be responsible for project implementation; (c) monitor the project targets and results in coordination with the local entities; (c) handle relevant procurement, financial management, and disbursement management, including the preparation of withdrawal applications under the project; and (d) ensure that all reporting requirements for IDA are met according to the project Financing Agreement. 3.
