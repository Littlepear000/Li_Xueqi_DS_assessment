STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. The Region 1. Economic growth in the Eastern Caribbean has long been volatile due to the concentration of activities in a few sectors and vulnerability to natural disasters and other external shocks.1 The region’s growth volatility is among the highest in the world, over twice the average for the Pacific Islands between 2000 and 2019 (Figure 1). Member countries of the Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS) rely heavily on tourism, which accounts for 35 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) on average and provides more than half of all jobs. As a result, economic performance is often captive to developments in the tourism sector and the business cycles of the tourism source markets – primarily the United States, Canada, and Europe. This impact has been especially strong during the so-called Great Recession that began in 2008 and the ongoing coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic. Dependence on tourism also amplifies the impact of extreme climate events such as hurricanes and cyclones because they can curtail tourist arrivals. The current recovery offers countries a chance for a vital strategic shift: resist the temptation to court a higher volume of tourists by offering the lowest prices and focus instead on developing high-value, sustainable tourism that caters to smaller numbers of more culturally and environmentally responsible visitors. “Blue Tourism” focuses on activities that generate low environmental impacts, conserve natural resources, and support local communities. Blue tourist spending can generate significant value to countries. For example, in 2019, high-value marine ecosystem-based tourism contributed an estimated 30 percent of Saint Lucia’s and Grenada’s total GDP, and 23 percent of Saint Vincent and the Grenadines’ (SVG).9 Studies from Fiji show that investments in marine protected areas boosted tourism income through additional spending by visitors, increasing aggregate household income by US$3,171 and creating 34,000 new local tourism jobs.10 7. To support recovery and the transition toward high-value tourism, the Eastern Caribbean region needs to strengthen cooperation between governments to build climate resilience and protect the integrity of their shared coastal and marine natural assets. These assets underpin the countries’ shared blue economy in sectors such as tourism and fisheries, which represent the next frontier of growth. Bolstering climate resilience of the islands’ coastal communities and their infrastructure is key to ensuring equitable development in priority sectors. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. The Blue Economy is defined as an approach to promote sustainable and integrated use of marine resources for economic growth, improved livelihoods, and job generation while preserving the health of ocean ecosystems.11 It covers a range of interdependent sectors, including fisheries, coastal tourism, and waste management.12 Fisheries, for example, provide the hospitality industry with quality and affordable seafood, while the tourism sector supports fisher livelihoods and generates employment. Waste management, meanwhile, is crucial to the health of fisheries and other marine resources and ecosystems which are, in turn, critical for the beaches and other natural assets that attract holiday makers. Going forward, anticipating and adapting to the impacts of climate change will be an essential component of the blue economy, ensuring that low-carbon and climate-resilient measures are incorporated across sectors to ensure sustainability of marine resources and continued generation of their benefits. 9. Women-owned businesses have been hardest hit by the pandemic and it is essential that a vibrant Blue Economy invest in supporting women to enter high-value market segments. Women face a significant gender pay gap of up to 23 percent in Grenada, which is part of a wider gender pay gap in the OECS labor market, despite women attaining relatively high levels of education.13 A recent survey showed that in 2019 men-owned and managed businesses had a financial turnover twice that of women-owned and managed businesses, and that there was a high risk of this gender pay gap widening further in 2021.14 The survey also found that women in tourism are concentrated in smaller, less profitable business sectors than men. Tourism is the single most important source of economic growth and export revenue in OECS countries, and the COVID-19 pandemic has had a devastating effect on the industry. Prior to the crisis, the tourism industry’s contribution to GDP was close to 40 percent in Grenada and SVG, and nearly 70 percent in Saint Lucia, while employment was more than 40 percent in Grenada and SVG and nearly 70 percent in Saint Lucia. The sector is highly concentrated in the North American market, increasing its vulnerabilities.15 Overnight international leisure tourists contribute the most to travel and tourism expenditures (59 percent), followed by yachting (22.4 percent) and regional tourism (4.8 percent), while cruise expenditures are small at 4 percent. The COVID-19 pandemic has been devastating to the sector with tourism’s contribution to GDP and employment falling by more than 55 percent and 20 percent, respectively, in 2020. While tourism has gradually begun to pick up in the second half of 2021, with the return of international tourists, low levels of tourist arrivals during such a prolonged period have left many firms in a fragile position. The recovery is expected to accelerate in coming months as pressures from the pandemic ease further and protocols are relaxed. The planned reopening of regional festivals will further encourage the return of regional tourists. In the short term, however, the sector will remain vulnerable to the evolving situation with the pandemic. 11. The adverse impact of COVID-19 brings an opportunity to rethink the sector and work to establish a more sustainable and resilient industry that leverages high value blue tourism markets . Developing a climate-resilient, environmentally sustainable blue tourism product is vital to the future of the industry. While the high-level policy framework related to tourism is broadly adequate in the OECS countries, most countries face important regulatory and enforcement constraints. Coastal habitats are not effectively protected, with a need to strengthen regulations, monitoring, and enforcement. Concerns about the coastal environment include inadequate waste management, marine pollution, and beach erosion. In addition, local communities have expressed concerns that the sector has not been sufficiently inclusive with most activities concentrated in a few enclaves, and despite the importance of women’s participation in the sector, significant gender wage gaps persist. Transitioning to a more sustainable and higher-value tourism model that embraces blue tourism will be a process, requiring a mix of policy changes, marketing, capacity building, and infrastructure development. Sustainability and blue economy approaches need to be mainstreamed into existing regional and national tourism policies. This will require improved inter- agency collaboration on issues of environment, tourism, fisheries, transport, and health. To foster a more inclusive tourism model, Micro-Small-Medium Enterprises (MSME) in the tourism sector should adopt COVID-19 safety protocols as well as access finance to invest in productivity enhancements, adoption of digital technologies, and upgrades and diversification of their products to meet the higher standards of blue tourism markets. 12. Regional collaboration in tourism will be critical during the current economic recovery period. A regional approach can help rebuild the Eastern Caribbean brand in the aftermath of the pandemic. Regionally aligned and consistent COVID-19 safety protocols will create streamlined systems for travelers and opportunities for intra-regional tourism (especially in the yachting and cruise sectors). Across the Caribbean, the fisheries sector is strategically important as a source of livelihoods, employing more than 300,000 people directly and indirectly, and contributing to food security and poverty alleviation.16 The sector earns more than US$295 million per year from exports. It saves the region at least three times as much in foreign exchange because production volumes are four times the volume of export, accounting for close to 5 percent of GDP in some countries. In 2017, marine capture production in the region totaled 156,691 metric tons with an estimated value of more than US$518 million.17 Shrimp, queen conch, reef fishes, deep slope, bank fishes, and coastal and offshore pelagic comprise the bulk of the capture and represent the most economically important species in the sector. Fish are a major source of protein, especially for rural communities, where poverty is higher relative to national averages. In general, fish consumption is high in Caribbean Community (CARICOM) countries, and in most cases higher than the world average, ranking second only to poultry as a source of protein. Despite the immense importance of this sector for Caribbean economies and their nutritional needs, significant gaps concerning the economic contribution of women to the sector persist. 14. Climate change effects are particularly acute for fisheries and the marine ecosystems upon which they depend. Fish distribution is changing and important habitats such as coral reefs are being damaged or lost, leading to declining stocks, due to the impacts of climate change. In addition, landing sites are highly vulnerable to extreme weather events linked to climate change, such as hurricanes and cyclones, as well as prolonged effects from sea-level rise and increases in ocean temperature. Many species harvested by Caribbean states are highly migratory and transboundary in their distribution. The close proximity of Caribbean states to one another, coupled with a combined Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) of 2,060,721 km,2 means that many of the resources come from shared stocks. For that reason, realistic assessment and effective management of the stocks are only possible through regionally coordinated efforts. 15. Aquaculture, the world’s fastest-growing food production system, has considerable potential to generate new blue economy jobs in OECS countries, while addressing climate change impacts on wild fish stocks and diversifying local economies away from tourism and traditional capture fisheries. However, climate change, including higher ocean temperature, salinity, and acidification, and increased frequency and intensity of storms, is likely to hamper aquaculture production. The commercial aquaculture sector in the Eastern Caribbean remains small-scale, focusing on seamoss mariculture and on-shore farming of tilapia and shrimp, with negligible contribution to overall fish and seafood production in the region.18 The lack of scale in off-shore aquaculture is due to challenging environmental conditions and the absence of adequate space demarcation and supporting policies. Onshore farming is constrained by scarce flat land and freshwater shortages. 16. Three important developments have opened the way for expanding aquaculture in the OECS. These are (1) new technological options for growing a wider range of species that are being piloted in, and for, small islands, (2) higher export of seafood products such as seamoss, and (3) greater demand for farmed seafood in local markets. Marine pollution remains a major problem in the OECS region, damaging valuable natural capital, lowering quality of life for local people and visitors, and harming the tourism and fisheries sectors. An estimated 80 percent of marine pollution comes from land-based sources, mostly untreated wastewater, litter, and agricultural run-off. But industrial pollutants, heavy metals, and discharge from hotels and cruise and cargo ships also contribute to the problem. A recent study noted that an average of 2,014 litter items per kilometer were found on selected Caribbean beaches and coastal areas compared to a global average of 573.20 Much of the region’s marine litter emanates from landfills due to climate-induced storms and coastal flooding that disperse pollution into the sea. 18. Although many Caribbean SIDS have banned use of single-use plastic bags and Styrofoam, lack of integral planning for the waste sector and mechanisms to enforce the rules persist. Solid waste management and disposal are substandard as well. Upstream, revenues received by waste management authorities are insufficient to cover collection costs and to finance installation, maintenance, and replacement of waste collection bins and vehicles in a timely manner. Downstream, waste is often disposed of informally in waterways and open-air dumps, from which it flows direct to the ocean during climate-induced events such as storms and coastal flooding. Recycling, meanwhile, remains a nascent industry. Improving waste management will become even more important as the OECS endeavors to attract back a shrinking number of international tourists. 19. Sargassum mega-blooms associated with changes to ocean currents and temperature have become a major threat to tourism by polluting beaches. There are recent efforts between OECS countries, the private sector, and scientific community to support circular economy solutions that allow collecting, composting, and converting sargassum into byproducts such as soil fertilizer that has commercial demand. This investment also generates climate benefits, through sargassum’s role in mitigating storm surges and reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from agriculture due it being a low-carbon alternative to synthetic fertilizers. 20. Transboundary considerations in three key sectors—tourism, fisheries, and aquaculture—require special attention to ensure equitable economic recovery, climate resilience, and sustained progress over the long term. OECS countries share common fish stocks, which means that both climate change and unsustainable practices in the waters of one country have implications for all. Similarly, shared ocean currents bring litter and sargassum to the beaches of all three aforementioned states, which hampers the tourism industry. A regional approach is essential to address climate vulnerability and these transboundary issues, and to ensure that one state’s efforts are not undone by the actions of another. 21. The OECS Commission had played a strategic role in strengthening regional integration, but some of its initiatives need updating. The Commission took important steps to promote a common approach to ocean governance with adoption of the Eastern Caribbean Regional Ocean Policy (ECROP) and its accompanying Strategic Action Plan. The ECROP provides a basis for better coordination and management of shared marine resources, but there remains a need for further harmonization and strengthening of the regional regulatory framework in certain sectors. For example, the current OECS Regional Fisheries Policy dates from 1999 and does not include a regional strategy to address climate change impacts nor Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated (IUU) fishing. II. SERIES OF PROJECTS DESCRIPTION 25. UBEC will be implemented as a SOP in three distinct five-year phases over a fifteen-year period (each project will have a life cycle of five years) involving multi-sectoral blue economy investments to support Caribbean countries to strengthen their climate resilience and accelerate economic recovery from the impacts of COVID-19. This first Phase of the UBEC SOP will be financed with National IDA credits and grants, leveraging Regional IDA resources on a 1:2 ratio and further supplemented by PROBLUE Recipient Executed Trust Fund (RETF) grants. Phase 1 of UBEC, to be implemented between fiscal years 2022 and 2028, involves Grenada, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and the OECS Commission and focuses on three interrelated blue economy sectors: tourism, fisheries, and waste management. Phase I will support national and regional activities with a total financing envelope of US$56 million in IDA credits (national and regional) and grants (regional) along with US$4 million in PROBLUE trust fund grants. Phase 2 of UBEC will involve additional OECS economies and other Caribbean economies (IDA and IBRD) and is programmed to begin in FY23. 26. The UBEC SOP will address the persistent gender pay gap of women, who are key players in all sectors of the OECS blue economy and who are directly harmed by deterioration of coastal and marine ecosystems. A 2017 survey37 revealed that women’s mean compensation was 10 percent lower in Saint Lucia and 23 percent lower in Grenada. Women are traditionally employed in domestic, low-pay, female- dominated occupations (74 percent in Saint Lucia and 82 percent in Grenada), while men tend to occupy more financially rewarding occupations. Although female tertiary school enrollment is twice that of the male level in most OECS countries, the high human capital of Caribbean women has not translated into competitive opportunities in the labor market. For example, in the Caribbean tourism sector, women were estimated to account for more than 54 percent of all hospitality employees but to own a smaller share of tourism-related enterprises.38 Women are more likely to lose income due to closure of hotels and restaurants during the COVID-19 pandemic because they fill jobs that are easily furloughed in the absence of customers. Moreover, the fisheries sector is highly susceptible to hydro-meteorological climate risks that threaten the livelihoods of women who depend on income from pre- and post-harvesting activities. These gaps are symptomatic of factors shaping labor market outcomes for women, such as sectoral segregation, teenage pregnancy, early marriage, and lack of access to finance. The UBEC SOP is explicitly designed to pro-actively help women enter the more productive tourism sectors and close the gap in access to finance by facilitating equitable amounts of funding to women-owned MSMEs through the MSME matching grants program. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Series of Projects Design 43. The SOP design reflects lessons learned from previous World Bank projects. Specifically, UBEC reflects lessons from the following projects: CROP (P159653), OECS Regional Tourism Competitiveness Project (P152117), Jamaica Foundation for Competitiveness and Growth Project (P147665), Caribbean Digital Transformation Project (P171528), OECS Regional Health Project (P168539), Pacific Islands Regional Oceanscape Program SOP (P131655, P152938, P151780), Pacific Resilience Program (P156344, P156335), and the Statistical Innovation and Capacity Building in the Pacific Islands SOP (P168122). 44. Specific lessons learned incorporated into the UBEC SOP design include: a) A regional approach: Persistent capacity constraints of small island states in the OECS region call for a regional approach to address transboundary issues such as marine pollution, fisheries management, and sustainable tourism. While activities vary by country, depending on need and priority, harmonization of country and regional activities is a central focus of the design. In addition, experience in the OECS region has shown that regional approaches cannot be managed successfully without the involvement of regional institutions, which is one of the reasons the OECS Commission is part of this SOP. In addition, flexibility in project design was highlighted as important for regional projects where participants are starting from different places and have varying levels of capacity. This lesson is reflected in the differentiated list of activities each country has selected and the relative 18 The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) timing of implementation for each set of sectoral activities; thus reflecting each country’s unique starting point and priorities. b) Involvement of the Ministry of Finance: Proactively involving ministries of finance in national projects was correlated with better performance because this ministry is better able to assess the financial aspects of the project and the viability of achieving its results. As such, active involvement of the ministries of finance in the three participant countries reflects this lesson learned. c) Strengthened ocean governance: Lessons learned include the importance of (1) robust ocean policies and institutional frameworks, capacity building, and enhanced regional collaboration to achieve sustained growth and resilience, and (2) integrated, multi-stakeholder approaches that support the blue economy and ensure effective ocean governance. d) Matching grants: The matching grants design incorporates lessons from the 2016 World Bank review of matching grants40 programs (Box 1) as well as experience with similar programs in Zambia, Jamaica, and Ghana. Lessons learned include (1) MSME support programs achieve better results when they provide intensive, sustained, and custom-tailored business development support, (2) MSMEs must have a clear commercial link with their markets, (3) establishing a clear focus of growth-oriented MSMEs can improve employment and diversification outcomes, (4) professional management of MSME programs promotes accountability and entrepreneurial dynamism in the way MSMEs are selected and supported, and (5) effectiveness of technical assistance and grants for MSMEs in times of economic crisis is critical, especially if they work within regional supply chains. e) Strengthened implementation capacity: The SOP will reflect lessons learned from previous operations to ensure that a fully staffed PIU is established at both the national and regional levels and complemented by technical advisory consultants, given the multi-sectoral nature of the SOP. f) Reduced implementation inter-dependencies: While the SOP shares a common regional framework and approach, the design enables each country and regional institution to implement its own program of activities at its own pace. This will mitigate the risks of all recipients having to move in tandem with implementation. g) Emphasis on stand-alone SOP implementation: To simplify implementation, each country and the Commission will have a separate POMs and CERC Manuals providing clear guidance as to how each entity will implement its set of activities. 40 World Bank Group 2016B. 19 The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) Box 1. IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis 54. Technical Analysis: The technical design of the SOP is supported by analytical work carried out by the World Bank. A series of in-depth regional and national sector studies, funded by PROBLUE, were completed during preparation to inform the SOP design, including (1) blue economy roadmaps, (2) diagnostic assessments of fisheries governance and management, (3) a “deep dive” study of waste management including marine plastics debris, and (4) detailed assessment of opportunities, challenges, and policy gaps facing a transition to a blue economy through tourism. Based on these studies and consultation with the clients, SOP activities were defined and harmonized at the regional level. The OECS Commission worked closely to align activities and ensure that their areas of focus were well suited to their comparative advantage. Finally, designers applied standardization across activities to ensure a regional scope, as reflected in the shared results framework. The SOP is designed to support economic recovery and jobs generation in the short term by investing in diversification and enhancing sustainability and productivity in the tourism, fisheries, and waste management sectors. For the longer term, the SOP seeks to strengthen and expand the competitiveness of the region’s blue economy and resilience of the shared coastal and marine natural resources. The SOP is predicated on a number of policies and programs in Grenada, Saint Lucia, and SVG, including National Oceans Policy and Strategic Action Plans; Blue Economy Policies for sustainable management of oceans and coastal resources; Tourism policies; Fisheries and Aquaculture Policies; and Waste Management Policies. The expected benefits from this SOP include (1) greater contribution from high-quality and better-managed marine and coastal assets in the long term, (2) healthier coastal ecosystems, (3) improved coastal and marine ecosystem management, which will ultimately support more productive fisheries and a more sustainable tourism industry, (4) increased production of sustainable goods and services by sector stakeholders, (5) MSMEs and communities reflecting increased sustainable productivity, and (6) increased regional integration for a vibrant blue economy. 55. Economic and Financial Analysis (EFA): The UBEC SOP’s economic (income and welfare) benefits include (1) additional income for fishers gained from skills-training programs, (2) extra income by the willingness to pay (WTP) of visitors if there is improvement in Marine Protected Area (MPA) conservation, and (3) income earned from the reduction of waste in the islands (see full Economic and Financial Analysis in Annex 6). The SOP’s focus on developing the work skills of local people concerning sustainable practices in fisheries and aquaculture, high-value tourism, and waste management will contribute to these benefit streams. In addition, the emphasis on enhancing marine-based tourism infrastructure and waste collection infrastructure (Component 2.2) will improve visitor experience and contribute to benefit streams 2 and 3, listed above. The EFA shows that SOP-supported investments will bring substantial financial and economic benefits to local communities and other potential beneficiaries in the SOP area and the Caribbean in general. For the EFA, a "business-as-usual" (BAU) baseline assumes future development trends follow those of the past with no changes in policies and practices. The incremental economic analysis shows that the net present value (NPV) would reach US$118 million (lower bound) and US$173 million (upper bound) in the baseline scenario (20 years, between 6 and 9 percent discount rate). The investments evaluated for the economic and financial analysis would generate a BC ratio between 24 The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) 3.16 and 3.87 and an Internal Rate of Return (IRR) between 37.19 percent and 37.83 percent. The results of the quantitative simulations are robust in terms of sensitivity analyses. Assuming a 15-year project, the NPV varies between US$90 million and US$125 million, a BC between 2.68 and 3.15, and an IRR between 36.58 percent and 37.25 percent (between 6 and 9 percent discount rate). Results for the 10-year project still show positive impacts: the NPV varies between US$52 million and US$70 million, a BC ratio between 2.0 and 2.26, and an IRR between 33.43 percent and 34.17 percent (6 and 9 percent discount rate). The Environmental and Social Risk rating for the SOP is Substantial. This is due to the potential adverse impacts of investment in sensitive areas and waste management, a dispersed geographical area of implementation, potential restriction of access to natural resources, elite capture, and harm to the livelihoods of vulnerable populations. There is also the potential for displacement of people. In general, investments spread across three countries risk environmental and social harm if there is failure of measures to coordinate activities and guard against disruption. 60. The environmental risk is Substantial. This is due to assessed risks related to investments aimed at improving tourist sites and harvest and post-harvest fisheries facilities; construction of a waste separation, recovery, and treatment center; and construction of a composting center. Risks are expected to be site-specific and related to (1) waste management, handling, and disposal, (2) community health and safety, (3) workers’ occupational health and safety, and (4) air, water, and noise pollution. Depending on scale and location, construction- related impacts may occur in ecologically sensitive areas on land or in water. Activities such as rehabilitation of coastal ecosystems and tours and research at Ramsar sites may disturb marine habitats and generate occupational health and safety risks. Risks related to upgrading landfills include management of emissions of leachate, toxins, and greenhouse gases. While Fish Aggregating Devices (FADs) can reduce pressure on reefs and improve local food security, FADs can risk making stock vulnerable to overfishing by aggregating individuals, altering migration routes, and redistributing stocks. The capacity of the PIUs in the three countries to manage the environmental risks is limited but will be strengthened through dedicated support from the World Bank throughout implementation. 61. The social risk rating is Substantial. The main social risks and impacts include (1) dispersed geographic area, which encompasses onshore and offshore areas of three countries in the OECS, including small islands and cays that are difficult to reach and present challenges to monitoring of social standards, (2) potential restriction of access to fishers, tourism operators, and yacht personnel to areas in which they usually earn a livelihood, due to strengthened regulations and policies, (3) negative reaction of formal and informal businesses in the three sectors to new policies and standards (such as licensing systems for tourism operators, SPS applicable to people in the fisheries sector, and a financial framework for waste management) and the potential that they could be cost-restrictive, (4) potential exclusion of some groups from SOP benefits and activities (particularly access to the MSMEs’ matching grants schemes, COAST insurance, and infrastructure investments) if there is inadequate socialization of SOP activities, (5) potential land acquisition, involuntary resettlement and/or economic displacement at the subprogram level, particularly related to infrastructure works under subcomponent 2.2, (6) limited adverse site-specific impacts from civil works, and (7) community health and safety and labor conditions across a wide geographical area. Additional risks due to the ongoing impact of COVID-19, particularly in case of a prolonged recovery period, will continue to be assessed during implementation. 65. Macroeconomic risk to achieving the SOP objectives is deemed to be High. While some Caribbean countries undertook deep and sustained fiscal consolidation to put their public debt stock on a downward trajectory 27 The World Bank Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Caribbean (UBEC) (P171833) over recent years, some reversal of that trend occurred with the onset of the pandemic and related fiscal challenges. Across the OECS, economic recovery is fragile, and countries remain vulnerable to shocks and high public debt. In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic continues to pose major risks to the region directly through damage to public health and indirectly through the drop in tourism activities. Regional collaboration will be needed to improve communication regarding lockdowns and visitor protocols. Recovery will also depend on improving vaccination rates. Economic recovery in OECS countries also relies heavily on revival of economic momentum in major countries from which tourists come, notably the United States and Europe. Exogenous shocks from global markets and natural disasters could dampen growth and constrain fiscal space, leaving limited resources to support SOP activities. The SOP is designed to stimulate economic recovery by creating new jobs in the blue economy, expanding sustainable and resilient tourism, and supporting sustainable fisheries management and improved waste management. The TA provided by development partners should help governments in the region maintain fiscal sustainability, build economic resilience, and manage macroeconomic risks. 66. The SOP will be implemented at the national and regional levels. Participating countries are Grenada, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and the OECS Commission as the regional entity. The implementation arrangements are designed to ensure clear ownership of the SOP and strengthen regional and inter-ministerial coordination among and within the participating countries. At the national level, the MoF of Saint Lucia, MOIID of Grenada, and MoT of SVG will be responsible for implementation, in close collaboration with the line ministries for each sector. The OECS Commission will be responsible for regional coordination. From the FM perspective, the project will be managed by National Integrated Program Planning in the Department of Finance in Saint Lucia; Accountant General’s office in Grenada; and the PSIPMU in the MoF in SVG, respectively. 2. The PSC will have oversight of the SOP. The PSC will consist of a Permanent Secretary-level representative (e.g., Director) from the ministry of finance of each participating country and a representative from the OECS Commission (director general or delegate), who will be responsible for (1) oversight of annual workplans, (2) discussion and agreement on regional policy issues related to the blue economy including guidance to ensure that any legislation agreed upon at the regional level is quickly enacted in each country, (3) oversight of regional fiduciary matters, and (4) oversight of reporting from national PIUs. The PSC will meet quarterly. The chair of the PSC will rotate among the three participating countries with the OECS Commission acting as Secretariat and member of the PSC. 3. A regional PIU will be established in the OECS Commission. It will be responsible for facilitating regional coordination of the SOP and implementing the regional MSME matching grants scheme. The regional PIU will be responsible for (1) providing input and coordinating support to the PSC on strengthening and harmonizing regional policies among participating countries to support economic recovery, jobs growth, and ecosystem health (Subcomponent 1.1), (2) managing the regional MSME matching grants program to support MSMEs in the region (Subcomponent 2.1a), (3) managing regional M&E and consolidating national M&E data received from the national PIUs, and (4) carrying out fiduciary functions for regional activities (Component 1). The OECS Commission PIU will be staffed with the following positions: (1) a full- time project manager who specializes in M&E, (2) a full-time procurement specialist, (3) a full-time environmental and social specialist, and (4) a full-time financial management specialist. Additional technical specialists will be retained on an as-needed basis to support implementation of regional activities. The regional MSME matching grants program will also have an implementation team comprised of a senior grant advisor/deputy project manager, a grants advisor, a financial management assistant, and an administrative assistant. 4. Each country will establish a national PIU responsible for implementing national-level activities and coordinating with relevant national ministries. National-level PIUs will have a full-time project manager who will be housed at MoF in Saint Lucia, MoT in SVG, and at MOIID in Grenada. The full-time project manager will be responsible for ensuring delivery of all national activities, including obtaining approvals for procurement and ESF instruments, data collection, and reporting on national M&E indicators. The OECS marine and coastal assets offer a unique opportunity for strengthening the region’s blue economy and recovering from the economic impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic through increased growth, jobs, and inclusion. The OECS marine ecosystems (coral reefs, mangroves, sea grasses, sandy beaches, and rocky shores) provide a wide array of goods and services (seafood, tourism, coastal protection, and climate resilience from reefs and mangroves, and GHG sequestration). These have long been undermined by unsustainable anthropogenic practices, including poorly planned coastal development, climate change, and ineffective management that has allowed overexploitation. The economic impact of COVID-19 in the Eastern Caribbean has underscored the importance of building back safer, more sustainable and climate-resilient tourism, fisheries, and waste management sectors. 2. Regional-level activities under UBEC consider priorities expressed by participating countries and the experience of the OECS Commission in advancing the blue economy to date. Consideration includes lessons learned from several projects such as the CROP and the OECS Regional Tourism Competitiveness Project. UBEC regional activities are designed to help harmonize regulations and boost cooperation. This regional approach is essential to address transboundary issues such as fisheries, marine litter, and climate resilience, and to coordinate in shared priority areas such as tourism marketing and entrepreneurial development, particularly for women. SOP Components 3. Component 1: Strengthening Governance, Policies, and Capacity Building (US$1.6 million IDA): A robust enabling environment that unlocks and sustains economic growth, job generation, environmental health, and climate resilience is critical to the viability and competitiveness of blue economy sectors. The pandemic has further underscored the urgency for these reforms and inter-regional cooperation. This component focuses on national and regional policy frameworks, strategies, institutions, and capacity building by the public sector to support economic recovery and jobs and improve the management of natural assets that contribute to the region’s marine environmental health and climate resilience. 4. Subcomponent 1.1: Strengthening Regional Policies, Institutions, and Coordination (US$1.6 million IDA). Harmonizing regulations and government procedures and boosting regional cooperation will allow for greater economies of scale in the region. The OECS Commission plays a strategic role in strengthening integration in the Eastern Caribbean. The regional approach is essential to address potential transboundary threats; ensure that national management efforts are not undermined by other countries’ efforts; facilitate development of sustainable, safe, and nutritious seafood; foster a prosperous and equitable tourism industry; and promote efficient and reliable solid waste management regionally. These objectives are particularly important for maintaining food security and livelihoods across the region, intra- regional tourism, climate resilience, and the coordination of initiatives to phase out single-use plastics. This subcomponent will support: 5. An update of the OECS Common Tourism Policy to increase competitiveness based on a blue economy approach. OECS regional interventions in the tourism sector will also include development of standards for beaches, marinas, and boat operators; implementation and coordination of activities outlined in the regional marketing strategy completed under the OECS Regional Tourism Competitiveness Project; and 48 development of regional knowledge exchanges for blue tourism activities. The SOP will leverage existing standards and certification programs at the regional and national levels to improve implementation, management, and enforcement. The OECS Common Tourism Policy and the operating standards for tourism hotspots such as beaches and marinas will incorporate guidance on conducting business in a sustainable and climate-smart manner, for example, outlining the need to conduct climate change impact assessments for new sites and updating old sites to best adapt to changing conditions. 6. Implementation of an updated OECS Regional Fisheries Policy, including the development of a regional strategy for IUU fishing. This will include strengthened capacity for intergovernmental coordination. The OECS Regional Fisheries Policy will enhance safety at sea protocols to buffer against heavy storms and shifting fishing sites due to change in currents and ocean temperatures. 7. Coordination and harmonization of regional SPS measures by developing a model for the movement of key seafoods among OECS member states and providing support to countries to adapt to the standards (including tools, training, and diagnostics). Transporting catch from shifting fishing sites may also threaten seafood quality if sufficient fuel and refrigeration ice are not available for the lengthened journey. A harmonized OECS SPS will take such considerations into account to ensure that seafood products being sold and consumed in the region are safe. 8. A mapping exercise towards developing a Waste Management Strategy and Implementation Plan for the OECS. This plan will clearly lay out a vision for waste management in the region, documents OECS member states have done and are doing, and what remains to be done to get them where they need to be. This will help the OECS define a clear path towards the St George’s Declaration Goal: integrated approaches to chemicals and waste management through sustainable consumption, production, and management practices that reduce waste and pollution. Shifting currents and increasing ocean temperatures and densities due to climate change also have a role in transporting solid waste, particularly plastics and microplastics, across the region. A regional OECS Waste Management Strategy will serve to improve environmental monitoring of plastics pollution to identify sources and tackle them. 9. Subcomponent 2.1a: Regional MSME Matching Grants Program (US$6.5 million total: US$5.5 million IDA, US$1 million PROBLUE). The Regional MSME grants program aims to finance BDS and matching grants to increase productivity and job creation and upgrade the capabilities and resilience of MSMEs and communities in the blue economy value chains (such as tourism, fisheries and aquaculture, and waste management). To strengthen resilience, the SOP will support MSMEs in undertaking concrete climate- related analyses of their operations. For instance, if a new tourism venture relies on a particular beach or coral reef patch, climate change analysis could provide insight into rates of coral bleaching or soil erosion that might potentially hinder the venture. MSMEs in the region, particularly ones that are women-led or owned, have suffered badly during the COVID-19 crisis because they lack capacity, cash flows, and diversity of market and resources. COVID-19 brings an opportunity for MSMEs to build back better while respecting the integrity of the marine and coastal resources in line with the region’s blue economy vision. The Regional MSME grants program seeks to fund BDS and investments that aim to support tourist operators, fish workers, and coastal communities to increase employment, productivity, and innovation. 49 This will be accomplished while ensuring sustainable management of marine and coastal resources and enhancing market linkages and business relations with other value chain actors, with an emphasis on women-led and owned businesses. 12. A special focus will be development of MSMEs that work within regional supply chains across two or more of the participating countries. Because the OECS countries in the SOP face many similar challenges, the grants will favor supply chains that work across two or more countries, if possible. Also, the Regional MSME matching grants program will consider national or regional level co-financing by individual firms receiving the BDS, with co-financing amounts depending on the size of firms, consortiums, or communities. The BDS will be tailored to the specific growth needs of each MSME beneficiary as well as addressing gender-related information asymmetry. The support provided will include (1) an initial diagnostic on the MSME’s competitiveness potential and constraints, (2) problem solving and coaching on identified business challenges, including those that particularly affect women-owned firms, (3) provision of specific technical support, such as quality improvement or standards development, (4) improvements in know-how of buyer and market requirements, terms, and conditions, and (5) mentorship to successfully complete grant applications, including mobilizing the capital for the beneficiary’s portion of the matching grant. To ensure sustained engagement, MSMEs will commit to taking part in the Regional MSME grants program for a minimum of one year. 13. The Government of Grenada aims to bolster the country’s recovery and blue economy by placing a focus on economic growth, generation of jobs, and diversification, while continuing efforts on the climate change adaptation and mitigation fronts. Grenada’s high levels of economic, social, environmental, and climate vulnerability have been amplified by the economic setbacks of the pandemic. The tourism sector—the main pillar of economic growth in Grenada accounting for 57 percent of GDP and 52 percent of employment in 2019—has been the hardest hit in terms of revenue and job loss. The fisheries and aquaculture sector also endured significant revenue falls because of the pandemic. Grenada’s inadequate solid and plastic waste management system is a major contributor to marine pollution and threatens all economic sectors, particularly those dependent on healthy marine ecosystems. The UBEC SOP, building on these efforts, is designed to stimulate economic recovery in Grenada and support marine and coastal resilience by strengthening the sustainability and competitiveness of three critical and interconnected sectors: tourism, fisheries and aquaculture, and waste management. UBEC could have spill-over benefits for Grenada by improving regional governance and capacity building led by the OECS to address transboundary fisheries, intra-regional tourism, and marine litter. 2. Grenada has one of the largest fishing fleets in the eastern Caribbean, with most of the effort and catches from commercial operations destined for export. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimated annual catches at 2,550 tons in 2017, 70 percent being tuna and tuna-like fishes. Catches of small pelagic fish (jacks) that are used as bait in offshore fisheries are declining mainly due to climate change and habitat loss. Longline and trolling are used to target ocean pelagic species, gillnets and fish pots for the demersal fisheries, and beach seine for the inshore pelagic fishery. In 2016 the fishing fleet numbered about 2,028 vessels. Recent expansion of large pelagic fisheries has resulted in a growing number of longline boats. About 2,743 people were directly employed in the fishing sector in 2013, with about 86 percent of fishers working full-time. It is estimated that only 75 percent of fishers in Grenada are registered with the government. Almost all registered fishers are commercial, but their businesses are generally small scale. Most subsistence fishers, meanwhile, do not register. Women make up about 30 percent of registered vessel owners. 3. The Government of Grenada is attempting to further develop the industry by adopting measures to increase fish exports and improve fisheries trade. In the past decade, fisheries policy has allowed the expansion of production from the ocean pelagic fishery by facilitating greater fleet capacity. The aquaculture sub-sector, on the other hand, is rather nascent. It has typically focused on inland tilapia farming with a most recent focus, albeit in very small quantities, on seamoss. While aquaculture employment records are limited and not adequately reflected in fisheries policy, the sector shows promise for growth and employment generation, particularly for women. 4. Tourism is critical to Grenada’s economy and the COVID-19 pandemic has had a crippling effect on it. Small businesses recorded major declines in 2020. Prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, the annual gross value added from the sector had reached US$339 million with 527,000 visitors (including overnight, cruise, and yacht visitors). Overnight visitors (mainly leisure) generated the bulk of tourism expenditures (82 percent), distantly followed by yacht visitors (15 percent).46 Cruise visitors made a modest contribution (3 percent 46 In 2019, business tourists amounted to 7 percent of the total for overnight business tourists. 54 of total expenditures) despite their large numbers (338,000). The United States has been the most important source market for overnight visitors (38 percent of total visitors) and was growing at the fastest pace, followed by intraregional Caribbean (17 percent) and United Kingdom visitors (15 percent).47 5. If key challenges are met, Grenada could position itself well over the medium term in several high-value markets that leverage blue tourism. Despite the pandemic’s devastation on tourism in 2020 and the first half of 2021, progress in vaccinations in some of Grenada’s key source countries, pent-up demand, and the branding of islands as “safe havens” will help the sector’s recovery in the second half of 2021. But its tourism sector must deal with threats from climate change. Most of its major resorts and significant tourism infrastructure are on the coasts, making them vulnerable to further climate-induced rises in sea level, while the beaches are at risk of continued erosion from the rising waters. Developing a climate- resilient, environmentally sustainable blue tourism product is vital to the overall sustainability of the sector in Grenada. While the country’s high-level policy framework related to tourism is broadly adequate, it has important gaps on regulatory issues. Coastal habitats are not effectively protected, with the need to upgrade regulations, monitoring, and enforcement. Concerns about the coastal environment include waste management, sedimentation, litter, marine pollution, and erosion of beaches. Some tourist operators have noticed a significant decline in reef health. Protection zones could be an important ESF tool. Transitioning to a more sustainable and higher-value tourism model that embraces blue tourism will be a process, requiring a mix of policy changes, marketing, capacity building, and infrastructure development. To ensure this transition is inclusive, MSMEs in the tourism sector could benefit from further help to adopt COVID-19 protocols as well as access to finance to invest in productivity enhancements, adoption of digital technologies, and upgrades and diversification of their products to meet the higher standards of blue tourism markets. 6. Grenada generates about 40,000 tons of waste annually, at a rate of 1.83 kg per capita per day. The collection system, operated by Grenada Solid Waste Management Authority (GSWMA), is well developed, gathering more than 90 percent of generated waste. Along with curbside collection in separate bins, black and blue plastic bags are used in some service areas. The country has two official dumpsites, Perseverance in Grenada and Dumfries in Carriacou island. In 2017, 20,365 vehicles accessed the Perseverance site to discharge approximately 38,500 ton of solid waste. The Government of Grenada has received financing from the Caribbean Development Bank (CDB) since 2019 to convert the Perseverance landfill into a sanitary landfill with rehabilitation of cells, new cell development, and leachate collection and treatment. Private parties in Grenada play an important role in recycling. A deposit-refund system currently in place (mostly for glass bottles) has efficiently raised the recycling rate, although officials cite the lack of government incentives to run the system, which could prolong the use of glass bottles. 7. Grenada’s efforts to minimize plastic waste are commendable. A far-reaching “Non-biodegradable Waste Control Act” provided for a ban on single-use plastics from February 2009 and a ban on the importation of polystyrene, commonly called Styrofoam, from October 2018. Under the campaign of “Pure Grenada,” many hotels, restaurants, and stores have moved to the use of alternative biodegradable products. Grenada was the first among the SIDS to implement a household service charge for SWM services in residential areas. This has provided an important source of funds, 16 percent of all operational costs. 47 Annual growth of U.S. overnight visitors during 2014-2019 amounted to 12 percent compared to 5.5 percent for other overnight visitors. 55 8. Subcomponent 2.1.(b): Expansion of the regional climate-risk insurance for fisheries (US$0.7 million IDA): The SOP will continue to support Grenada to scale up efforts for the COAST fisheries insurance. COAST is an innovative hydro-meteorological and climate-risk insurance product designed to promote food security, enhanced livelihoods, resilient fisheries, and improved fisheries management in the Caribbean. This subcomponent will enable the Caribbean Catastrophe Risk Insurance Initiative (CCRIF) Segregated Portfolio Company (SPC) to continue providing what is the only available catastrophe coverage for the fisheries sector to Grenada. Grenada will be able to select the amount of insurance coverage, depending on its risk profiles and priority needs. This Subcomponent will assist Grenada (1) to purchase COAST parametric insurance for its fisheries and aquaculture sector beyond its current pilot phase (the annual premium is approximately US$100,000 per year), and (2) provide TA to update the fisher registry of those eligible for COAST insurance (all fish workers in the value chain, including processing plants and fish markets, which are operated mostly by women). The insurance covers two parametric categories, Adverse Weather and Tropical Cyclone. 19. Adverse Weather: This category covers losses caused by adverse weather that prevent fish workers from carrying out their usual activities. Payouts are based on measurements of the adverse weather reaching a pre-defined threshold. Measurements could be based on high waves caused by windstorms and/or strong rainfall. Adverse weather events are defined as days in which either the rainfall or the wave height estimates exceeds its respective threshold (differentiated by small and medium boats). The days end when both estimates are below the threshold. Estimated losses are indexed to associated weather measurements to determine payout.49 20. Tropical Cyclone: This category covers livelihood protection measured as direct damage by tropical cyclones events (winds and storm surge) to fishing vessels, fishing equipment, and infrastructure. Payouts are triggered by cyclone-induced strong winds and storm surges reaching a pre-determined threshold. The size of payout is based on the indexed losses of fishing infrastructure, vessels, and fishing equipment associated with observed high winds (a one-minute sustained wind speed at the height of a 10-meter storm surge). 21. COAST is expected to be a game changer by linking insurance coverage to sound fisheries practices. To qualify for benefits, fish workers will need to register with the government. This will advance a much- needed formalization of the sector, especially in terms of inclusiveness, by extending the insurance to fish vendors and processors, who are mostly women. Registration could be a building block for a control 49 Estimated losses are calculated by multiplying the unit value of an individual fishing day by the number of fish workers in the corresponding port or landing site. Country losses are obtained by adding up the individual losses and then aggregating them on a yearly basis, because the model aims to identify years with very bad weather and long interruptions of fishing activities. Events of fewer than three days are not considered in the account of total annual losses. 58 system for granting access to fisheries, regulating fishing effort, and improving fisheries management and subsequently fish stocks and value-chain resilience. These measures could encourage policy reforms in the fisheries sector to strengthen coastal resilience and build a stronger foundation for the blue economy. The size of the payment from the insurance system to a government will depend on the size of the sector, as measured by the number of fish workers formally registered. It is important to note that payouts are not intended to cover major capital damage to fisheries assets. Instead, they are meant to provide cash flow and prevent fish workers from stopping their work after an event. Most importantly, COAST payments will fund business continuity, enabling fish workers to recover more quickly and get back to work. This, in turn, will help the whole island community to recover because fishers play a critical role in providing food after a disaster, particularly in isolated towns. In the event of a payout, the Ministry of Finance of Grenada will follow procedures for management of COAST in line with its current COAST Operational Manual. 22. The insurance covers two parametric categories, Adverse Weather and Tropical Cyclone. 19. Adverse Weather: This category covers losses caused by adverse weather that prevent fish workers from carrying out their usual activities. Payouts are based on the adverse weather reaching a pre-defined threshold. Measurements could be based on high waves caused by windstorms and/or strong rainfall events. Adverse weather events are defined as days in which either the rainfall or the wave height estimates exceeds their respective thresholds (differentiated by small and medium boats). The days end when both estimates are below the threshold. Estimated losses are indexed to associated weather measurements to determine payout.56 20. Tropical Cyclone: This category covers livelihood protection measured as direct damage by tropical cyclone events (winds and storm surge) to fishing vessels, fishing equipment, and infrastructure. Payouts are triggered by tropical cyclone-induced strong winds and storm surges reaching a pre-determined threshold. The size of payout is based on the indexed losses of fishing infrastructure, vessels, and fishing equipment associated with high winds (one-minute sustained wind speed at the height of a 10-meter storm surge). 21. COAST is expected to be a game-changer by linking insurance coverage to sound fisheries practices. To qualify for benefits, fish workers need to register with the government. This will propel a much-needed formalization of the sector, especially in terms of inclusiveness, by extending the insurance to fish vendors and processors, who are predominantly women. Registration could be a building block for a control system for granting access to fisheries, regulating fishing effort, and improving fisheries management and subsequently fish stocks and value-chain resilience. These measures could encourage policy reforms in the fisheries sector to strengthen coastal resilience and build a stronger foundation for the blue economy. 22. It is important to note that the payouts are not intended to cover major capital damage to fisheries assets. Instead, they provide cash flow and prevent fish workers from stopping their activities after an event. In addition, the range of the insurance system’s payment to a government will vary depending on the size of the sector, as measured by the number of fish workers registered. Most importantly, COAST payments fund business continuity, enabling fish workers to recover more quickly and get back to work. This, in turn, will help the whole island community to recover because fishers play a critical role in providing food after a disaster, particularly in isolated towns. Subcomponent 2.1.(2): Expansion of the regional climate-risk insurance for fisheries (US$1.5 million IDA): The SOP will continue to support SVG to scale up its efforts concerning the COAST fisheries insurance. COAST is an innovative hydro-meteorological and climate risk insurance product designed to promote food security, enhanced livelihoods, resilient fisheries, and improved fisheries management in the Caribbean. This subcomponent will enable the CCRIF SPC to continue providing SVG with the only available catastrophe coverage for the fisheries sector as a measure to increase fisheries resilience. SVG will be able to select the amount of insurance coverage, depending on its risk profiles and priority needs. This subcomponent will assist SVG to (1) purchase COAST parametric insurance for its fisheries and aquaculture sector beyond its current pilot phase (the annual premium is approximately US$100,000), and (2) update the registry of fishers eligible for COAST insurance (all fish workers in the value chain including processing plants and fish markets, which are operated mostly by women). The insurance covers two parametric categories, Adverse Weather and Tropical Cyclone. 20. Adverse Weather: This category covers losses caused by adverse weather that prevent fish workers from carrying out their usual activities. Payouts are based on measurements of the adverse weather reaching a pre-defined threshold. Measurements can be based on high waves caused by windstorms and/or strong rainfall events. Adverse Weather events are defined as days in which either the rainfall or the wave height estimate exceeds its respective threshold (differentiated by small and medium boats). The days end when 73 both estimates are below the threshold. Estimated losses will be indexed to associated weather measurements to determine payout.63 21. Tropical Cyclone: This category covers livelihood protection measured as direct damage by tropical cyclone events (winds and storm surge) to fishing vessels, fishing equipment, and infrastructure. Payouts will be triggered by tropical cyclone-induced strong winds and storm surges reaching a pre-determined threshold. The size of payout will be based on the indexed losses of fishing infrastructure, vessels, and fishing equipment associated with high winds (one-minute sustained wind speed at the height of a 10- meter storm surge). 22. COAST is expected to be a game-changer by linking insurance coverage to sound fisheries practices. To qualify for benefits, fish workers need to register with the government. This will advance a much-needed formalization of the sector, especially in terms of inclusiveness, by extending the insurance to fish vendors and processors, who are mostly women. Registration could be a building block for a control system for granting access to fisheries, regulating fishing effort, and improving fisheries management and subsequently fish stocks and value-chain resilience. These measures could encourage policy reforms in the fisheries sector to strengthen coastal resilience and build a stronger foundation for the blue economy. 23. It is important to note that the payouts generated for the two parametric categories are not intended to cover major capital damage to fisheries assets. Instead, they are meant to provide cash flow and prevent fish workers from stopping their activities after an event. The size of a payment from the insurance program to a government will vary depending on the size of the sector, as measured by the number of fish workers formally registered. Most importantly, COAST payments will fund business continuity, enabling fish workers to recover more quickly and get back to work. This, in turn, will help the whole island community to recover because fishers play a critical role in providing food after a disaster, particularly in isolated towns. In the event of a payout, the implementing ministry of SVG will conduct procedures for management of COAST in line with its COAST Operational Manual. 24. The SOP is designed to strengthen the enabling conditions for blue economy, economic recovery, and resilience of selected coastal assets in the three participating island countries (Grenada, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and Saint Lucia). The SOP focuses on three main blue sectors: tourism, fisheries, and waste management. The first phase is designed to support marine and coastal resilience with activities that include investment in coastal infrastructure. The coastal infrastructure investments will promote the coordinated development and conservation of marine and coastal resources to maximize the resultant economic and social welfare equitably without compromising vital ecosystems’ sustainability. Sustainable management of marine and coastal resources, other innovative practices, and policy, regulatory, and institutional enhancements will help countries address environmental threats including plastic pollution, sanitation chemicals, and sargassum. 3. The SOP will operate in pilot sites in Grenada, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines selected based on multiple urgencies, environmental importance and opportunities for existing efforts to be scaled up. Risks for habitat and water quality would increase, making future recovery efforts more challenging and expensive. The incremental economic analysis for the Unleashing the Blue Economy of the Eastern Caribbean SOP shows substantial benefits for beneficiaries in areas served by the SOP, and substantial benefits for Eastern Caribbean society at large. Overall, the Net Present Value (NPV) is projected to reach US$118 million (lower bound) and US$173 million (upper bound) in the baseline scenario (20 years, between 6 and 9 percent discount rate). The investments evaluated for the economic and financial analysis will generate a benefit-cost (BC) ratio between 3.16 and 3.87 and an internal rate of return (IRR) between 37.19 percent and 37.83 percent. Thus, the economic and financial analysis shows that if SOP implementation is effective and efficient, it will bring substantial financial and economic benefits to local communities in the SOP area and the population of Caribbean island countries in general. 27. The results of the quantitative simulations are robust in terms of sensitivity analyses. Assuming a 15-year SOP, the NPV varies between US$90 million and US$125 million, a BC between 2.68 and 3.15, and an IRR between 36.58 percent and 37.25 percent (between 6 and 9 percent discount rate). Results for a 10-year SOP still show positive impacts: the NPV varies between US$52 million and US$70 million, a BC ratio between 2.0 and 2.26, and an IRR between 33.43 percent to 34.17 percent (6 and 9 percent discount rate). Benefit assumptions of the analysis are based recognized studies of the fisheries, aquaculture, tourism, and waste management sectors and Product Mapping and action plan reports of the SOP. 28. Estimates shown correspond to a lower bound because they represent benefit streams derived from Components 1 and 2, applying a very conservative approach. The SOP’s economic value is likely to be higher because the analysis did not include other non-economic global and local benefits such as the resulting improvement in food security. In the qualitative approach, it is important to emphasize that the SOP’s climate adaptation policies such as climate-smart fisheries and aquaculture will also contribute to environmental sustainability, mitigating the impact of climate change on fisheries and aquaculture in the Eastern Caribbean SIDS. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) points out that these policies aim at improving efficiency in the use of natural resources to produce fish and aquatic foods, countering climate vulnerability, and building resilience across the intervened countries.103 In specific, potential multiple-benefit actions would reduce overfishing and excess capacity, implement the ecosystem approach to fisheries and aquaculture management (including Integrated Coastal Zone Management (ICZM) and MPAs), strengthen the knowledge base, and promote integrated aquaculture and agriculture, including use of flooded/saline land and water bodies, among other benefits.104 29. Indeed, we expect that Components 3 and 4 will have additional benefits, but they are difficult to measure due to the social and environmental qualitative approach based on SOP management. It is worth noting that this last component is needed to carry out the main components efficiently to achieve the desired results through the strengthening of governance and capacity for sustainable, productive activities and SOP coordination, monitoring, and evaluation. Additional incremental benefits relate to better public service delivery resulting from coastal and marine resource management and potential investments for local communities.
