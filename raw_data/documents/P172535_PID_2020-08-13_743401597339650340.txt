Introduction and Context Country Context After two decades of consistent economic growth1 and reduction in poverty2 , the COVID-19 crisis is causing the first recession in Colombia in 20 years and the worst in more than a century. The economy is expected to contract by 6.7 percent in 2020. The fiscal deficit is expected to widen to 8.2 percent of GDP and general government debt is expected to increase to 66.5 percent of GDP in 2020. It is anticipated that poverty will increase between 3.0 and 9.1 percentage points, depending on the severity of the economic contraction. The presence of approximately 1.82 million migrants from Venezuela3 places added strain on the resilience of the economy. In response, the Government of Colombia (GoC) rolled out early on a program of measures to save lives, protect the poor and sustain firms, while continuing its focus on prudent macroeconomic policies and medium term development priorities such as competitiveness, resilient and sustainable housing and infrastructure, and strengthened human capital formation.4 As the COVID-19 crisis continues, the GoC is further prioritizing interventions that concurrently tackle these longstanding development priorities and catalyze recovery from COVID-19. Access to affordable and safe housing is critical to addressing C olombia’s poverty reduction and equity objectives. Inadequate and substandard housing is directly correlated with poverty, inequality, inadequate access to public services, and poor education and public health outcomes. Since 2005, Colombia has made progress in nearly halving the quantitative deficit. However, the qualitative deficit has not decreased, and approximately 23 percent of all Colombian households still live in inadequate, substandard housing. Resilient and inclusive housing has been a long- standing priority in Colombia, as it is critical for improving living standards and reducing poverty, while also mitigating disaster risks and promoting climate sustainability. The COVID-19 crisis is now highlighting even further the critical importance and priority of quality housing, especially for the poorest and most vulnerable families living very densely in slum like conditions, to reduce the risks from over-crowding, inadequate sanitation, and contagion. Migration from Venezuela has exacerbated the housing challenge in Colombia. The GoC has embraced migrants from Venezuela and instituted a progressive policy framework to support their economic and social integration. Nonetheless, the rapid arrival of a very large numbers of migrants is straining Colombia’s integrative capacity and financial resources. This strain is severe in the near term, until the long-term positive effects of migration on economic growth materialize, and the COVID-19 crisis is compounding the challenge. Migrants from Venezuela disproportionately settle in areas that tend to be poorer and lack access to public services. Since the onset of COVID-19, migrants from Venezuela have been highly affected through increased risks of contagion, reduced employment, and depressed incomes.7 The cumulative economic impact on migrant households will drastically reduce their capacity to cover basic needs, including housing. Rapid assessments indicate that unemployment among migrant populations has increased from 19.2 percent to 42 percent as a result of the pandemic. As public shelters shut down due to the risks of contagion, and unemployment rises, an increasing number of Venezuelans find themselves living in the streets. Temporary camps8 are emerging in parks around Bogotá, as migrants have been unable to pay rent and are forced to find new places to shelter. Of those Venezuelans with access to housing, approximately 90 percent live in rental units.9 Poorer households are forced to pay daily rates in insecure and informal transactions that are 20–50 percent higher than in the formal market. International experience shows that most migrants and refugees stay permanently in their host countries, even after conditions in their countries of origin have changed, highlighting the importance of a focus on long-term integration, including through adequate housing. The ability to safely house poor, vulnerable and migrant households in Colombia is complicated by the country’s high exposure to natural disasters and extreme weather events related to climate change. Sectoral and Institutional Context Colombia has developed a strong legal and institutional framework that supports the efficient and sustainable housing development. This framework rests on three key pillars. Specifically: • First, the Law 3 of 1991 successfully created a framework for private sector-led housing development, resulting in a significant increase in new housing development and homeownership. GoC support for housing under this framework of market-based housing development solutions has benefited more than 1.2 million households since 1991. • Second, Colombia’s legal and institutional framework promotes sustainable land use development through Municipal Land Use Plans (Planes de Ordenamiento Territorial, POTs) that set guidelines to support efficient and sustainable housing market development – including considerations for hazard risk management. • Third, the National Housing Fund (Fondo Nacional de Vivienda, Fonvivienda) has an established track record for the efficient and transparent management and delivery of subsidies to complement government housing programs. Fonvivienda – a dependent entity the Ministry of Housing, City, and Territory (Ministerio de Vivienda, Ciudad y Territorio, MVCT) - manages an average annual budget of US$525 million and has delivered more than 500,000 housing solutions since 2009 through a variety of programs. Project Description The project’s components include: (a) targeted subsidies under the home improvement subsidy program (CDVD) and a rental subsidy program (SDP) for migrants from Venezuela; (b) the development of basic community infrastructure to improve living conditions for migrants from Venezuela and host communities; and (c) support for housing policy development and institutional strengthening at the national municipal level. A more detailed project description is provided in Annex 2. Component 1: Improve homes and neighborhoods for low-income families (IBRD US$86.0 million, GPG14 US$13.5 million, and GCFF15 US$29.7 million). This component will finance: (a) subsidies to improve housing quality (home improvement subsidies for eligible households under the CDVD Program and rental subsidies for migrants from Venezuela under SDP Program); and (b) targeted infrastructure investments in selected municipalities most affected by migration from Venezuela. The PIU will be responsible for overall coordination and technical oversight of the project and will ensure compliance with fiduciary management, reporting requirements, and monitoring activities, including implementation of the environmental and social management plans. The project will rely on an implementation model that has been successfully used for a decentralized and large-scale delivery of housing programs in Colombia, enabling the GoC to mobilize US$6.3 billion for housing subsidies since 2013between 2012 and 2019. Under this model, Fonvivienda hires a trustee (i.e. a consulting firm) through an open, public, and competitive process and create an autonomous trust. MVCT and Fonvivienda create two committees—a trust committee and a technical committee—which will be responsible for the implementation of Subcomponent 1.1, including managing subsidy resources and contracting the entities responsible for carrying out and overseeing home improvement works. A similar mechanism will be implemented for Subcomponent 1.2, for the construction of new parks and green spaces and SACUDETE centers. Neighborhood upgrading interventions (i.e. childcare centers, local roads and footpaths) under Subcomponent 1.2 will be executed by the MVCT, through the DEUT. Due to the MVCT legal framework, in this case it will be the MVCT (through DEUT) that will hire a trustee (a non-consulting firm) through an open, public, and competitive process for managing resources only. MVCT (through DUET) will be the entity responsible for hiring contractors to execute works and the oversight entities. Figure 1. Institutional Arrangements for Project Implementation (Component 1) Overall, for the implementation of Component 1, which accounts for 87 percent of project funding, five trustees will operate through five autonomous trusts to manage resources for: (a) home improvement subsidies in urban areas; (b) home improvement subsidies in peri-urban and rural areas; (c) temporary rental subsidies; (d) social and public infrastructure executed by MVCT/Fonvivienda; and (e) social and public infrastructure and urban upgrading interventions executed by MVCT/DEUT. Fonvivienda has already hired two trustees, one for (a) and one for (c). Both are ready to start implementing the home improvement subsidies and the temporary rental subsidies. The hiring processes according to the settlor trust instructions for (b), (d) and (e) are planned to be completed within the first six months of project implementation. A single autonomous trust for the execution of interventions cannot be used given the different nature of the programs, their legal basis, and their execution characteristics. The specific implementation details of the project are specified in the draft Operations Manual. .
