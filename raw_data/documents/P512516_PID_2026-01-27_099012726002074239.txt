Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Syria is making progress in its transition towards stabilization, recovery, and reintegration, while continuing to face major challenges after 14 years of conflict. More than one year after the December 2024 political transition, the new Syrian government under the leadership of President Al-Sharaa has maintained overall political stability. Modest economic growth is projected for 2025 and 2026, and Syria has benefitted from the lifting of major economic sanctions and renewed participation in international financial systems and investment discussions. At the same time, the security situation remains fragile, with occasional outbreaks of violence, and the country faces massive development challenges and continued humanitarian needs. Continued international engagement and financing will be critical to support Syria in its post-conflict transition. 2. Sound management of public funds and transparency about their use are prerequisites for the Syrian state to gain the trust of its citizens and the international community as it seeks to mobilize resources and deliver services to the population. Fourteen years of conflict and economic mismanagement have weakened the capacity and legitimacy of Syria’s state institutions. A disciplined and transparent approach to public financial management (PFM) will be a critical foundation for Syria’s path to recovery and development. The Ministry of Finance has identified support for PFM reforms as a priority, highlighting the immediate need to update the relevant legal framework, rebuild human capital for core government functions, advance digitalization, and establish accountability and transparency. Sound management of public funds will also be needed to mobilize external assistance for the country’s massive reconstruction needs, with expectations from the international community of adequate oversight of reconstruction investments, payroll, and the flow of funds to provide these resource flows. 3. The World Bank is increasing its support to Syria, with a growing engagement being developed in close collaboration with the government. The World Bank approved a $146 million grant from the International Development Association Page 2 Official Use Only The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) (IDA) in June 2025 for the Syria Electricity Emergency Project as its first financing to the new government. The government and the World Bank are now discussing a strategy to expand this engagement, aligned with the principles and priorities of a draft national recovery plan being developed by the government. The emerging strategy is focusing on three foundations for recovery and sustained economic growth: stabilizing the economy and core government systems; restoring essential services and social stability; and enabling growth and private sector-led recovery. PFM is one cross-cutting priority identified in the draft national recovery plan aligned with these three foundations for recovery and growth. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Fiscal space is severely constrained, undermining the government’s ability to meet people’s needs. In the past fifteen years, revenue collection has shrunk from close to 20 percent before the conflict to less than 5 percent of GDP due to lower oil and tax revenues, a collapse in trade under sanctions, the expansion of the informal economy, and a weak collection capacity. Custom duty collection is marginal despite the heavy economic reliance on imports of commodities since 2011 (World Bank, 2024) and tax revenue relies almost exclusively on direct taxation. Limited fiscal resources and the prioritization of security expenditures crowd out other government spending, especially capital expenditure. From 2012 to 2024, the budgeted fiscal deficit of the Syrian government averaged 10 percent of GDP, excluding off-budget military spending and electricity subsidies, despite subsidy cuts in recent years. Syria’s total debt was around US$27 billion at the end of 2024 (128 percent of GDP), of which US$22.3 billion (104 percent of GDP) was _For Official Use Only_ external. Domestic debt sharply increased to about US$5 billion in March 2025 (24 percent of GDP).1 5. The Legislative Decree 54 of 2006 on Basic Financial Law (BFL) of 2006, still valid until its planned review, establishes the core legal framework for Syria’s PFM system. This legal framework governs the preparation, execution, and oversight of the state budget by various institutions. As part of the Ministry of Finance (MoF’s) commitment to PFM reforms, it has initiated steps to amend the BFL and the project plans to provide a structured and consultative approach to ensure alignment between the law and the envisaged medium to long-term reforms. The Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) report dated March 2006 noted several PFM challenges. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank issued a report in 2010 on “Modernization of Public Financial Management�?. The report noted a modest implementation of the previous IMF and World Bank recommendations. 6. Syria’s PFM systems, institutions, and processes continues to face significant challenges. The World Bank scoping mission to Damascus (May 27-29, 2025) identified the following challenges: automation - transaction processing is mainly manual/paper-based; budgeting - weak preparation, leading to budget overruns; procurement – The legal framework has various gaps and outdated practices, hindering competition and transparency. The procurement management capacity is limited, and institutional arrangements are inefficient; Human resource and payroll managements processes – in need of digitalization and integration; treasury management/Treasury Single Account (TSA) – no consolidated government cash management and absence of a digital interface between Treasury and Central Bank of Syria (CBS); accounting and reporting – outdated standalone system with ad hoc reports. Reports are also not published online; revenue management – low tax revenue collection; public Investment Management – low budget allocations for capital projects; internal Audit – need for systematic risk-based audit approach; and external Audit – audit reports not timely and not published. 7. Without TSA, government cash resources are dispersed across multiple bank accounts. This fragmentation leads to inefficient cash management, as it becomes difficult to consolidate cash balances and optimize their use. It also increases borrowing costs and reduces transparency and accountability. Line ministries and municipalities operate some 3,400 bank accounts at the CBS (as of May 2025). The Central Bank of Syria (CBS) currently uses an in-house Page 3 Official Use Only The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) developed core banking system (Nahas) that has significant limitations and needs to be upgraded to provide the required capabilities for accounting, finance, and payments, as well as the appropriate IT and cyber-security standards. Taxes are collected through MoF branches located in various governorates. In most cases, payments are made in cash, while in some instances, they are made in cheques to the MoF. Non-tax revenues such as administrative fees and licenses are typically paid via bank transfers. 8. There are instances of significant variance between approved budget and actual expenditures at aggregate and composition levels which show that the budget is not being implemented as intended at the approval stage. The root causes of these variances are the degree of realism of revenue forecasting, time taken to complete procurement and payment, and inadequate in-year reporting to identify issues for corrective action. Macro-fiscal analysis and forecasts are still nascent and require development to support annual budget management. There are no economic plans to guide budget preparation. 9. Public procurement is governed by a fragmented legal framework. The legal framework is uncertain and lacks provisions on key aspects such as transparency, conflict of interest, independent complaint mechanisms, value for money; nor has a procurement manual been issued to assist practitioners in proper implementation and compliance. Management capacity and institutional arrangements suffer from inefficiency and a culture of avoidance of responsibility, while procedures and practices show the preferential treatment of state-owned enterprises, weak procurement planning, and a lack of transparency in bid evaluation and contract management. Some gaps and areas of uncertainty in the legal framework have been dealt with by issuing interpretative circulars, an approach that, while _For Official Use Only_ providing a valuable service, can eventually contribute to fragmentation of the legal framework as circulars get proliferated in the absence of a workable database. Despite the presence of some modern procurement principles, the public procurement system continues to face the same weaknesses and shortfalls noted in the World Bank’s Country Procurement Assessment Report (CPAR) of September 2010. 10. Personnel/establishment and payroll management are paper based (documented and shared on excel sheets). While the personnel roll in the various line ministries and paylists are reconciled manually every month, there is currently no unified and comprehensive database of public sector employees. Based on official personnel records for the central government, which excludes staff at the Presidency, the Ministry of Defense, and State-owned Enterprises (SOEs), the Central Bureau of Statistics reports that in 2022 there were 911,955 government employees in central institutions, ministries, and the People’s Assembly. Government expenditures for salaries represented between 15 percent and 30 percent between 2017 and 2023. The transition government intends to integrate employees of the Idlib de facto authorities into the civil service and harmonize the two pay scales. This adds a layer of complexity as there are reportedly wide discrepancies between them. The Ministry of Administrative Development (MoAD) is reviewing the personnel register and HRM rules and currently verifying staff through personnel files provided by line ministries. A Microsoft Access Database is being used with the aim of compiling data for digitizing the personnel roll/establishment register. C. The project will lay the foundation for a rigorous and transparent approach to PFM as Syria embarks on its path to recovery and long-term development. The direct project beneficiary is the MoF, and the ultimate beneficiaries will be the Syrian citizens who will benefit from better strategic allocation and use of public funds for socio-economic development in a transparent manner. The project will not finance activities in disputed territories and conflict- affected areas. In fragile situation such as Syria, it is critical to first establish a robust structure and culture for project oversight, technical leadership, and management of the overall reform program. The Public Financial Management Reform Coordinating Unit (PFMRCU) that will be established in MoF will report to the PFM Reform Oversight Steering Committee (PFM-ROSC) chaired by the Minister of Finance. This committee will provide policy guidance and address any potential resistance to the reforms. A Government PFM Reform Coordinator with project management experience will oversee the daily coordination of reform activities. This coordinator will serve as the primary point of contact for the Reform Implementation Teams (RITs) to ensure the appropriate sequencing of activities funded by various donors. The RITs will comprise of government designated focal staff to absorb skills and knowledge from Technical Assistants (TAs), promoting the sustainability of the reform efforts. The PFM Donor Group (PFM-DG) will ensure mutually reinforcing support based on a government-led PFM reform agenda and foster policy dialogue. The PFM-DG will table key issues at the Syria Partnership Coordination Forum. Page 5 Official Use Only The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) 14. To support the MoF’s readiness to handle large-scale external financing for recovery and reconstruction efficiently and transparently, the project will support the operationalization of a Syria External Assistance Fiduciary Section (SEAFS) in the MoF. The SEAFS centralized structure in MoF comprising government designated staff will manage the fiduciary functions for this project and institutionalized for externally financed projects using country systems reinforced with risk mitigation measures. For Procurement, SEAFS will not operate as a central procuring department for all contracts financed by external donors. Instead, its role will be pivotal in providing fiduciary and capacity-building support to Project Implementation Units (PIUs), which will retain overall responsibility for procurement execution. This approach ensures that procurement remains decentralized at the project level while benefiting from strong upstream fiduciary support, thereby streamlining processes and reducing unnecessary delays. 15. Over time, and in addition to its core coordination role, the PFMRCU will support the integration of climate- sensitive/resilience measures across PFM reforms, as relevant. This may include coordination of initiatives such as climate budget tagging, tracking of adaptation- and mitigation-related expenditures, and exploring opportunities to strengthen the public financial system’s capacity to support the country’s climate-related objectives over time. Component 2: Capacity development for essential public financial management and procurement functions. 16. Beyond sound legal and institutional frameworks, PFM improvement requires capability at the level of individual PFM practitioners. A Training Needs Assessment (TNA) will be undertaken based on Functional Review and Job Analysis for the Central Finance Agencies (Ministry of Finance, Central Organization for Financial Control (COFC), _For Official Use Only_ Central Commission for Control and Inspection (CCCI)). This assessment will inform a comprehensive capacity development strategy to support the professionalization of the PFM cadre. The strategy will be monitored through a plan for post-training evaluations (Kirkpatrick Training Evaluation Model) to identify the extent to which the skills and knowledge gained are improving individual and organizational performance. The sustainability of PFM capacity building will require a well-defined career structure to encourage staff to develop PFM skills and acquire PFM qualifications with opportunity to rise through the hierarchy of the PFM cadre. 17. A PFM Competency Framework based on job analysis will be prepared as the key instrument to ascertain the required capability and learning outcomes to effectively perform the key PFM functions The Syria PFM curriculum will aim to cover the PFM Process Architecture according to the ‘whole system approach’ of the Chartered Institute of Public Finance and Accountancy (CIPFA). However, as a forward-looking education program, the curriculum will be broadened with fiscal forecasts, budget preparation and management, sector strategies, performance budgeting, procurement, fiscal transparency and participation, and PFM legal framework. Whilst the curriculum and learning modules will comply with internationally acceptable standards, the design will respond to Syria’s applicable legal framework. The professional training program will include elements of climate-responsive public financial management, covering topics such as climate budget tagging, climate finance tracking, fiscal risk assessment for climate shocks, and green fiscal policy design. These training modules will build public officials’ capacity to integrate climate risk and opportunity into fiscal planning. Face-to-face training will be supplemented by mentorship and on- the-job learning to ensure attainment of the desired competencies. 18. The Accounting and Audit Council (AAC) as the sector regulator (under the MoF) and the Association of Syrian Certified Accountants (ASCA) as the Professional Accountancy Organization (PAO) will play critical roles in the proposed professionalization program. The ASCA will set curriculum, administer examinations, and issue certificates. The project will provide advice on strengthening Law no. 33 of 2009 (Law Regulating the Accounting and Auditing Profession) and the institutional arrangements for ASCA to meet the International Federation of Accountants (IFAC) Statements of Membership Obligations (SMO). Formal accreditation/certification would fall under the ASCA as the PAO since this will maintain academic rigor and credibility. To ensure sustainability, the PFM training program will be Page 6 Official Use Only The World Bank Syria Public Financial Management Capacity Strengthening Project (P512516) constituted as a PFM Academy within the Financial Technical Institute (FTI1) in MoF. Training of Trainers (ToT) will build sustained capacity to deliver in-county training. Training will be geared towards creating a cohort of suitably qualified PFM practitioners with the skills to perform a broad range of tasks and who will contribute to improving PFM performance. 19. The project will support issuance of a new public procurement law to replace the existing Uniform System of Contracts Law No. 51 which currently governs public procurement in Syria. This new law will be designed to support a modern, fit-for-purpose procurement framework. The primary objective is to establish a legal environment that supports competitive bidding, prevents corruption, enhances sustainable procurement, and promotes the efficient use of public resources (Value for Money). Following the establishment of the new public procurement law, implementing regulations will be prepared and adopted to facilitate its application. The new public procurement framework will support the establishment of a regulatory body that will be mandated for oversight of all public procurement activities, policy setting, development of standard bidding documents, development of the single procurement portal as well as training and public awareness, etc. Development of modern procurement tools will include the following: Issuing Standard Bidding Documents (SBDs), establishing a Single Procurement Portal (SPP), establishing a Complaint Handling Mechanism, and capacity building and professionalization of the procurement workforce. 20. The Project will support the MOF in improving PFM systems through Just-in-Time (JIT) technical assistance and _For Official Use Only_ knowledge-sharing in response to emerging needs. The JIT support will mainly assist the MoF and other agencies in the following areas: (i) developing a new PFM strategy with a time-bound action plan, (ii) establishing a database of State-owned Enterprises (SOEs), (iii) peer learning with practitioners from other countries, (iv) undertaking the verification of the civil service personnel register and data matching with the payroll system in coordination with the Ministry of Administrative Development (MoAD), (v) supporting the review and drafting of (a) PFM laws, (b) regulations of existing laws, (c) if enacted, regulations of said new PFM laws, and (d) preparation of comprehensive operating procedures manual, (vi) developing a strategic plan for COFC, (vii) establishing interface between CBS payment systems and MoF related systems, (viii) pay and grading reform led by the MoAD, and (ix) PEFA (including climate and gender) and any other PFM diagnostics, policy notes, sector strategies, and reform action plans that will be agreed with the MoF in due course. Assistance in other related areas could be also provided if needs arise. Component 3: Syria Integrated Financial Management Information System (SIFMIS) 21. This component will finance the supply, installation, integration, testing, user training, and commissioning of the Syria Integrated Financial Management Information System (SIFMIS), associated end-user devices, hardware, network infrastructure, and related support services. The envisaged Commercial Off-the-Shelf (COTS) SIFMIS will support procedural and control systems to ensure the legality, accuracy and timelines of fiscal information in the following core PFM functions: budget preparation, budget execution, human resource/payroll with biometric registration (including robust data protection and privacy safeguards), expenditure control, cash management, purchasing, revenue management, fixed asset registration, inventory management, accounting, reporting, audit trails, and financial reporting/analytics with in-built dashboards for online publication. The implementation approach will be incremental, starting with the core modules (budget upload, general ledger, revenue recording, expenditure, reporting), and will operate in recording mode during the first phase. An agile mindset of focusing on business process simplification and a user-friendly and ‘fit-for-purpose’ solution with flexibility to respond to change rather than following a strict plan will be paramount. After the core modules are launched in recording mode during the first six months of the system ‘going live’, transaction processing will transition to workflow approval mode with mandatory document attachment and implementation of more modules/functions. These may include the following: budget preparation and execution, purchasing and online bill submission, treasury management and commitment control, payroll with biometric registration, non-tax revenue collection, fixed asset register, inventory management, financial reporting with dashboards including through a Syria Budget Transparency Portal3. _For Official Use Only_ While accepting flexibility, a formal change control process would be established to avoid scope creep during the system implementation. User Acceptance Testing (UAT) and end-user training will be conducted before giving clearance for the system to ‘go-live’. Change management will be central to the implementation approach, and the business process re-engineering will consider the absorptive capacity of the end-users.
