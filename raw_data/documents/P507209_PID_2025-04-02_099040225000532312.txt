Introduction and Context 1. Tunisia’s economy in 2024 experienced a cautious recovery in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, albeit with challenges and opportunities. The country has one of the most diversified economies in Africa, with a historically large services sector that has grown over the past decade.1 In 2023, significant export gains came from tourism recovery2and export-oriented industry.3 However, severe drought, tighter financing conditions and the pace of economic modernization efforts impacted growth in 2023, leaving the economy below its pre-COVID 19 pandemic level state. Real GDP growth in 2024 was 1.4 percent, compared to 2.7 percent in 2022.4 This translated into an unemployment rate of 16 percent in quarter 3 of 2024 and a labor force participation rate of 46.2 percent, 1.5 percent below pre-COVID 19 pandemic levels.5 Further, the limited growth adds to the challenges Tunisia faces in covering its external financing needs despite an improving external balance. Pressure on public finance remains significant. While the fiscal deficit moderated in 2024, it continues to be higher than pre-COVID period (6.3 percent of GDP in 2024 versus 2.9 percent in 2019). Debt level remains in the range of 80 percent. 2. Economic challenges and vulnerability to climate change are affecting Tunisians unequally . Inequality, as measured by the Gini Index, increased from 32.8 to 33.7 between 2015 and 2021, mainly driven by disparities within urban and rural areas and between regions. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Tunisia’s health system continues to face the impact of an ongoing demographic and epidemiological shift. The share of the population above 65 is high in Tunisia (9 percent), almost twice the average for MENA countries (5 percent), and well above peers such as Algeria, Egypt, and Morocco. Non-communicable diseases (NCD) continue to be the main source of the disease burden, causing 86 percent of deaths, with particularly high and increasing rates of hypertension and diabetes. In 2016, population-level prevalence of hypertension was at 29 percent, and of diabetes 16 percent, placing Tunisia amongst the countries with the highest rates in the MENA region. The control rates for these diseases are particularly low, with 14 percent of cases for diabetes and 11 percent of cases for hypertension controlled.7 As a result of these low control rates, the prevalence for chronic kidney disease amongst diabetics is 39 percent, with only 17 percent of diabetic patients screened for the condition.8 There are over 100,000 new cases of strokes and acute cardiovascular incidents, which require urgent intervention. Tunisia has a high share of road traffic accidents, with over 2,200 deaths and 10,000 injuries each year; most of this burden is for younger than 50, which creates an economic burden. These low control rates not only increase morbidity and mortality but also impose inefficiencies presenting an upwards pressure on health expenditures. 4. Climate change is anticipated to increase health risks and emergencies faced by Tunisia's most vulnerable communities, exacerbating environmental health concerns. Tunisia’s Mediterranean location makes it highly vulnerable to climate change, with extreme weather events such as heat waves, heavy rainfall, and drought becoming more frequent. Rising sea levels could impact nearly 78,700 people by 2100.9 Four years of drought, have significantly reduced agricultural production, increasing food insecurity and malnutrition.10 Climate-related hazards pose high risks to 26 percent of the population (18.9 percent exposed to drought, 7.3 percent to heatwaves, and 1.3 percent to floods). 11 Under a high emission scenario, the mean annual temperature is projected to rise by 5.3°C in 2100, causing as many as 56 deaths per 100,000 in the elderly (65+ years) by 2080 compared to the estimated baseline of 4 deaths per 100,000 annually between 1961 and 1990.12 Other negative health impacts associated with climate change in Tunisia include the recurrence and emergence of vector-borne diseases (malaria, leishmaniasis, dengue and West Nile fever), an increase in water-borne and food-borne diseases, and the aggravation of diseases related to air pollution.13 Rural poor populations, as well as outside workers, women, children, the elderly are disproportionately affected.14 Environmental factors such as poor air quality, water contamination, and exposure to hazardous chemicals further exacerbate the burden of NCDs. 6 World Bank Group. Tunisia struggles with disease surveillance, impeding its ability to efficiently detect, prevent and respond to health emergencies. Tunisia’s list of notifiable diseases includes common communicable diseases affecting both humans and animals, such as influenza, COVID-19, rabies, yellow fever, and various forms of hepatitis, as well as diseases such as leishmaniasis, meningitis, and tuberculosis. Compounding these challenges, a rise in microbial resistance due to a 38 percent increase in antibiotic consumption driven by overuse of antibiotics, which has reduced the effectiveness of treatments, particularly for vulnerable populations. Tunisia has the highest antibiotics consumption rate in the world.15.While Tunisia mandates disease surveillance and epidemiological data collection, weaknesses remain in integrating surveillance platforms, conducting risk assessments, and ensuring coordination between human, animal, and environmental health sectors under a One Health approach. Although Tunisia has made progress in response capacity under International Health Regulations (IHR), the COVID-19 pandemic exposed critical gaps in emergency preparedness, resulting in one of the highest mortality rates in the region. 6. Institutional arrangements for emergency and pandemic preparedness and response are coordinated by the Ministry of Health. The country has mechanisms in place to detect, prevent, and respond to health crises such as pandemics (e.g., COVID-19), though challenges remain in coordination, resources, and implementation. The Strategic Health Operations Center (SHOCROOM – Centre Stratégique d'Opérations Sanitaires) was established in 2010 with support from World Health Organization (WHO) to service as a 24/7 public health emergency coordination hub, operating within the Ministry of Health and directly linked to the Minister’s cabinet. Initially designed to manage the H1N1 pandemic, it has since played a pivotal role in responding to major national and international crises, including: (i)the 2011 humanitarian crisis at the Tunisian Libyan border; (ii) response efforts to terrorist attacks and mass casualty incidents; and (ii) the management of the COVID-19 pandemic. A new decree will formally establish the SHOCROOM as Tunisia’s National Public Health Emergency Operations Center (PHEOC). 7. Tunisians encounter multiple barriers to accessing quality healthcare services due to organizational constraints within the health system and significant disparities in service availability. Health services are organized in a traditional pyramid structure, with the majority of health system capacity managed publicly. While there are over 2,000 primary health centers (centre de santé de base – CSB), only 419 perform outpatient medical consultations daily, and many operate once a week, resulting in exceptionally low utilization rates. Disparities in healthcare availability and access between urban and rural populations are stark. Rural areas in Tunisia face significant limitations in health infrastructure, essential services, and specialized care. A shortage of healthcare professionals and physicians further exacerbates the challenge, making quality medical services difficult to access. Reports indicate that only 34 percent of rural Tunisians live within walking distance of a healthcare facility. Additionally, 43 percent of Tunisians reported having to forgo medical care at least once in the past year, with 33 percent experiencing this multiple times. 16 8. Tunisia's primary health care system faces challenges related to fragmented accountability, administrative burdens, and an inefficient health information system. National health goals exist, but their implementation is scattered across various ministries, while district-level physician chiefs are overwhelmed with administrative tasks. Although some supportive supervision exists in immunization, it has not been extended to chronic disease management. The health system remains largely paper-based, with electronic initiatives like the Electronic Health Records pilot (DMI) facing connectivity and functionality issues, and no unified registry for chronic conditions. Facility capacity has improved, but gaps remain, including outdated equipment and restrictions on task-shifting basic medical duties to nurses. While some innovations exist, their implementation remains inconsistent, contributing to an overall fragmented and inefficient healthcare experience. 9. Consequently, most patients seek services at hospitals, which drives up operating costs and reduces the ability to serve populations in harder-to-reach areas. Regional and university hospitals are unevenly distributed; and patients in disadvantaged areas need to travel over 300 kilometers to reach essential and emergency health services. Access to healthcare is particularly challenging in peri-urban and rural areas, raising concerns about health equity. A forthcoming World Bank analysis of travel times to emergency facilities shows that over 1 million people in peri-urban and rural areas (zones 6 and 8) are beyond the government's 45-minute target, compared to more accessible urban centers. In rural areas (zone 8), nearly 650,000 people (around 65 percent of population) face travel times exceeding 45 minutes, significantly limiting their timely access to emergency care. Meanwhile, in peri-urban areas (zone 6), almost 395,000 people (around 40 percent of population) fall outside the target, highlighting a stark contrast with urban centers like zone 1, where 88 percent of the population experience such delays. These disparities pose serious health equity concerns, as delayed access to emergency care increases risks for vulnerable populations. Furthermore, climate-related hazards will exacerbate these challenges, with extreme weather events such as floods disrupting roads and further prolonging travel times, disproportionately affecting those in rural and peri-urban communities. 10. Pre-hospital emergency services face challenges due to the lack of standardized priority systems, inadequate information management, and inconsistent adoption of Electronic Health Records (EHR) across healthcare facilities . The pre-hospital emergency medical services operate with six Emergency Medical Assistance Departments (SAMU) and 37 Mobile Medical Units for Emergency Care and Resuscitation (SMUR). In 2024, these pre-hospital emergency teams conducted 11,526 primary interventions, urgently responding to victims and critically ill patients at the scene. Additionally, 16,908 secondary interventions were carried out to transfer critical patients between healthcare facilities, ensuring continuity of care and access to specialized medical services. However, the medical regulation centers that manage dispatch operations currently lack a standardized priority system, and information management remains inadequate. SAMU continues to rely on paper reports, lacks centralized data collection, and does not integrate with hospital and EHR records. The implementation and utilization of EHR, also referred to as Dossier Médical Informatisé (DMI), vary significantly across different levels of the emergency services. In University Hospitals, EHR systems have been widely implemented, with 50 percent fully installed and utilized, while the remaining 50 percent are installed but only partially exploited, indicating challenges in adoption or system optimization. In regional hospitals, the situation is more complex. While 32 percent of hospitals have fully installed and operational EHR systems, a significant 52 percent have EHR installed but only partially utilized, reflecting similar adoption challenges. Furthermore, 16 percent of regional hospitals have not installed EHR systems at all, highlighting a gap in digital transformation. The situation is more concerning in district hospitals and CSBs, where EHR systems are neither installed nor used. The absence of digital health records at this level suggests a critical barrier to efficient patient data management, coordination of care, and overall healthcare service improvement. 11. Hospital emergency departments are generally well-equipped but face challenges in coordination and optimizing patient pathways, particularly for critical cases requiring intensive care. For critical patients, the major issue is the shortage of intensive care unit beds. Tunisia had around 90 ICU beds before the COVID-19 pandemic, which increased to 520 during the crisis as the health system rapidly expanded its emergency and intensive care capacities. However, many of these additional beds were in field hospitals that have since closed, leaving the country with approximately 150 functional ICU beds, some of which are only for post-surgery care. This results in Tunisia having about 1.25 critical care beds per 100,000 people, compared to 9.6 in South Africa and 46.5 in Turkey. Consequently, some patients spend their entire hospital stay in the ED, with estimates indicating that up to 60 percent of those needing admission remain there. Specialized emergency services are also hampered by administrative and organizational shortcomings, often dealing with general emergencies instead of specialized cases. 12. In response to lagging health outcomes and fragmented governance, the Government has developed a National Health Policy (PNS). Developed through national dialogue, the PNS focuses on three core principles: (i) strengthening governance, (ii) improving transparency, and (iii) deepening citizen engagement. The key pillars include (1) scaling up family medicine to strengthen primary care (2) introducing an essential benefits package for all citizens, (3) merging financial risk protection schemes for universal coverage, (4) enhancing public service delivery to reduce inequalities, and (5) promoting multisectoral prevention approaches. These reforms aim to boost patient trust, health worker motivation, and system efficiency. By 2030, Tunisia aims to reduce maternal mortality from 45 to 19 per 100,000, obesity rates from 26 percent to 20 percent, out of pocket expenses (OOP) from 38 percent to 25 percent, and expand basic health insurance coverage to 100 percent of the population and increase government health spending to 6 percent of GDP. Project Description 14. The components are financed with US$108 million of IBRD financing and US$17.2 million grant from the Pandemic Fund. Component 1: Strengthening Health System Resilience (US$24.9M, including US$17.2M RETF from the Pandemic Fund) 15. Component 1 focuses on strengthening health system resilience through investments in surveillance systems, climate change adaptation, and laboratory capacity for emergencies and pandemic preparedness. It aims to enhance Tunisia's ability to effectively detect, assess, and respond to public health risks before, during, and after health emergencies, with coordination across sectors, including human, animal, and environmental health. Key activities under this component include: 1) strengthening and integrating data for surveillance, preparation, response, and recovery for public health emergencies; 2) enhancing laboratory capacity and quality management for effective diagnostic networks and improved biosafety standards; 3) supporting energy efficiency systems and climate change adaptation and mitigation; and 4) improving waste management systems. Component 2: Supporting reorganization of preventive and primary health services (US$50 M) Page 6 The World Bank Tunisia Health System Strengthening Project (P507209) 16. Component 2 will contribute to transforming primary health care to improve the efficiency, distribution and quality of services. It will support efforts to establish family and community health as the entry point to the health system within the National Health Policy. It will do so through three subcomponents: i) strengthening the organizational structure and governance of PHC and strengthening human resources for health at the PHC level through reorganization plans at each of the circonscriptions; ii) upgrading the capacity of CSBs based on the PHC service reorganization plans at each of the circonscriptions; and iii) supporting the deployment of a comprehensive national PHC information system. Component 3: Strengthening emergency medical services ($48M) 17. Component 3 will enhance all phases of emergency care including pre-hospitalization, hospitalization and critical care and inpatient services. This will improve the pre-hospitalization services including emergency call management and ambulance dispatch systems. It will also focus on the hospitalization phase by investing in upgrading and scaling up the triage system and ensuring efficient patient transfers, especially to hospital wards and to intensive care to ease the burden on emergency departments. Finally, it will support with primary care and intensive care services. Additionally, this component will support adopting green technologies and reducing energy consumption in emergency management systems. Component 4: Implementation Support, Training and Project Management (US$2M) 18. This component will provide technical assistance and capacity-building support to the Ministry of Health, strengthening project management functions. It will finance technical assistance for fiduciary tasks, including the deployment of information systems, audits of project financial statements, procurement management, and support the implementation of environmental and social (E&S) measures to ensure alignment with the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF). MOH has designated E&S focal points from different directorates, environment and social risk management consultants who would support with the coordination and reporting across the different units. A GBV specialist will be appointed to support the Project. Regular monitoring and reporting on project implementation will also be supported by the component. Training under this component provided by the World Bank, will focus on enhancing fiduciary aspects, monitoring and evaluation, while also integrating E&S risk management into the Project implementation. This will build capacity within the MOH to identify, mitigate, and monitor environmental and social impacts throughout the project lifecycle. The component will support management of key environmental and social issues and risks such as healthcare waste management, climate resilience, and improving energy efficiency in healthcare facilities as well as social risks, including SEA/SH, exclusion risks, exposure to biohazards, and grievance management. The environmental risk rating for the Tunisia Health System Strengthening Project is rated Moderate. Key environmental risks include infrastructure-related impacts associated with establishing health data management systems (sub-component 1.1) and rehabilitating PHCs (sub-component 2.2). These activities will occur within existing government- owned facilities, avoiding land clearing or new development, and are unlikely to affect biodiversity or ecologically sensitive areas. However, rehabilitation and minor construction activities may lead to localized, temporary environmental impacts including, air and noise pollution, wastewater management, solid waste generation, and occupational health and safety (OHS) risks, particularly when conducted within operational health facilities. Given the multiple project sites and varying local capacities, ensuring compliance with environmental standards and contractor obligations may be challenging. Another significant environmental risk is healthcare waste management, including biomedical waste, outdated equipment, and E-waste. These wastes require proper handling, segregation, and disposal to prevent contamination risks to healthcare workers, patients, and surrounding communities. Poorly managed healthcare waste, particularly hazardous and infectious materials, can contribute to environmental pollution and public health risks. Mitigation measures will be embedded within the project design as subcomponent 1.4 specifically targets hospital waste management improvements, reinforcing national capacity for medical waste segregation, collection, transportation, treatment, and disposal. The Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF) developed under the COVID 19 project will be updated to reflect the requirements of the current Project. The revised ESMF will outline detailed screening procedures, compliance measures, and mitigation strategies for managing environmental and social risks in healthcare facilities. The ESMF will also include measures for the safe disposal of medical waste, decommissioning of outdated equipment, and E-waste, ensuring alignment with national regulations and international best practices. Furthermore, a healthcare waste management plan will be integrated into Project Operations Manual, specifying clear protocols for handling, storage, and treatment of hazardous materials. Additionally, the project will evaluate the feasibility of introducing incineration facilities for treating high-risk medical waste. This assessment will examine environmental and public health risks, regulatory compliance, and cost-effectiveness. The incineration option will only be pursued if it meets international environmental standards and includes robust emission control technologies to mitigate air pollution risks. Overall, while the project presents moderate environmental risks, these risks can be effectively managed through well-designed mitigation measures, strengthened institutional capacity, and close monitoring of environmental compliance. 20. The social risk rating is Moderate. Social risks are not expected to be significant, but are there a number of risks that will need to be managed through mitigation measures as identified in environmental and social instruments, and institutional capacity strengthening to improve monitoring and risk management. Risks include the potential for exclusion or discrimination of project beneficiaries especially among vulnerable and disadvantaged groups; and non- inclusive and inaccessible stakeholder consultation into a number of assessments, evaluations, and reforms under components 1 and 2. Another key risk is the potential for weak grievance management and a grievance mechanism (GM) that is not currently fit for purpose to track and address project-related grievances in a timely manner. To address risks and incidents related to sexual exploitation and abuse/sexual harassment (SEA/SH), specialized capacity is needed to develop and manage a confidential, survivor-centered, ethical, non-judgmental, and responsive incident response process with a referral pathway for GBV support services. These GM reforms will be included in the updated Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP). 21. There is a risk for data privacy breaches during the digitalization of medical records . The Borrower’s policy framework for personal data protection has been evaluated with measures to be included in the legal agreement. OHS and community health risks stem from potential mismanagement of healthcare waste (component 1.4), noncompliance and monitoring with biosecurity and biosafety protocols and the safe handling of biological material (pathogens, viruses and bacteria under component 1.2). Small construction activities are expected in existing health facilities, these activities may potentially expose the community to risks such as noise and dust pollution, disturbances from construction vibrations, as well as hazards such as exposure to accidents and disruption to service delivery during construction. The Ministry of Health (MOH) will serve as the implementing agency for the Project, covering both the IBRD loan and the Pandemic Fund. Implementation arrangements have been formalized through an internal ministerial note . The Project will be managed by a technical coordinator and an administrative coordinator. The Director General of Health (Directeur Général de Santé - DGS) will act as the technical coordinator, overseeing project activities, supervising daily operations, and engaging relevant technical departments to support implementation. As the Ministry of Health's focal point, the DGS will be the primary liaison with the World Bank, ensuring effective communication and coordination on project-related matters. The DGS will also collaborate with various MOH departments, the World Bank, and other stakeholders to facilitate project execution. The Director General of Common Services (Directeur Général des Services Commun – DGSC) will serve as the administrative coordinator, responsible for fiduciary management, including procurement, financial oversight, and compliance with environmental and social standards. This directorate will also handle budgeting, planning, reporting, and monitoring and evaluation (M&E) functions. Under Component 4, efforts will be made to strengthen the directorate’s capacities to enhance project implementation. 24. The Directorate of Environmental Hygiene and Protection will oversee the alignment and implementation of the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF). The Directorate of Environmental Hygiene and Protection reports to the General Director of Common Services. The MOH has designated a full time E&S coordinator from the Directorate of Environmental Hygiene and Protection to coordinate among stakeholders and report on ESF progress.
