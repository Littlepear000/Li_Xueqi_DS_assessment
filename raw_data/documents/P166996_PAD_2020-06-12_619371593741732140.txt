Page 6 of 102 The World Bank Ghana Jobs and Skills Project (P166996) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context1 1. The Republic of Ghana, with a population of about 30 million, is classified as a lower-middle- income country with a per capita income of US$5,194 (in 2017 purchasing power parity dollars) in 2018. Ghana achieved its current income status through strong, sustained economic growth. The country’s per capita national income grew by an average of 4.8 percent per year between 1993 and 2005, by 7.7 percent per year between 2005 and 2012 (peaking at 14.4 percent in 2011), and by 5.6 percent per year between 2012 and 2016. Ghana’s economic growth performance during the 2000s is mainly attributed to the sharp increase in prices for its main commodity exports, cocoa and gold, and to the start of commercial oil and gas production in 2011. 2. The country’s favorable economic growth performance has been accompanied by a substantial reduction in the prevalence of poverty. Based on the national food poverty line, the extreme poverty rate fell from 36 percent in 1991/92 to 16.5 percent in 2005/06 to 8.4 percent in 2012/13, allowing Ghana to meet the United Nations Millennium Development Goal of halving poverty by 2015. Its current extreme poverty rate, measured in 2016/17, is 8.2 percent. In terms of micro determinants, the reduction in poverty is found to be associated with increases in employment, agricultural income, access to electricity, and education attainment. It is also associated with a shift in employment from agriculture to industry and services. 3. Ghana’s poverty reduction story is not without problems, however. There are three main areas of concern. First, the pace of poverty reduction has slowed over time. Second, the association between economic growth and poverty reduction has weakened over time. Third, the poverty reduction record varies substantially by the country’s regions, corresponding with spatial differences in economic activity and output. In some regions, poverty rates have remained persistently high. 4. The inequality and shared prosperity picture also indicates some concern. Income inequality in Ghana has risen over the 1990s and 2000s, driven by the changes in poorer regions in the country. Measured in 2016/17, Ghana had a Gini index value of income of 0.43, and the poorest 40 percent of the country’s population received 26 percent of national income. Consistent with rising income inequality, growth in average consumption spending of the country’s total population was higher than for the poorest 40 percent of the population over the 1990s and 2000s. These trends suggest that Ghana’s gains in prosperity have not been progressive. 5. To raise and sustain its economic growth performance, the country would need to (a) increase private investment and productivity; (b) have stronger macroeconomic policy management, which includes increased domestic revenue mobilization and improved public debt management; and (c) protect against possible volatility in its economic growth, which, in the past, has been mainly driven by fluctuations in global commodity prices and by increased public spending tied to election cycles. 1Sources for this section include Republic of Ghana (2017), World Bank (2018a), Ghana Statistical Service (2019a), and the World Bank’s World Development Indicators database (https://databank.worldbank.org/source/world-development-indicators). B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 7. Ghana’s labor force has been expanding rapidly, from 6.5 million in 1993 to 12.1 million in 2017 and it is expected to continue to grow.2 The sectoral distribution of national income has shifted from agriculture to mainly services. Accompanying this trend, the country’s sectoral distribution of employment has also shifted from agriculture to mainly services. Today, services dominate in terms of their contribution to national income and employment. Measured in 2016/17, agriculture, industry, and services accounted for 18.3 percent, 25.5 percent, and 56.2 percent of national income, respectively.3 Similarly, in 2016/17, agriculture, industry, and services accounted for 41 percent, 14 percent, and 45 percent of total employment, respectively.4 The value added per worker is currently twice as high in services as in agriculture, but the productivity levels in both sectors have stagnated over the 2000s. Value added per worker in industry has increased over time and is now 50 percent higher than in services. All these trends are driven in part by a dramatic shift in the population distribution toward urban areas. 8. Based on 2016/17 data, while average labor outcomes for youth–officially defined by Ghana to be individuals ages 15–34 years–are not dramatically different from average outcomes for the working- age population in general, average labor outcomes vary significantly across subgroups among youth. While they possess more education on average than the working-age population in general, youth have a lower rate of labor force participation and a higher rate of unemployment (Table 1). While the likelihood of labor force participation is similar between young women and young men, young women are more likely to be underemployed and more likely to be working in services and in self-employment activities.5 Poor youth (that is, youth in the poorest 40 percent of the population) are more likely to be in the labor force than their nonpoor counterparts; they are also more likely to be working in self-employment activities and in agriculture. Youth with disabilities have substantially lower average education attainment than youth without disabilities and are significantly less likely to be in the labor force. They are also much more likely to be self-employed when they are working. Compared to their urban counterparts, rural youth are much more likely to be working in self-employment activities and in agriculture. When working in wage employment activities, young women, persons with disabilities, and rural persons have substantially lower average wage earnings than young men, persons without disabilities, and urban persons, respectively. These differences between subgroups in average wage earnings do not account for differences between subgroups in average productive characteristics. 9. The vast majority of Ghanaians are self-employed. In 2016/17, 70 percent of the working-age population and 63 percent of youth were self-employed. Traditional apprenticeship training is a major source of skill acquisition, especially for youth who have low levels of education and come from poorer households. In 2016/17, among the working-age population, the participation rate in apprenticeship training was 22 percent, and the average length of training was 2.5 years. Of apprenticeship training participants, 87 percent had less than nine years of schooling, and 34 percent came from the poorest 40 percent. In comparison, the participation rate in Technical and Vocational Education and Training (TVET) programs (that is, the formal TVET sector) was much lower, at 12 percent. In addition, 92 percent of TVET program participants had more than nine years of education (as expected, given education completion requirements for TVET program participation), and 84 percent came from the richest 60 percent. The predominance of low-productivity employment activities stems partly from a substantial proportion of youth going through low-quality, informal apprenticeship training. In 2012, close to half a million people were engaged in informal apprenticeship training, compared to 40,000 people in the formal TVET system.6 Most people trained in informal apprenticeships do not benefit from quality training. Informal apprentices depend on the limited knowledge and willingness of the master craft person to train and are limited in their learning by the narrow range of services that the master craft person provides. 12. Despite the poor quality of existing informal apprenticeship training, households seem nevertheless to value apprenticeships, as indicated by households and/or apprentices usually paying a fee to the master craft person in exchange for on-the-job training. Given the large number of youth entering into apprenticeship training agreements each year, improved apprenticeship training can lead to appreciable gains for both apprentices and training enterprises.7 International best practices suggest that the quality of training can be improved by extending training to both apprentices and training enterprises. The knowledge of master craft persons should be upgraded and certified; a dual training system combining on-the-job training with classroom training should be introduced to allow the apprentice to acquire trade-specific vocational and socioemotional skills; the skills of apprentices should be certified; and training providers in the process should be accredited. The whole process should ensure that incentives of apprentices, master craft persons, and training providers are aligned, and that training is provided in accordance with standards. 13. Weak support systems for entrepreneurship and small business development also contribute to poor labor market outcomes in Ghana. In addition to having low skill levels, youth face higher barriers to self-employment stemming from the lack of access to finance, land, social networks, and other resources compared to older adults. Labor intermediation constraints include lack of relevant, up-to-date, and actionable labor market information. According to the Ghana Labour Force Survey 2015, job seekers barely used internet-based and other intermediation tools. Among people who actively searched for work, more than two-fifths contacted friends and relatives for information.8 Fewer than 5 percent of respondents indicated that they searched the internet or newspaper advertisements or used formal employment services. The Ministry of Employment and Labour Relations (MELR) is developing a labor market information system that would provide the needed information on labor market conditions, including on job seekers and job opportunities, with the potential to serve as the primary basis for improved job intermediation services. The first phase of the development of the Ghana Labor Market Information System (GLMIS) has been completed with the basic software in place. District Public Employment Centers (PECs), managed by MELR, have a key role to play in gathering and inputting the needed labor market information into the GLMIS as well as in providing job connection services. The PECs are however in poor condition in terms of infrastructure and resources, and their staff are oriented toward conducting labor inspections instead of providing labor market information and job connection services. 14. The Government Strategy for Technical and Vocational Education and Training Transformation sets out the landscape for formal and informal sector skill training in Ghana. The strategy developed by the Council for Technical and Vocational Education and Training (COTVET) and the Ministry of Education (MOE) identifies key challenges confronting skill training. COTVET’s five-year strategic plan (2018–23) aims to improve access, quality, financing, and coordination of skill training. Almost all youth employment programs in the country contain some form of entrepreneurship training. The Student Entrepreneurship Business Model, initiated by COTVET, aims to enhance professional and entrepreneurship skills for TVET students. There are 13 TVET institutions currently administering this training. The Graduate Entrepreneurial Business Support Scheme, under MELR, operates a public-private partnership modality and aims to support graduates to successfully enter self- employment. Finally, a wide range of entrepreneurship training programs are administered by the National Board for Small Scale Industries (NBSSI). NBSSI is the apex Governmental body overseeing the promotion and development of the micro and small enterprise sector in Ghana. The Government views the sector as having the potential to contribute substantially to improving labor outcomes and economic productivity and growth. 16. The Government launched the Coordinated Programme of Economic and Social Development Policies (2017–2024): An Agenda for Jobs: Creating Prosperity and Equal Opportunity for All, which emphasizes the need for the creation of more jobs that are gainful and productive. Specific policies introduced by the Government in the past decade to address poor labor market circumstances include the country’s National Employment Policy 2015 and National Youth Policy 2010.9 In addition, the Government has developed acts to legalize certain agencies such as the Youth Employment Agency and the National Youth Authority. These agencies are mandated to formulate policies and implement programs that promote youth development and the effective participation of youth in the economic development of the country. 17. The Government continues to invest heavily in several employment programs, with the majority focusing on youth. An inventory of youth employment programs in Ghana revealed 40 major programs, of which 18 are implemented by the Government.10 These youth employment programs focus on TVET training, entrepreneurship training, apprenticeship training, employment services, and direct employment. Ghana Jobs and Skills Project and COVID-19: An Instrument for Relief and Recovery Support to Poorer Workers and Enterprises Mainly due to measures taken by the Government of Ghana to control the spread of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19), significant numbers of households in Ghana are likely to experience a large, unanticipated negative shock to their labor income. Poorer, less-educated individuals in rural and urban areas, who tend to be employed in informal economic activities, will likely be affected most by this shock. Along with emergency relief (the immediate need) during the fallout period, economic recovery initiatives are essential for restoring jobs, livelihoods, and economic activities for workers and enterprises during the recovery. Informal employment in Ghana can be broadly stylized as follows: 11 • Most workers are informal (rural: 94 percent; urban: 80 percent). 12 • Most informal workers are self-employed (rural: 86 percent; urban: 70 percent). 13 • Informal wage workers are mainly employed in micro and small enterprises (or as casual or domestic workers). • Informal workers are more likely to be poor or vulnerable to falling into poverty than formal workers. There are 18.3 million informal workers in Ghana (53 percent reside in rural areas), of which 52 percent are engaged in farming. Outside of farming, 41 percent of informal workers are engaged in wholesale and retail trade, followed by manufacturing (23 percent), construction (8 percent), accommodation and food services (6 percent) and transportation and storage (6 percent). The noted subsectors are all heavily dominated by informal employment−over 90 percent of all workers in these subsectors are informal. Several of these subsectors are those expected to the worst affected by the mitigation measures (physical distancing and stay-at-home) instituted by the Government. The proposed project can provide economic relief to workers and enterprises affected by COVID-19. Specifically, it can allow for special cash transfers on top of originally proposed transfers (grants) to on- the-job apprentices, entrepreneurs, and micro and small enterprises, supported by project funds. To promote economic recovery, the project aims to • Provide quality-assured on-the-job apprenticeships to develop skills, followed by facilitation to apprentices to support self-employment activities or to secure wage employment opportunities; • Provide training, grants, counseling, and facilitation for self-employment (entrepreneurship) activities and to micro and small enterprises; 11 All patterns are based on own estimates using data from the Ghana Living Standards Survey 2016/17. The interventions under the project are consistent with the aims and priorities of the World Bank’s approach paper A Framework for Economic and Social Operational Response to COVID19 and Africa Region: A Framework for Operational Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic and Global Crisis. The monitoring system will also allow for tracking the impact of COVID-19 on beneficiaries. C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 18. Under this component, the project will support apprenticeship training for at least 25,000 individuals over the project implementation period. The main implementing agency for this component is COTVET. 29. Ghana has a longstanding traditional apprenticeship program where a young individual acquires vocational skills by working under a master craft person. Under the National TVET Qualification Framework (NTVETQF), approved by Parliament in 2012, apprentices become learners when they participate under the NTVETQF. Under an existing Government-supported initiative, participating employer and trade associations and cooperatives link individuals to apprenticeships. 30. Evidence from descriptive and evaluative studies of traditional apprenticeship training systems (and interventions that attempt to leverage traditional apprenticeship training systems) in Ghana and other developing countries indicates that the economic returns to such forms of training are limited. Markedly raising returns will require transforming the traditional system, in which the provision of training is generally basic, makeshift, and of poor quality. 31. Recent evidence from Ghana indicates that the typical individual attracted to apprenticeship training is 23 years old, has completed a little more than seven years of schooling, is a rural resident, is female, and has no or weak labor market attachment in terms of employment or hours worked.19 Evidence from the Ghana Living Standards Survey 2016/17 provides qualitatively similar results for those who report participating in an apprenticeship program. This is the expected beneficiary profile for apprenticeship training under this component. 32. This component aims to offer a more formalized, standardized and quality-assured apprenticeship system, which facilitates the uptake of technology. The component will make use of formal training providers for apprenticeship training delivery, underpinned by a sound quality assurance system and integrated more into the Government’s TVET strategy and system. 33. The project will support competency-based training to individuals. Apprenticeship training will combine workplace experience learning offered by a master craft person and an accredited training provider which may be classroom-based training. Individuals will be selected for apprenticeship training based on standardized criteria, which are relevant, transparent, and objective. Learners will seek at least a certificate of competency on the NTVETQF (which is an eight-level qualification framework). 34. This project will cover up to the fifth level in the NTVETQF, which is equivalent to the Higher National Diploma. Most individuals are expected to complete one or two levels of competency over 9−18 months. By supporting training up to the fifth level (which will entail the development of appropriate curricula and the training of trainers), the project supports the Government’s aim of promoting industrialization and providing the needed skilled workforce.20 19Hardy et al. 2019. 20Industrial subsectors of interest include agro-processing; pharmaceuticals; aluminum, iron, and steel; automobile assembly; textiles, garments, and apparel; petrochemicals; and machines and machinery components. The learner will also participate in a generic program offered by the training provider in the subjects of English, mathematics, science, information and communication technology (ICT), technological innovation, climate change mitigation and adaptation, health and safety, interpersonal relations, and general entrepreneurship. The project will also support literacy and numeracy training through nonformal education for apprentices, as may be necessary. To raise the quality of competency-based training, the project will support (a) Training of master craft persons who provide training under this component, to help ensure that their own skills are upgraded and that they can deliver training to their apprentices. Accredited training providers will train master craft persons to attain one or two competencies above the expected training level for apprentices; (b) Registration of public and private training providers and progress toward accreditation and the provision of competency-based training, in line with the NTVETQF; and (c) Capacity development of accrediting bodies to effectively perform their role in accrediting training providers. 37. To raise the relevance of apprenticeship training, the project will support the activation and operationalization of sector skill bodies, whose overall aim is to partner with the private sector to make training responsive to employer demand and to improve the governance and management of the training system. The sector skill bodies will strengthen industry standards and training curriculum, quality assurance, skills audits, and capacity development of private and public training providers. Specifically, the sector skill bodies will produce demand-driven labor market and skills intelligence, outline career pathways, develop occupational standards and assessments, and make recommendations to COTVET to ensure that qualifications, curricula, and learning materials reflect the occupational standards; to develop skills strategies; and to facilitate training. 38. The Government will also seek to assist apprenticeship training beneficiaries (master craft persons and apprentices) under this project to register with appropriate trade associations and to obtain social security, health insurance, Tax Identification Numbers, and formal worksite addresses. 39. The project will also support the capacity development of employer and trade associations and cooperatives to enable them to perform their roles of (a) selecting individuals for apprenticeship training, (b) linking individuals who are selected for training to master craft persons, (c) monitoring and supporting apprentices or learners and master craft persons, and (d) linking trained individuals to jobs. 40. The project will adopt the voucher mechanism under the existing Ghana TVET Voucher Project to train apprentices and master craft persons, using training providers. The financing mechanism includes an internet-based MIS, which tracks individual beneficiaries and monitors their progress and performance. The voucher mechanism was established, and is operated, by the Government with financing from the German Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and Development and the German Development Bank. The project incorporates lessons and successes from COTVET’s experience, including from the current TVET Voucher Project. Successes include the greater encouragement of the private sector in competency-based training delivery; the enhancement of the Ghana Education Service Technical Examination Unit as an awarding body of competency-based training; the enhancement of the investment of training institutes in processes, tools, and materials; and innovation by master craft persons in developing new tools and processes for raising the productivity of their income-generating activities. Lessons include the importance of eliminating fees paid by prospective apprentices to master craft persons to incentivize the take-up and continuation of training and making available training guidelines and other materials in printed form. COTVET has also learned that there needs to be an intensive effort to reach out and support the accreditation of training institutes in rural areas (currently all the accredited training institutes are in cities and towns) and of training institutes that can serve more than one region (such as those that are near regional borders). Most women who enter the labor market opt for private nonagricultural self-employment, and a large share of men do the same, as indicated by the Ghana Living Standards Survey 2016/17. Occupation information from the same survey suggests that self-employed workers tend to be employed in low- productivity, low-earnings activities. Individuals seeking to enter self-employment are likely to require intensive training in entrepreneurship and small business management, as well as needing business start- up capital for them to be able to enhance their employment outcomes. Key lessons learned for increasing the likelihood of business success include screening individuals for entrepreneurship training who already have vocational skills, conducting needs assessments to help align the level of training with the learning abilities of prospective training participants, having small class sizes and conducting study visits to businesses, developing networks or associations among training cohorts to aid cross-learning and cross-support, providing intensive mentorship and coaching after training as participants start and run their businesses (which helps the training participant to avoid delays and overcome hurdles in starting a business and helps sustain the participant’s motivation), and linking businesses to input and output markets. 53. Administrative information from past and current entrepreneurship training programs of NBSSI indicates that the typical participant is 21–30 years old, is female, and has a junior high school or senior high school education. This is the expected beneficiary profile for this subcomponent. 54. Entrepreneurship training. Three levels of entrepreneurship training will be offered: basic, intermediate, and advanced. The training will be based on standardized, quality-assured packages, customized to the baseline needs and abilities of the target beneficiary population in different locations. All participants will be expected to successfully participate in and complete basic training, which would take one week in total. Of those who complete basic training, 30 percent are expected to successfully participate in and complete intermediate training, which would take an additional two weeks in total. Advanced training, which all recipients of business start-up capital grants are expected to successfully participate in and complete, will take another three weeks in total. The training curriculum will include climate change mitigation and adaptation and occupational health and safety (OHS). 55. All training will be delivered using an experiential participatory approach conducive for adult learning. All training will also be district based and nonresidential. 56. All advanced training participants will receive mentoring and coaching support. This support encompasses guidance to prepare business plans, meet business registration and licensing requirements, and connect with input and output markets. 57. Before the training activities in each location, NBSSI will undertake outreach, sensitization and orientation, and a needs assessment. It will also screen and select individuals for entrepreneurship training based on standardized, transparent, and objective criteria. 58. Competitive business start-up grants. Those who successfully complete intermediate training will be eligible for competitive business start-up grants. These grants will be available to individuals or small, self-formed groups. Selection for the grant will (a) require the preparation of a business proposal and other requirements, with the aim of screening for motivated, promising applicants, and (b) be based on standardized criteria which are relevant, transparent, and objective. Grant size can vary based on a transparent, standardized formula, with absolute caps. Preliminary estimations indicate that the grant size is expected to range between US$1,000 and US$5,000. As relevant, a portion of the grant will be made available in cash to the grantee, to be used as working capital and to pay for business registration and licensing fees. As relevant, a portion of the grant will also be assigned for the purchase of needed tools; equipment; or labor-complementing, energy-efficient, and climate-smart technologies from Page 22 of 102 The World Bank Ghana Jobs and Skills Project (P166996) certified technology suppliers, with advice and support from the Ministry of Environment, Science, Technology, and Innovation (MESTI). F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 125. As discussed further in the technical and economic analysis section of this document (section IV.A), the skills development and job creation activities proposed under this project are consistent with recommendations from recent studies conducted for Ghana (for example, a review of the World Bank’s lending portfolio from a jobs lens and a synthesis of youth employment programs), as well as studies of youth employment programs globally which provide guidance for program design.28 These studies indicate the need to address multiple constraints to unlock labor market gains for individuals and recommend actions such as profiling individuals for entry into programs, provision of customized services, engagement with youth on design and implementation aspects, incentives for providers, and intensive monitoring for greater program success. These recommendations are reflected in the design of the project activities (sections II.B and III.B). The studies indicate that programs are more successful if they explicitly target jobs. The PDO-level indicators for the project are consistent with this recommendation (section II.A). 126. The studies also point out that greater gains can be achieved from supply-side interventions by concurrently addressing labor demand-side constraints related to enterprises. By relaxing potential constraints related to financial capital, technical knowhow, information, and behavioral elements, the project provides support, through competitive grants for training and complementary technology inputs, 27 World Bank 2018d, 2019b. 28 Datta et al. 2018a; Datta et al. 2018b. Complementing this specific effort, the parallel Ghana Economic Transformation Project (P166539) aims to improve the enabling business environment, and provide a range of direct support, for entrepreneurship and micro and small enterprise growth. 127. The project also considers important gender-specific lessons from the micro-entrepreneurship literature. The majority of microenterprises in low-income countries are owned by women.29 However, women-run enterprises tend to observe a lower return to capital than those of men, often due to the dual role that women play in balancing business considerations with household responsibilities.30 While evidence is absent on what additional interventions could potentially bridge the gender gap in the return to capital, the project, through Subcomponent 2.1, aims to promote women’s participation in micro - entrepreneurship and stimulate the productivity and earnings of these activities, including providing services tailored to address the constraints (household responsibilities and others) that women can experience. 128. A technically sound project design is a first step. Success hinges on effective implementation. Given this, the project is additionally designed to account for institutional capacity and the Government’s record of implementation progress and performance of World Bank-financed projects. Institutional arrangements for the project are fitted for inter-ministry coordination and co-ownership and oversight of the project. However, at a fundamental level, project components and activities are designed to be mutually exclusive across implementing ministries and agencies, reducing the risk of poor implementation performance due to interlocking parts. Notwithstanding, the project allows for interflows across project components, administered by different ministries and agencies, with the potential for enhanced labor market gains for individuals. For example, individuals who undergo apprenticeship training under Component 1 can seek (a) entrepreneurship training and competitive business start-up grants under Subcomponent 2.1, (b) employment in micro and small enterprises that receive competitive grants under Subcomponent 2.2, or (c) labor market information and job connection services from PECs under Component 3. 129. An integral part of this adjustment is stronger arrangements for monitoring and evaluation arrangements; the use of evidence and feedback for decision-making; citizen, civil society, and private sector engagement; and grievance management, features that are traditionally underprioritized in Government interventions. 130. IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical and Economic Analysis 149. Technical analysis. The choice of interventions supported by the project (apprenticeship training, entrepreneurship training, business start-up capital grants, grants to preexisting micro and small private enterprises, job connection services, and labor market information services) and their technical design features are consistent with available theory and international empirical evidence on generating and enhancing positive impacts on employment and labor earnings. 150. The size and significance of positive impacts of skills training programs on employment and labor earnings crucially depend on specific program design features. Programs found to be more effective include those that (a) screen for promising, motivated candidates for training, (b) combine workplace and classroom training, (c) combine technical and vocational training with individualized behavioral and other support, (d) assess trainees and provide feedback to them through their training period, and (e) 32 Republic of Ghana 2017. Page 42 of 102 The World Bank Ghana Jobs and Skills Project (P166996) implement sound quality assurance measures.33 These design features are reflected, and receive special attention, in the apprenticeship training component. 151. Evidence from Ghana evaluating what was in actuality the traditional apprenticeship training shows no short-run positive effects on the likelihood of employment and average labor earnings for both women and men.34 COTVET, through past, ongoing, and planned efforts, including through this project, is seeking to transform the traditional apprenticeship training into standardized, competency-based training which is quality-assured. An apprenticeship training intervention with similar aims and design features, and targeted to a similar population, as the intervention supported under this project was administered by the international development organization BRAC in Bangladesh. The intervention was found to have large short- and long-term positive effects on the likelihood of employment and average earnings, and larger labor market effects for women than men.35 The intervention was also found to have a substantial benefit-cost ratio. 152. Addressing multiple constraints, such as the lack of technical and vocational skills, business management knowhow, and financial capital, can produce positive impacts for individuals who choose self-employment.36 Consistent with this evidence, the project supports entrepreneurship training along with competitive business start-up capital grants for promising, motivated individuals and groups. 153. Evidence is largely absent on the impacts of grants for private enterprises. As one piece of evidence, an evaluation conducted in Nigeria finds that competitive grants awarded to promising, motivated enterprises produced positive impacts on enterprise survival, profits, and workforce size.37 Consistent with this evidence, the project supports competitive grants to private enterprises with high employment growth potential to train their workforce and improve business processes. 154. Job connection services can facilitate matching between job seekers and vacancies.38 Consistent with this evidence, the project supports the upgrading and strengthening of district PECs to more effectively provide job connection and labor market information services, underpinned by the GLMIS. 155. Page 88 of 102 The World Bank Ghana Jobs and Skills Project (P166996) ANNEX 1: Implementation Arrangements and Support Plan COUNTRY: Ghana Ghana Jobs and Skills Project Implementation Arrangements 1. The project is multi-sectoral, with multiple ministries and agencies partnering on project activities. This necessitates an encompassing arrangement, with a nodal authority. The overall institutional arrangements for coordinating, overseeing, and managing project activities comprise (a) a Project Inter- Ministerial Oversight Committee, (b) a Project Steering Committee, (c) a Project Coordinating Unit, (d) a Project Technical Committee, and (e) implementing and partnering agencies. The Government sees this hierarchical arrangement as efficient and pragmatic, and as providing for due diligence. However, the institutional arrangement for the project is untested in Ghana and will require close World Bank and high- level MOF oversight for early identification and resolution of issues. 2. Project Inter-Ministerial Oversight Committee. The primary function of this committee will be to provide overall strategic advice for the project and to ensure inter-ministry and inter-agency cooperation. Members of the committee will comprise the Ministers of Education; Employment and Labour Relations; Environment, Science, Technology, and Innovation; Finance; and Trade and Industry. 3. Project Steering Committee. The primary function of the committee will be to (a) approve annual work plans; (b) review project status, progress, and performance; (c) help further inter-ministry and inter- agency cooperation; and (d) resolve any outstanding project implementation issues. Members of the committee will comprise the Chief Directors or Directors or their representatives from COTVET, MELR, MESTI, MOF, and NBSSI. 4. Project Coordinating Unit. The primary functions of the unit will be to (a) convene and support oversight committee meetings; (b) prepare consolidated project status, progress, and performance reports for the oversight committee and the World Bank; (c) coordinate across implementing and partnering ministries and agencies; and (d) undertake due diligence and ensure quality assurance of, and accountability for, project implementation, including of financial management, procurement, and safeguards. The coordinating unit will be managed by MOF. 5. Project Technical Committee. This committee will be composed of officials from all coordinating, implementing, partnering agencies (COTVET, MELR, MESTI, MOF, and NBSSI), with the Project Coordinator as the chair of the committee. The main function of this committee will be to supervise and advise on technical aspects of project implementation. 6. Project implementing and partnering agencies. The main implementing agencies are COTVET, NBSSI, and MELR. The main partnering agency is MESTI. Implementing agencies will be primarily responsible for implementation of project activities that fall under their respective purviews, comprising all elements including financial management, procurement, safeguards, monitoring and evaluation, capacity development and technical assistance, and research and analysis. Page 89 of 102 The World Bank Ghana Jobs and Skills Project (P166996) 7. Component-specific implementation arrangements are as follows: (a) Component 1: Provision of apprenticeship training for jobs. COTVET will be the implementing agency for this component. COTVET will partner with the Alternative Education Agency to provide functional literacy and numeracy to apprentices. It will also partner with NBSSI to develop the capacity of employer and trade associations and cooperatives and to support master craft persons and apprentices with their businesses, drawing on NBSSI’s network of BACs. (b) Component 2: Provision of entrepreneurship and micro and small enterprise support for jobs Subcomponent 2.1. NBSSI will be the implementing agency for this subcomponent. MESTI will be a partnering agency. It will provide advice and support to grantees on labor- complementing, energy-efficient, and climate-smart technologies. Subcomponent 2.2. COTVET will be the implementing agency for the subcomponent. A dedicated SDF Oversight Committee composed of relevant Government, private sector, and development partner members will provide high-level oversight and guidance for the fund. A private sector member will chair the committee, and the committee will have majority representation by the private sector. Government representation will comprise MOF, COTVET, MELR, NBSSI, and MESTI. COTVET will competitively hire a firm consultancy to manage and operate the fund. COTVET will have primary oversight responsibility for the contracted firm. MESTI will be a partnering agency for this subcomponent. It will provide advice and support to grantees on labor-complementing, energy-efficient, and climate-smart technologies. NBSSI will also be a partnering agency for this subcomponent. Its BACs can provide facilitation and other support to grantees during or after grant implementation. (c) Component 3: Operationalization of the Ghana Labor Market Information System, upgrading of district PECs and services, and independent performance reviews of Government youth employment and skills development programs. MELR will be the implementing agency for this component. A GLMIS Technical Committee composed of representatives from relevant ministries and private sector and civil society organizations will provide guidance for the development, operationalization, and use of the GLMIS. A Program Review Technical Committee composed of representatives from relevant ministries will provide guidance on conducting performance reviews of, and providing technical assistance for reforming, selected youth employment and skills development programs. (d) Component 4: Capacity development, technical assistance, and project management support for enhanced skills and jobs impact. To meet needs for capacity development, technical assistance, and project-management support, the coordinating, implementing, and partnering agencies (COTVET, MELR, MOF, NBSSI, and MESTI) will implement their respective procurement of goods, works, and services. Page 90 of 102 The World Bank Ghana Jobs and Skills Project (P166996) Support Plan 8. The project implementation supervision and support plan is informed by lessons learned from supervising and supporting other World Bank-financed projects in Ghana. Notwithstanding, the multi- sectoral nature of the project is relatively new, and this can pose significant challenges for project implementation. Given this, planned supervision and support by the World Bank is expected to be intensive to allow for (a) oversight and proactive actions to address any identified weaknesses in project implementation; (b) timely development and implementation of needed risk-mitigation measures; and (c) timely and high-quality advisory and technical support for the design and implementation of data collection, research, and analysis activities to be carried out by third parties contracted by the Government using project funds. 9. Project implementation supervision and support by the World Bank will comprise at least two formal implementation review missions every year throughout the project period, with review findings and recommendations recorded in Aide Memoires and Implementation Status and Results Reports. The Government will also prepare implementation status reports prior to each supervision mission, with the necessary updates on results and indicators, implementation challenges, and risk-mitigation measures. In the intervening periods, shorter missions with more specific objectives of high priority may be carried out by country-based staff. Accra- and Washington-based World Bank staff and contracted local and international consultants will maintain continuous project implementation supervision and technical and advisory support through in-person, audioconference, and videoconference meetings, and email and phone communications. Modalities for Disbursements under the DLI-based IPF components (Components 1 and 2) 26. The total resource allocation to be implemented by a result-based financing approach using DLIs is estimated at US$160 million under Components 1 and 2 of the project. As per the design, disbursement under this component is tied to the achievement of results which will be measured and valued in monetary terms for each respective year through a set of identifiable and measurable DLIs over the project implementation period. 27. Satisfactorily achieving the defined DLIs as identified in the PAD and in the Financial Agreement will constitute the primary basis for triggering credit disbursements under the project. The DLIs have been individually priced, and as such the eligible disbursement amount will be the sum of the achieved DLIs multiplied by the unitary monetary value (price) as per the disbursement schedule. 28. Since this is an IPF, the underlying principle will be to disburse an initial advance, after project effectiveness. The initial advance shall be based on a forecast of the funding required to potentially achieve the set of DLIs in each year, based on the approved Annual Work Plan and Budget. Subsequently on a half-yearly basis, the Recipient will provide satisfactory documentary evidence including (a) acceptable IUFRs, (b) EEP spending reports, and (c) evidence for verification of the set of DLIs for that specific year which have been achieved. These reports will then form the basis of documenting the advances made into the Designated Account. Further advances will be made on the approval of the net cash requirements (next six months forecast of expenditure) as reviewed and approved by the World Bank. 29. It must be noted that, while noncompliance with a DLI in a period will result in funds associated with that DLI being withheld, disbursement associated with the achievement of other DLIs will not be affected. In situations where the project can meet any of the DLIs beyond the value of the total advances granted, the project can request for such earned amounts as a reimbursement into any Government account other than the Designated Account. 30. Where achievement of a DLI cannot be certified, an amount equivalent to the DLI value will be withheld or considered as undocumented and outstanding obligation on the Recipient. This amount will be paid at any later date, during project life, and at the discretion of the World Bank’s task team when such achievement can be verified. The task team may consider that a later achievement of the DLI performance would not qualify for disbursement against the unmet DLI if it determines that the on- schedule (timely) achievement of the DLI is critically fundamental to achieving the overall objectives of the project. A schedule of the DLI matrix, with the indicative DLI value, is included in the Annex to Schedule 2 in the Financing Agreement.
