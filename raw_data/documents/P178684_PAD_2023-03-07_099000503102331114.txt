STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) is the largest country by area in Sub-Saharan Africa – with an estimated population of 92.4 million.1 With its abundant and diverse natural resources, including critical minerals needed for the green energy transition, a strategic location at the center of Africa, and a large and young population, DRC has considerable economic potential. However, weak governance and institutions, mismanagement of natural resources, and protracted conflict and violence have all hindered its progress in developing human capital and infrastructure, leading to economic underperformance and high levels of poverty. Due to its long history of conflict, political upheaval and instability, and authoritarian rule, DRC is also a fragile country. 2. The third-largest population of people living in poverty in the world lives in DRC and the number of poor is growing. Poverty reduction is hampered by high population growth—the population growing at an average of three percent from 1961 to 2020—combined with historic weak economic growth, with a real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth rate of 1.6 percent over the same period.2 This resulted in a 60 percent decline in GDP per capita in 2020 compared to its 1960 level. Although the two existing household budget surveys (2005 and 2012) have revealed a decline in extreme poverty from 94.3 to 77.2 percent, the number of poor people has increased by about 1.5 million every year. The extreme poverty rate (established at US$2.15 per day in 2012, 2017 PPPs) was 62.3 percent in 2022. The economic consequences of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, through rising global food costs and higher oil prices, could exert stronger pressure on fiscal deficit, on inflation and on households’ consumption thus exacerbating poverty and inequality.3 3. DRC ranked 164th out of 174 countries on the 2020 Human Capital Index (HCI), reflecting decades of conflict and fragility, and limiting development and growth. The country has an HCI score of 0.37, which is below the 0.40 average for Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), meaning that a Congolese child born today can expect to achieve only 37 percent of his/her potential, compared to what would have been possible if provided with a full, quality schooling experience and optimal health conditions. The main contributors to this low HCI are the low child survival rate of children under the age of 5 years (0.91), stunted growth of children (the share of non-stunted children is 0.57) and quality of education (while a 4-year-old child is expected to complete 9.1 years of schooling before his/her 18th birthday, factoring in what children actually learn, the number of expected years of schooling is only 4.5). While girls fare better in terms of the health components of HCI, they lag behind boys both in terms of expected years of schooling (4.3 for girls and 4.7 for boys) and learning outcomes (Harmonized Test Scores of 308 for girls and 312 for boys).4 4. DRC ranked 151st out of 170 countries in the 2021 Gender Inequality Index, which assesses national gender gaps using reproductive health, empowerment, and the labor market as its key benchmarks.5 Only 14.3 percent of women hold parliamentary seats. Similarly, the Gender Development Index is 0.885, indicating that women’s human development is about 89 percent that of men’s (UNDP 2022). Across DRC, girls and women face profound and interconnected disadvantages compared to men. The COVID-19 pandemic contributed to widening existing gender gaps, especially for women and girls who were most affected by COVID-19 restrictions and school closures. First, according to interviews8 with school principals, attendance dropped after schools reopened, following a seven-month closure period due to COVID-19 restrictions. Second, tensions in the food supply emerged in the areas most affected by COVID-19, resulting in a rise in malnutrition among young children, as evidenced by surveys undertaken by the Social Sciences Analytics Cell (SSAC). Further, there was a decrease in the use of contraception, an increase in pregnancies, and a decrease in the use of prenatal care. The increase in early pregnancies is pushing young girls out of school. Finally, the incidence of sexual- and gender-based violence increased dramatically, with rates more than doubling since the beginning of the COVID-19 crisis.9 6. Addressing gender disparities, by investing in girls’ empowerment, learning, and health, and by expanding their economic opportunities will be critical for the Government to revitalize the economy and put the country on a path towards sustainable development. Achieving lasting results in improving girls’ education will be particularly important as it can be a powerful transformative force for the girls, their communities, and the economy at large. Girls’ education, particularly at the secondary level10, is a factor that consistently positively influences not only girls’ lives, but also improves many other development outcomes, such as a reduction in child and maternal mortality, improvements in educational outcomes of their offspring, a reduction in poverty and the achievement of equitable growth11. 7. Climate change poses a serious risk to DRC’s sustainable development. Due to a combination of political, geographic, and social factors, DRC ranks 177th out of 181 on the 2018 Notre Dame Global Gain Index,12 illustrating its high vulnerability to climate change. DRC is therefore one of the most climate vulnerable countries globally. Additionally, DRC is the 5th least prepared country in addressing climate risks and vulnerabilities13 – highlighting high vulnerability to the effects of climate change, as well as a lack of preparedness in making investments to address it.14 8. DRC suffers from one of the world’s most complex and protracted humanitarian crises in the world. The humanitarian situation continues to deteriorate in many parts of the country, particularly in the eastern provinces, where there has been a recent increase in conflict and violence. Attacks against civilians, including internally displaced people (IDPs), have increased over the past year, especially in North Kivu, South Kivu, and Ituri. Sectoral and Institutional Context 9. Primary and secondary education in DRC includes public and private schools, with public schools managed under two regimes: écoles conventionnées (schools under convention) and écoles non-conventionnées (schools not under convention).16 In 2020, public schools constituted most schools: 87 percent in primary education and 84 percent in secondary education. Écoles conventionnées account for 80 percent of public elementary schools and 78 percent of public secondary schools17. They are run by faith-based organizations (FBOs) under a 1977 agreement18. This agreement establishes that the State “organizes” education and churches “manage” the schools. The State defines the curriculum, oversees quality, and pays staff and operating costs. The FBOs hire teachers and mobilize additional resources as needed, including for new infrastructure. They have their own administration and inspection services. Écoles non-conventionnées are managed directly by the Government. Both types of schools are supervised by administrative structures called management offices (bureaux gestionnaires; BGs). Secondary school enrollment levels are low. The net enrollment rate (NER) in lower secondary is 31 percent, and 34 percent in upper secondary, with large differences between provinces (Figure 1). Among provinces of focus, as per the 2022-26 World Bank Group (WBG) Country Partnership Framework (CPF), some have very low levels of female participation, particularly the central Kasai provinces and the eastern provinces of Ituri and South Kivu. 12. Girls are less likely than boys to make the transition from primary to lower secondary school and to complete lower and upper secondary education. There are also wide variations across provinces. In 2020, girls’ passing rate for primary education’s final examination was estimated at 78 percent nationally, 2 percentage points lower than that of boys. Among the ten provinces of focus under the FY22-26 CPF, girls perform worse than boys in the end-of-cycle examination in eight provinces, the exceptions being Kasai Oriental and Kwilu.23 Gaps widen further in the transition between primary and lower secondary, with girls on average nine percentage points less likely to complete primary school and go on to lower secondary school.24 Furthermore, girls are less likely to complete upper secondary education25, with a completion rate for boys of 46 percent and of only 29 percent for girls. Primary and lower secondary completion rates are higher for boys than girls in most provinces. Rural girls are particularly vulnerable as they are 23 percentage points less likely than rural boys to enter grade 12. 13. Learning levels can be improved. No independent assessment of learning outcomes in secondary education has been completed, but evidence suggests that learning levels in secondary schools are not optimal. At the start of secondary education, only 27 percent of students have met the minimum threshold for language competency, and 18 percent have met the minimum threshold for mathematics.26 In 2022, only 64 percent of candidates passed the secondary certification examination (administered at the end of upper secondary school). 14. Of the 27,168 public secondary schools, 7,391 have a student-classroom ratio (SCR) of 50 or above in Grade 7 and 5,237 in Grade 8.30 In addition, the existing infrastructure in many public schools is in poor condition. The scarcity of water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) facilities negatively impacts girls’ participation and performance.32 The lack of private, gender-segregated toilets with running water makes it difficult for girls to manage menstrual hygiene at school and makes them more likely to stay home during menstruation. This lack of adequate facilities leads to gaps in their attendance, undermines their academic achievement, and increases the risk that they will drop out of school entirely.33 18. DRC has low connectivity penetration rates and weak Information Communications Technology (ICT) infrastructure. The mobile phone penetration stands at 46 percent of the population (compared to 83 percent in SSA) and Internet penetration is at 14 percent (compared to 30 percent in SSA). Only about 13 percent of secondary schools have electricity.34 Geolocation statistics indicate that over 84 percent of secondary schools have access to a cellular network,35 although national 3G coverage is only 20 percent (compared to 60 percent on average throughout Africa), with almost no LTE (Long-Term Evolution) coverage.36 19. Widespread harassment of students by teachers, including sexual abuse, exploitation and sexual harassment (SEA/SH), has been linked to dropouts in secondary schools in DRC. Physical and sexual violence against girls is higher in DRC than in neighboring countries. Teenage girls and younger women are particularly vulnerable, and local community engagement in school management practices is not sufficient to reduce such pervasiveness. Of the girls aged 15 to 19, 38 percent have experienced physical violence and 16 percent have experienced sexual violence.37 The scarcity of female teachers is one of the contributing factors to this situation. Female teachers represent only 16 percent of the workforce in public secondary education43.44 Various factors contribute to this low rate, including the relative difficulty women (compared to men) face to relocate, financial insecurity if the teaching position is not permanent and salaried, a work environment that is not always welcoming to women, and traditional norms pertaining to professional roles at the secondary level. Household poverty is one of the main obstacles to secondary school participation, particularly for girls. The proportion of secondary-school age girls who are not in school ranges from 12 percent among the wealthiest quintile to 45 percent among the bottom quintiles. Gender disparity also increases with poverty. While the proportion of out-of- school children in the top quintile is roughly equal for girls and boys, the gender disparity steadily increases as the household becomes poorer, rising to 13 percentage points for the poorest quintile (45/32 percent for girls/boys).52 The main constraints are the direct and opportunity costs associated with schooling. SEA/SH in the school environment are a threat to girls’ participation and well-being and require a robust and multi-dimensional set of measures for mitigation and response. The implementation of a SEA/SH reporting and response system under EESSE project has revealed that GBV affecting girls at the secondary level is not uncommon, particularly in the Kasai provinces. The project will build on the SEA/SH accountability and response framework, as well as the reporting and referral system that was developed under EESSE, to offer similar mitigation, protection and response measures in secondary schools in the target provinces (see the six sets of activities described in paragraph 54). 63. Results-based financing is effective in supporting policy implementation. The ongoing EESSE project has demonstrated that disbursement-linked results incentivize the MEPST and the Government in general to implement policy. Results to date include the universal signing of codes of conduct, the appointment of school-level focal points to address SEA/SH, and the addition of over 100,000 teachers to the payroll. The project therefore incorporates performance-based conditions to incentivize the recruitment of female teachers – the lack of which constitutes an important barrier to creating and sustaining a girl-friendly school environment. Also building on lessons learned, PBC targets and protocols have been formulated to include carefully crafted language to avoid any ambiguity in the interpretation of results and provide adequate guidance for a rigorous verification process in which all parties can have confidence. In addition, the project’s support to female teacher recruitment will benefit from and build upon other reforms supported under EESSE, notably the introduction of merit-based recruitment and the strengthening of policy and procedures pertaining to career paths and professional development. 64. Lifting barriers to access is effective in increasing participation. At the primary level, the Government’s policy introducing fee-free schooling has conclusively demonstrated that lifting barriers to participation identified by households results in increased enrollment. The project’s design addresses the main access barriers identified at the secondary level, particularly those related to adequate infrastructure and (in Kasai Province) financial constraints. These will particularly benefit girls, as they are disproportionately affected by increased distances to school, lack of adequate sanitation facilities to manage menstrual hygiene, and the direct and opportunity costs associated with schooling. 65. Pre-service teacher training and professional development systems need further integration. In general, these two systems have tended to operate in isolation, though (Quality and Relevance of Secondary and Tertiary Education Project, PEQPESU, P149233) made progress in this area by supporting the alignment of teacher college programs with revised secondary curricula. The project will build on this, primarily by continuing to ensure such alignment of curricula strengthened with project funding, by supporting teacher training college (ISP) staff to participate in the training of key Réseaux d’Ecoles de Proximité (Proximity School Network – REP) members), and by equipping the ISPs with Smart Labs in order to enable them to build the capacities of their teacher trainees to use IT for education, and to develop content for CTPD that will be made accessible to teachers in the target provinces. 66. Curriculum strengthening requires integrated operational support to be effective. Prior donor support to strengthen curricula and syllabi, including from the World Bank, has sometimes stopped short of ensuring the revised education programs are actually delivered to students in schools. There are instances of curricular documents being revised but not delivered to schools, reflected in updated textbooks, or integrated into teacher professional development programs (pre- or in-service). The project will work to remedy such shortcomings, ensuring that curricula strengthened under PEQPESU are delivered to the responsible CUPs at school level; that the CUPs and local inspectors are sufficiently familiar with their new approach and content so that this information can be further communicated to all relevant teachers and integrated into teacher mentoring practices; and that they are incorporated in newly developed textbooks. Sustainable teacher professional development requires the strengthening and quality control of local systems. Prior donor-supported projects, including World Bank-financed operations (such as PAQUE, P157922 and PEQPESU, P149233), have tended to support teacher professional development through short-term and cascade trainings targeting project-specific themes. Their one-off nature limits their impact; the targeted themes may not be tailored to local weaknesses in teaching practice; the multiple cascade levels combined with logistical challenges that are common in many areas of the DRC are such that it has been difficult to maintain quality in training delivery when large numbers of teachers are involved; the lack of follow-up has failed to build on the initial training; and the lack of monitoring has made it difficult to determine what, if anything, has improved in the classroom. The project builds upon lessons and best practices from around the region and the world by strengthening local systems for continuous teacher professional development (CTPD), and by building the capacities of key members of the REPs who are the front-line staff responsible for ensuring quality in teaching. In this approach, the project will provide these members with the tools and resources needed to observe teachers on a continuous basis and provide them with tailored feedback. In addition, a training model that minimizes cascade levels and hence slippages in quality will be used to build the capacity of these members and will be complemented by ongoing monitoring of classroom practices to discern changes towards more effective teaching and identify course corrections as needed. Finally, CTPD is more effective when linked to career paths that recognize the value of professional development and incentivize participation in professional development activities. In this regard, the project will benefit from reforms being supported under EESSE to strengthen career development, as noted above. 68. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis Technical Analysis 85. The project focuses on increasing participation and improving the quality of teaching-learning conditions. As such, it responds to the major needs at the secondary level its design is evidence-based. The inclusion of civil works and conditional scholarships components addresses the major constraints on access and participation, particularly for girls, and should also contribute to quality in light of the literature on the importance of the physical environment for learning. 86. The WDR 201862 recommends that learning be prioritized. To do so, it is crucial to assess learning, to base actions on evidence, and to ensure that all stakeholders share this focus, with all inputs being dedicated to strengthening teacher- learner interaction. The project supports the conduct of the first-ever learning assessment in secondary schools, as well as the first-ever assessment of teaching-learning practices. The project design reflects stakeholders’ shared focus: activities for curricula strengthening, pre-service training, continuous teacher professional development, and the design of textbooks and other teaching-learning materials are all interconnected. In addition, the project emphasizes the use of inputs to strengthen teacher-learner interaction by including activities to build teacher capacities and operationalize school-level systems to promote the effective use of textbooks and other teaching-learning resources, including information technology. 87. Another key message of the WDR 2018 is that teacher skills matter, yet most teacher training is ineffective. The report notes that targeted training with coaching aligned to best classroom practice can have benefits. The project’s design reflects this support for continuous professional development. Using a valid instrument for the observation and assessment of teaching-learning practices to be designed/adapted under the project, the project will develop the capacities of key personnel at the school and local level responsible for coaching to use the instrument, identify f strengths and weaknesses, and provide mentoring feedback. Further, the project will provide the coach and the teacher with resources on effective teaching-learning practices. 88. The issues of girls’ security and cultural norms discouraging continued female participation are addressed in the project design. Evidence indicates that girls experience SEA/SH at school. But, while they are exposed to risks during travel to school (though less so than in their villages), girls are less likely to experience violence at school. Negative cultural norms and girls’ limited control over their own lives are manifested for example in the disparity in enrollment rates between boys and girls in secondary schools, and in the high proportion of girls who give birth before their 18th birthday. Therefore, the use of civil works in the project to increase girls’ enrollment and reduce travel times is warranted; while the various measures included to mitigate and respond to risks of SEA/SH, and to create an empowering culture for girls (including through girls’ clubs, the inclusion of SRH in the curricula, and the recruitment of female teachers) are justified. 89. Technology can be a powerful tool in education, as the capacity of IT to store large amounts of content holds out the promise of addressing one of the key constraints at the secondary level, namely the shortage of teaching-learning materials. But the cost effectiveness, sustainability, and affordability of IT have to be considered carefully. Data source/ Agency Independent Verification Agency (IVA) and MEPST/DINACOPE Verification Entity IVA (DLRs 1.1 to 1.4) For the Year 2 DLR (DLR 1.1), the MEPST works in collaboration with local education authorities to ensure that 1,250 qualified female teachers have been recruited to vacant teaching posts of public secondary schools, which posts are in one of the ten provinces targeted by the project and appear on the DINACOPE payroll list as being mechanized and budgeted. The recruitment must be conducted on a merit basis, following the principles and procedures approved as part of the merit-based recruitment program developed and approved by MEPST as part of the Bank-financed operation, EESSE (P172341). Once the 1,250 teachers have been recruited and all of them have occupied their post for a period of at least 12 months and have received their monthly salary for each and every month they occupied their post, the MEPST informs the Procedure World Bank the DLR has been achieved. The MEPST contracts an IVA to verify that for each of the 1,250 posts the following conditions have been met: (i) prior to being vacant, the post was not occupied by a woman; (ii) the post is mechanized and budgeted, and appears on the DINACOPE payroll; (iii) the teacher has the formal qualifications necessary to occupy the post, as per MEPST rules and regulations; (iv) the teacher was recruited in conformity with the merit-based recruitment program approved by the MEPST subsequent to support of P172341; and (v) the teacher has received her salary for each and every month since occupying the post, and that this period is not less than 12 months. The sampling and detailed procedures for verification (for this and all subsequent years) will follow a methodology elaborated in the Project Page 58 of 74 The World Bank DRC Girls Learning and Empowerment Project (P178684) Implementation Manual (PIM). The methodology will include sampling of personnel files and financial institution records, as well as a sample involving physical verification. The physical verification will also include confirmation that the person indicated on the payroll is indeed the person occupying the post. Once the IVA verifies that the DLR has been achieved, the Project Coordination Team (PCT) informs the World Bank that the DLR has been achieved, providing the IVA report and supporting evidence. The World Bank disburses according to the formula given below, provided the Bank validates the verification evidence. The amount disbursed is in direct proportion to the numerical extent to which the target was achieved. That is, whatever percentage of the target is achieved, the same percentage of the amount allocated to the DLR may be made available for withdrawal by the Recipient. For example, if 100 per cent of the target is achieved (i.e. 1,250 teachers are verified to have satisfied the conditions elaborated above), then 100 per cent of the amount allocated to the DLR may be made available for withdrawal by the Recipient. If 20 per cent of the target is achieved (i.e. 250 teachers), then 20 per cent of the amount allocated to the DLR may be made available for withdrawal by the Recipient. The formula for DLR 1.1 may also be phrased as follows: USD 20,000 is made available for withdrawal by the Recipient for each percentage point achieved towards the target. (If not a whole number, the percentage point is rounded off to the nearest whole number). The verification procedure for Year 3 is the same, with one addition. Once an additional 1,250 teachers have been recruited and all of them have occupied their posts for at least 12 months, the project contracts an IVA to verify achievement of the result. Not only does the project verify that an additional 1,250 teachers have been recruited and have received their salary for at least 12 months, it also verifies that the posts to which teachers were hired in previous years (in this case, Year 1), and which have already been previously verified, continue to be occupied by female teachers and that these teachers continue to receive their salary every month. That is, the IVA verifies that the additional 1,250 teachers meet all the conditions identified above and that the prior years teachers (in this case from Year 2) have continued to receive their salary every month and are female. As before, once the IVA verifies that the DLR has been achieved, the Project Coordination Team (PCT) informs the World Bank that the DLR has been achieved, providing the IVA report and supporting evidence. The World Bank disburses according to the formula given below, provided the Bank validates the verification evidence. The amount disbursed is in direct proportion to the numerical extent to which the target was achieved. That is, whatever percentage of the target is achieved, the same percentage of the amount allocated to the DLR may be made available for withdrawal by the Recipient. For example, if 100 per cent of the target is achieved (i.e. cumulatively 2,500 teachers are verified to have been recruited to vacant posts, that the newly added 1,250 teachers have received their salary for at least 12 months and that the 1,250 prior years teachers have been regularly paid since being added to the payroll), then the full amount allocated to the DLR may be made available for withdrawal by the Recipient. If 40 per cent of the target is achieved Page 59 of 74 The World Bank DRC Girls Learning and Empowerment Project (P178684) (i.e. 1,000 teachers), then 40 per cent of the amount allocated to the DLR may be made available for withdrawal by the Recipient. The formula for DLR 1.2 may also be phrased as follows: USD 35,000 is made available for withdrawal by the Recipient for each percentage point achieved towards the target. The verification procedures for Years 4-5, as well as the principle underlying the formulae for disbursement, are the same as for Year 3. Given that the values of DLRs 1.3 and 1.4 are particular to each year, their formulae may also be phrased as follows. For DLR 1.3, USD 45,000 is made available for withdrawal by the Recipient for each percentage point achieved towards the target. For DLR 1.4, USD 60,000 is made available for withdrawal by the Recipient for each percentage point achieved towards the target. Targets may be achieved and disbursed against ahead of or behind the 5 year schedule, provided that the recruitments and salary payments meet the conditions elaborated above; and that the eligible expenditures are equal to or greater than the value of the PBC targets that have been achieved. For example, if by Year 4 no female teachers have been newly recruited to vacant posts and occupied their post for at least 12 months ; yet in Year 5, 5,000 female teachers have been recruited to vacant posts and paid for at least 12 months, and all the other conditions provided above are satisfied and verified: then the project can disburse all four PBC awards (for DLRs 1.1 to 1.4) for a total of USD 16 million. In all such cases, the amount that can be disbursed is also limited by the amount of eligible expenditures that have been accumulated, i.e. the amount to be disbursed cannot exceed the amount of eligible expenditures that have been accumulated. Country PFM situation and Use of Country Systems 18. The latest 2019 Public Expenditure and Financial Accountability (PEFA) assessment conducted highlights weak PFM performance especially in budget reliability and predictability and management of assets and liabilities, while the other pillars (budget transparency and comprehensiveness, budget cycle, predictability and control, accounting and reporting, external and internal audit) are considered basic. Despite some improvement, several provisions of the public finances law have not yet been implemented, such as the multi-year programmatic budgeting and the Single treasury account. Also, the establishment of a national network of public accountants recommended by the general regulation of public accounting is not yet effective, which leads to the absence of general State accounting and the absence of a declaration of compliance by the Supreme Audit Institution. Other main weaknesses are related to the excessive use of exceptional procedures in the expenditures chain, the excessive centralization of budget execution authority at central government, through the Ministry of Finance and the Ministry of the Budget but, a biased situation of the general treasury account linked to the presence of several bank accounts not subject to reconciliation. Disbursement and flow of funds arrangements 24. Disbursements for the IPF will be made in accordance with the World Bank Disbursement Guidelines for Projects, dated February 1, 2017. The DA will be replenished through Withdrawal Applications supported with quarterly unaudited IFRs. The PIM will provide details as to the management of the DA. The other disbursement methods which are: reimbursements, direct payments and special commitments are still available to the Recipient. The Government has a system and processes in place for the traceability of expenditures . DINACOPE is the government agency with the main responsibility to manage the process starting from the enrollment of teachers in the database, the periodic physical verification at schools, the preparation of the payroll, and the processing of school operating costs. Payment agencies transfer funds directly to the beneficiaries’ accounts. The PCT in collaboration with all the stakeholders of the public expenditure chain will work on a procedure from the identification of female teachers in the payroll till the payment of their salaries and ensure a reconciliation of the amount received from the payment agencies. Unaudited Interim Financial Reports. The financial management team of the PCT will be required to prepare an IFR on a quarterly basis. The formats and contents of the IFRs have been agreed upon between the World Bank and the implementing agencies at negotiation. The IFR reports will be submitted to the World Bank 45 days after the close of each quarter. The IFRs will include the following (i) a narrative on progress achieved during the period on activities implementation, to provide context for financial information analysis; (ii) a sources and uses of funds statement, presenting the situation of the period and the cumulative one since the beginning of the project; (iii) a statement of the use of funds by components, for the report period and cumulatively; (iv) the designated accounts reconciliation; (v) the disbursement forecasts of the upcoming six months; the situation of achieved PBC; (vi) summary of eligible expenses for the period; (vii) the annual budget analysis on accrual compared to forecast; (viii) the situation of unjustified advances and relevant explanation; (ix) the financial situation of contract under implementation. The FM manual will cover the IFR preparation procedure.
