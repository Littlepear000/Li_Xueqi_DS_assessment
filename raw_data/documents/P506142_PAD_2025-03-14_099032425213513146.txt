STRATEGIC CONTEXT 1. This Project Appraisal Document (PAD) covers phase two of the Transforming Brazil’s Agrifood System Multi-Phase Programmatic Approach (MPA) approved by the World Bank’s Executive Directors on November 7, 2024 along with the first phase (P180429, Report PAD00054). A. Country and State Context 2. Brazil’s real GDP grew by 3.2 percent in 2023 and is estimated to have maintained the same pace in 2024, driven by strong consumption, supported by a resilient labor market and fiscal transfers. Growth in 2023 was fueled by private consumption, fiscal stimulus, and declining inflation, while exports benefited from a record grain harvest and favorable external conditions. From January to September 2024, GDP grew 3.3 percent compared to the same period a year prior, underpinned by solid consumption and recovering investment. Growth is expected to moderate to 2.2 percent in 2025 and stabilize at 2.3 percent in the medium term, reflecting gains from recent structural reforms. The Government has made progress in improving the business environment, financial sector, and indirect taxation. However, faster, inclusive, long-term growth requires additional reforms to enhance competitiveness, reduce financial and market distortions, expand infrastructure investment, integrate into global value chains, and improve education quality. 3. In 2023, Brazil’s poverty rate (US$6.85 per day) fell to 21.3 percent, linked to improvements in economic conditions and social protection policies. Unemployment reached 7.1 percent, the lowest since 2014, reaching women, youth, and Afro-Brazilians. The Bolsa Familia Program1 helped to reduce poverty: coverage expanded by 2 million families, reaching 21.3 million, with the average monthly transfer increasing from R$ 394.48 to R$ 670.36. Finally, the real minimum wage increased by 2.8 percent, boosting the incomes of about 24.5 percent of the households in the bottom 40 percent with at least one formal worker. With sustained job growth, the poverty rate is projected to further decrease to 20.5 percent by 2026. 4. Brazil is expected to experience rapid changes in climate conditions, with impacts that will vary spatially. By the end of the century, the average temperature in Brazil is expected to rise, depending on the trajectory of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, by 1.7 °C to 5.3 °C from the 1986–2005 average.2 All of Brazil’s biomes are vulnerable to the impact of changes in temperature and rainfall, though with significant regional differences.3 In rural areas, more frequent and severe climate events, such as droughts and floods, are likely to cause major impacts on agriculture productivity, logistics (i.e., lowering conditions of rural roads) and delivery of water and sanitation services due to dwindling water supplies. 5. Though one of Brazil’s most developed states in terms of human development and economic performance, the State of Santa Catarina is a relatively small state in southern Brazil, with 3 percent of the country's population (7,610,361 people in 2022), and the 10th largest economy in Brazil. The State has experienced positive growth in recent years, the GDP per capita of R$ 58,401 is the third highest across Brazil’s states. In 2024, Santa Catarina exports reached US$11.6 billion, making in the 10th largest exporter in Brazil. 6. Despite the relevance and economic growth of Santa Catarina, inclusion remains a challenge in the State, along with the development of public policies that improve the livelihoods of the most vulnerable populations. Climate risks are amongst the most important risks that farmers of Santa Catarina must face.5 Climate risks in the State include drought, flooding, hail and frost that threaten rural and fishing lifestyles, agricultural sustainability, and the ability to meet rising demands for food, fibers, and biomass. The most immediate and tangible effects on Santa Catarina's production chains are related to erratic rainfall. Intense, prolonged droughts have negatively impacted water supply, leading to productivity losses, increased vulnerability to and outbreaks of pests and diseases, and higher production costs. Additionally, changes in rainfall patterns have resulted in more frequent short-duration, high-volume floods, causing crop damage, livestock and infrastructure losses, soil erosion and even deaths. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. Brazil’s agrifood system plays an important role in the national economy and in the livelihoods of the poor. The Brazilian agrifood sector is highly heterogenous. There are nearly 5 million rural properties distributed over 65 million of hectares of land, of which 3.9 million are classified as family farms. The sector plays an important role in the national economy (the primary agriculture sector accounts for 5 percent of the national GDP, and when, considering the entire agrifood system, its contribution reaches 26 percent). While the country’s agrifood system has performed well on aggregate, many family farmers still struggle. Rural poverty is estimated at 38.7 percent, more than double the urban poverty rate.6 Despite the recent growth and future potential of the agrifood system in Brazil, there are several challenges to be overcome: (a) low coverage is compounded by the poor-quality of agriculture public goods and services provided7; (b) low incomes of many family farmers, that can be attributed, in part, to their lack of minimum asset endowment; (c) the need to improve the sustainability of the agrifood system to enhance the climate resilience of farmers and decrease the negative impacts of the sector to climate and more generally the environment; (d) the lack of access to financing for sustainable agrifood system development, despite the use of preferential credit schemes as a key agriculture public support policy; and (e) the marginalization of women within the agrifood system. 9. Agriculture in Santa Catarina has the 10th highest added value in Brazil and contributes 6.7 percent of the value added by the local economy. The 2023 Value of Agricultural Production (VPA) reached US$13.28 billion and in the last 10 years it has had an average real growth of more than 2.5 percent per year. Despite the great diversification of agriculture in Santa Catarina, almost 60 percent of VPA results from just four activities: swine, 20.2 percent; poultry, 16.4 percent; milk, 12.3 percent; and soybeans, 10.9 percent. In 2023, the State’s agribusiness exports had the second-best performance in history, with US$7.49 billion exported, 65 percent of the total value of Santa Catarina's exports in 2023 and 4.5 percent of the total agricultural exports of Brazil. 10. In Santa Catarina, the productive structure of agriculture is characterized by the strength of family farming and the predominance of small rural properties. In the State, 183,000 agricultural establishments are registered, 78 percent of which are family farms, with a total area of 6.45 million hectares (ha) and an average area of 35.2 ha per establishment. The agrifood system employs more than 500,000 people.8 The State has a long history of collective action among farmers. As of 2017, 194 farmers organizations had been registered with a total of 15,759 associates (8 percent of farmers). Despite its growth and contribution to the country and State economy, the agrifood system in Santa Catarina faces several challenges to ensure more resilient and inclusive growth. 12. Challenge #1 – Delivery of last-mile agricultural public goods and services. The literature shows that agricultural public goods like rural infrastructure (rural roads, electrification, irrigation, information and communication technology, etc.) and services (land administration, food safety and agriculture health, agriculture innovation etc.) are fundamental for addressing food security and sustainable development.10 The State of Santa Catarina invests only 0.7 percent of its agricultural GDP in agricultural public goods and services. This level is below the OECD average (more than 5 percent), and what was observed in São Paulo (1.2 percent). Of the total State investment in public goods in the sector, 24 percent corresponds to agricultural innovation (Research and Development, extension) and the rest is distributed in infrastructure, health inspection, control services, and others.11 Unlike many other states in Brazil, most farmers in Santa Catarina receive some technical assistance and extension services. Between 55 and 60 percent of family farmers are attended by the State Company of Agricultural Research and Rural Extension (Empresa de Pesquisa Agropecuária e Extensão Rural de Santa Catarina – EPAGRI). Yet the State faces the need to maintain and strengthen these public services to the sector. The vast majority of rural communities are located in remote areas, leading to socioeconomic isolation due to deficient communication infrastructure for internet access and mobile telephony. Additionally, the logistical structure is compromised by undersized and poorly maintained roads, making them more vulnerable to climate change, which hinder the transport of inputs and production, thereby limiting the development of regions and production chains. 13. Challenge #2 - Building assets for an inclusive agrifood system. Many family farmers in the State still struggle with poverty, with household incomes falling below the monthly threshold to benefit from the Bolsa Família program.12 It is estimated that around 22 percent of family farmers of the State are benefiting from the Bolsa Família program. This struggle can be attributed, in part, to the lack of minimum asset endowment. There are also significant challenges in the inclusion of family farmers with limited land and impoverished rural populations, as well as Traditional People and Communities (TPCs). Twenty percent (37,000) of farmers in Santa Catarina have farms primarily for subsistence production and there are 67,700 agricultural establishments (37 percent of the total) with less than 10 hectares.13 Current literature highlights the significance of minimum asset ownership for production.14 Also, recent research provides compelling evidence for the effectiveness of supporting rural households through various productive inclusion phases using a multifaceted "graduation approach" and "productive alliances�? programs.15 These approaches and programs, combining productive asset transfers, technical and business management assistance, and access to saving accounts, have demonstrably led to long-term sustained increases in income and consumption for participating family farmers, therefore, lifting them out of poverty. 14. Challenge #4 - Agrifinancing for sustainable agrifood system development. In Santa Catarina, access to rural credit is higher than in the rest of Brazil18, but still quite low with 29 percent of farmers accessing some form of agrifinance.19 Furthermore, the current available agrifinance instruments could be strengthened to provide incentives for the adoption of CSA practices and technologies, contributing towards improvement in agrifood productivity, environmental goals, and improved livelihoods. In Santa Catarina, access to subsidized credit is conditioned, like the federal level, to the farmer having a registered CAR. The State also implements direct support to farmers to foster environmental compliance and PSE, but this only represents 1 percent of total direct support that the State provides to farmers.20 16. Challenge #5 – Enhancing women participation in agrifood system development. There are significant gender inequalities in family farming in Santa Catarina. Women run only 16.9 percent of the State’s family farms, and their farms are significantly smaller than those run by men – the average area of female-run family farms corresponds to only 72.9 percent of the area of male-run family farms. There is also a relevant gender gap in terms of access to technical guidance. While 84 percent of female farmers and 90 percent of male farmers have access to technical information through different media, the female participation in technical meetings and seminars is very unbalanced: only 22 percent of female farmers participate in these events, while 36 percent of the male farmers do. Participation in cooperatives and community associations is also highly imbalanced by gender – a gap of 17 percent in terms of participation in cooperatives and 15 percent in community associations. Finally, data on access to credit and access to agricultural extension services also show huge gender gaps. Thus, data from the Center for Socioeconomy and Agricultural Planning from EPAGRI shows that, in 2023, female family farmers (a) obtained only 14.7 percent of rural credit contracts made in the State; and (b) contracted only 16.6 percent of the amounts financed. These gender gaps on access to land, knowledge-sharing networks, credit and social capital reverberate in relevant inequalities in terms of the relevance of the earnings from agricultural production, participation in the market between male and female family farmers in the state and income distribution. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 17. The MPA and the Project are relevant for the World Bank Group’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Brazil (FY24–FY28). It aligns with the existing CPF Higher Level Objectives (HLOs) of: (a) greater productivity and employment; (b) greater inclusion of the poor and underserved populations; and (c) greener economy with reduced vulnerability to climate shocks. The MPA’s and Project’s alignment are realized through prioritized investments within the proposed pillars of the MPA. The pillar/Component 1 purpose is to enhance access to direct support by family farmers (including indigenous people and traditional communities) to overcome market failures. In this case, it will remove liquidity constraints these beneficiaries face when adopting new technologies and farming practices, leading to higher productivity, improved resilience to climate shocks and environmental sustainability. Investments under this pillar could contribute to HLO (a), (b) and (c). The pillar/Component 2 aims to strengthen the provision of agricultural public services and goods, with a focus on strengthening agrifood innovation systems, land administration, food safety and agriculture health and rehabilitation and expansion of last mile rural infrastructure. The investments under this pillar could improve agriculture productivity and access to markets in lagging areas. Therefore, the proposed pillar will contribute to the CPF HLOs (a) and (b). 18. The MPA and the Project are consistent with Brazil’s Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) and National Adaptation Plan (NAP). In the latest update of NDC21, Brazil pledged to reduce emissions by 48 percent compared to 2005 levels by 2025 and by 53 percent by 2030. Also, Brazil aims to achieve climate neutrality by 2050. Agriculture and environmental sector mitigation priorities in the NDC include strengthening low-carbon agriculture approaches, restoration of degraded pasture, integrated agroforestry systems, maintenance of ecosystem services through sustainable use of natural resources and promotion of sustainable agricultural practices, strengthening of protected areas, and environmental and forest management. The Project is aligned with these mitigation priorities through the adoption of low-carbon and CSA technologies and practices and support to restoration and conservation of natural resources and environmental management (Components 1 and 2) that would contribute to GHG emissions reduction from improved cropping systems, livestock management and better agriculture management, and increase carbon storage in soil and vegetation. Brazil's NAP purpose is to develop adaptation strategies to improve resilience to climate change, ensure food security and increase productivity. The NAP’s priorities include improving agricultural risk and vulnerability monitoring and assessing vulnerability of Indigenous Peoples to climate change. It also aims to strengthen agricultural extension services to deal with climate change mitigation and adaptation. The Project will contribute to these efforts through investments to build a more productive and resilient agrifood system (agriculture innovation on low-carbon and resilient agriculture, increased monitoring of climate and pests and diseases risks, climate resilient infrastructure in Component 2 and investments in fostering the integration of climate mitigation and resilience within public policies and programs in Component 3). Finally, the MPA and the Project respond to the agrifood system priorities identified in the World Bank Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) for Brazil22, which includes increasing agricultural productivity and climate gains as priority actions to be financed. The Project, furthermore, supports the World Bank’s Climate Change Action Plan 2021-25 and the Roadmap for Climate Action in Latin America and the Caribbean. Finally, the Project will contribute to gender equity objectives as proposed in the World Bank’s Gender Strategy 2024-2030, in particular strategic objective 2: Expand and enable women’s economic opportunities in rural areas of Santa Catarina. 19. D. Multiphase Programmatic Approach 20. The MPA Program enables a gradual expansion of agrifood public goods and services, and direct support to family farmers, to transform agrifood systems and a unique opportunity to inform public policies and programs. It fosters collaboration across federal and state governments to unify Brazil's fragmented agrifood policies into a cohesive national strategy, by (a) developing a shared national vision, (b) creating a tailored menu of investments for different government tiers, and (c) implementing a learning agenda through a robust monitoring, evaluation, and knowledge system, including evaluation of existing agrifood policies to identify challenges and generate knowledge for refining and scaling effective policies. The MPA's will be implemented through a three-pillar strategy: (a) Pillar 1: Provides direct support to family farmers and their organizations, reducing financial barriers to productive investments; (b) Pillar 2: Enhances the delivery of last-mile agrifood public goods and services to create better conditions for agrifood system development; and (c) Pillar 3: Creates a feedback loop where governments can shift towards green, resilient, and inclusive agrifood policies. 21. The MPA is structured into twelve phases over a 10-year period, concluding in 2034. It aims to benefit approximately 404,000 family farmer households (1.1 million people), including indigenous and traditional communities, with 50 percent of beneficiaries expected to be women. The program will also support farmers' organizations such as cooperatives and associations, impacting a broader range of agrifood sector stakeholders. 22. There is no update to the original MPA Program Results Chain. The MPA Program Development Objective (PrDO) remains the same and is to enhance resilience of family farmers in targeted states. The PrDO will be monitored by measuring progress on four program outcomes: (a) Increase in gross value of sales by family farmers and rural organizations in the agri-food system (disaggregated by male, female, and youth); (b) People with strengthened food and nutrition security (disaggregated by female, and youth) (CRI); (c) Hectares of terrestrial and aquatic areas under enhanced conservation and management (CRI); (d) People with enhanced resilience to climate risk (disaggregated by female, and youth) (CRI). Each proposed phase of the MPA will contribute to one or more PrDOs indicators, as appropriate, and it will also add relevant intermediary outcome indicators. D. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners 38. The challenges that the agrifood system in Santa Catarina faces are linked to various market failures, leading to lack of access to markets, low productivity, environmental degradation and low climate resilience of family farmers and TPCs. Public financing through this Project will be employed to reach areas where private sector investment incentives are not yet adequate to support family farmers without additional incentives, in line with the principles of maximizing finance for development. The Project will therefore seek to overcome these market failures by: (a) reducing liquidity constraints of family farmers through direct support to farmers and greater access to financial services (Component 1); (b) enhancing coverage and quality of agricultural public goods and services (Component 2); and (c) enabling the environment for greater private sector investment through repurposed public policies and programs (Component 3). 13 The World Bank Santa Catarina Rural Development Project for Sustainability and Innovation (P506142) 39. IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis 50. The Project will support investments in rural areas, promoting a more sustainable and climate-resilient agricultural production and a more competitive agrifood system, through increased access to finance and technical assistance, increased access to agricultural public goods and services, and better monitoring and analyses of agricultural policies and programs to ensure they are designed to achieve the results intended. The Project will be targeting more vulnerable territories and in particular traditionally excluded groups (women, TPCs) hence fostering their inclusion in the activities supported by the Project. The Project is also expected to strengthen interinstitutional coordination and operational capacity of key implementing agencies for improved public services in rural areas. 51. The Project is also expected to generate environmental benefits, such as restoration of degraded areas, recovery of springs and riparian vegetation along watercourses, increased adoption of climate-smart agricultural practices resulting in the provision of ecosystem services and a reduction in GHG emissions for the involved family farmers, in particular and the rural population in general. 52. The Economic and Financial Analysis demonstrates that the Project is expected to generate net incremental benefits for family farmers, rural entrepreneurs and communities, including vulnerable priority groups in the Project area. For the financial analysis, a cash-flow analysis to present the “with�? and “without�? scenario was conducted for the twenty identified models representing eight subsectors46, using a 10-year analysis period, and a discount rate of 10 percent.47 The results of such models demonstrate that there are opportunities for feasible businesses in all subsectors. Key-indicators of the analysis include net margins and return on labor. 53. The economic analysis, was carried out by converting the financial analysis to social prices, using conversion factors, opportunity cost parameters, and removing taxes and transfers. These economic benefits were then aggregated based on the phasing of Project results using a 15-year period of analysis, and a discount rate of 10 percent. An adoption rate ranging from 85 percent to 100 percent is also considered. The results of the ex-ante analysis of the economic profitability of the Project can be considered satisfactory, since the Internal Rate of Return (IRR) reaches a value of 16.7 percent, and the Net Present Value (NPV) corresponds to a value of US$53.4 million. This means that the Project generates sufficient benefit to recoup the initial investment and provides an additional benefit of US$53.4 million. Furthermore, the provision of ecosystem services was integrated into the analysis. These include water supply, climate regulation and water flow, air quality, erosion prevention, soil fertility maintenance, and pollination services. Economic values were taken from a recent global synthesis48 and calculated by landscape type, assuming weighting based on the latest forest cover and land use map of the State of Santa Catarina.49 The analysis considers a situation without the Project, where ecosystems provide 30 percent of their potential services, and a situation with Project, where they provide 80 percent (an increase of 50 percent). Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a project supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms or the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non-compliance with its policies and procedures, and the Dispute Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the attention of Bank Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond. The Institutional and Implementation Arrangements consider a multi-sectoral approach and is based on the Project’s scope and risk profile, as well as on the lessons learned from the implementation of previous operations with the State of Santa Catarina. The arrangements consider the current physical and human resources available in SAR, which will perform the role of Project coordinator and oversight, as well as EPAGRI and CIDASC that will be implementing part of the operation.52 2. Borrower and implementing agency. The State of Santa Catarina, through the Secretary of Agriculture and Livestock (SAR) will be the Borrower for the proposed loan, with the Federative Republic of Brazil serving as Guarantor. SAR will be supported in the implementation of Project’s activities by SAR’s affiliates, EPAGRI and CIDASC. 3. Project Management. SAR will establish an Executive Secretariat (SAR/SEE) that will act as the Project Management Unit (PMU) The PMU will ensure that counterpart resources are foreseen in the State’s budget. PMU’s fiduciary and environmental and social risk management teams will be trained on the World Bank Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers and Environmental and Social Standards, respectively. The PMU will be composed at the minimum of a general manager under the responsibility of the Executive Secretary, one specialist for monitoring and evaluation, a financial specialist, a procurement specialist, and one social and one environmental specialist. A Compliance unit, reporting directly to the Executive Secretary will ensure the monitoring and evaluation of Projects’ results, the compliance of Projects’ activities with WB rules and procedures, including the management of environmental and social risks, and he knowledge and learning agenda. 4. Project implementation. SAR/SEE, in collaboration with EPAGRI and CIDASC and other partner institutions (IMA, SIE, SAQ) will be responsible for formulating, implementing, and monitoring the Project’s sectoral interventions and results. The PMU will (a) elaborate the annual operational plan; (b) issue relevant technical or legal norms to support Project’s implementation; (c) prepare technical specifications and terms of reference for the activities to be financed by the Project; (d) monitor Project implementation and results; and (e) liaise with other governmental bodies to coordinate and implement intersectoral activities or for monitoring purposes. Technical Cooperation Agreements will be signed between SAR and institutions involved in the implementation of the Project (IMA, SAQ, SIE). EPAGRI and CIDASC will assign specialists responsible for managing procurement, fiduciary and environmental and social risks that will act, as Project Implementation Units (PIUs) under the general coordination of SAR/SEE. EPAGRI and CIDASC will also assign a technical focal point to ensure the planning, implementation, and monitoring of Project’s activities to be executed by their institution and will act under the overall coordination of the Director of the Technical Unit of the PMU. Within the PMU, the Investment Unit will oversee revising the proposed subprojects to be cofinanced under Component 1, ensuring the compliance with the selection criteria and procedures, Project’s objectives and will play an advisory role, in case improvement can be done to the investment proposal. At the regional level, coordination of Project’s activities will be ensured through EPAGRI’s regional directorate, under the general coordination of the PMU’s Project Manager.
