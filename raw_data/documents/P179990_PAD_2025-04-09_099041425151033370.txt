STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Bosnia and Herzegovina (BiH) has enjoyed macroeconomic stability over the last decade. Real GDP growth was driven by a surge in exports and robust growth in private consumption. However, a key economic challenge for BiH is the imbalance of its economic model: public policies and incentives are skewed toward the public rather than the private sector, consumption rather than investment, and imports rather than exports. Furthermore, the country’s economic growth over the last decade failed to generate enough jobs to improve employment outcomes for much of the population, resulting in still-high unemployment at around 16 percent (Labor Force Survey, 2019) and a steady outmigration rate, most prevalent among the youth. Between 2011 and 2019, BiH lost approximately 20,000 jobs, spread across many sectors. The country needs to shift to a business environment, making it conducive to private investment. Growing the private sector requires support to both vibrant small and medium-sized enterprises and the growth of larger companies, facilitates export performance and productivity improvements, and generates much- needed private sector employment. While addressing these imbalances in the economic model, the country must also ensure the sustainability and inclusiveness of future growth. 2. Climate change will have an impact on BiH’s energy sector. Climate impacts anticipated in the country include higher amounts and intensity of extreme weather events (ice-breaks, windfall, droughts, floods, thunderstorms), more frequent winds of higher intensity, and a rise in temperature in all seasons. Some of these impacts could directly affect the energy sector by reducing the availability of cooling water for thermal power plants, decreasing the efficiency of thermal power plant operations, disrupting energy supply infrastructure, potential flooding of open pit mines and increasing risk of soil erosion in existing operations. 1 Such impacts alongside BiH’s heavy reliance on coal-based power generation provide an opportunity for the country to address both mitigation and adaptation. 3. BiH is uniquely placed to fully decarbonize its power sector by 2050, while still retaining its energy independence and remaining one of the leading electricity exporters in the region. In fact, despite large capital investment requirements, BiH can afford to transition to a lower carbon economy and could lead the Western Balkans in decarbonization efforts. As outlined in the January 2024 Country Economic Memorandum for Bosnia and Herzegovina, decarbonizing the energy sector will require capital investments in the range from US$ 5.87 to US$ 7.51 billion by 2050. 2 The country is party to all key international agreements governing climate change. 3 Additionally, at the regional level, BiH is a contracting party to the Energy Community Treaty, and a signatory to the Sofia Declaration on the Green Agenda for the Western Balkans, which aligns with the European Union’s Green Deal. Domestically, BiH has pledged to cut greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 33.2 percent by 2030 as part of its updated Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs). A new Climate Change Adaptation and Low-Emission Development Strategy for 2050 is under preparation and BiH is furthermore finalizing the National Energy and Climate Plan (NECP), which defines targets (and sets policy measures) for energy efficiency, coal reduction, renewable energy, and GHG emissions by 2030. 4 Reducing coal dependence in the energy system is the first and most crucial step to achieving BiH’s domestic decarbonization targets. 1 https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/NAP-Bosnia-and-Herzegovina%20.pdf 2 https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/1ef09553-5a61-4ad8-822d-761dbff8b34d 3 The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement on Climate Change since 2002, 2007, and 2016 respectively. 4 Amongst the inputs to the NECP’s elaboration has been a Road Map for Just Transition in BiH Coal Regions, supported by the World Bank. Decarbonization in BiH requires considerable reduction in coal consumption and, therefore, coal production. At present, coal accounts for over 60 percent of GHG emissions, equivalent to 7.1 metric tons of CO2 emissions per capita. In 2022, 64 percent of domestic electricity was produced by coal-fired thermal power plants (TPPs), fed by 11 domestic coal mines with over 12,000 direct employees working in the sector (Map 1). This makes BiH the second largest coal producer and employer in the Western Balkans, after Serbia. In recent years, BiH produced approximately 14 million metric tons of coal per year, split evenly between Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBiH) and Republika Srpska (RS), used almost entirely to fire domestic TPPs. Though unbundling of the power sector has commenced, most coal- related operations remain integrated within the state-owned utilities of FBiH and RS 5. In FBiH, the state-owned power utility, Elektroprivreda Bosne i Hercegovine (EPBiH), operates seven of the Federation’s eight coal mines. Banovici mine, which is over 69 percent owned by the government of FBiH, is the only coal mine not under EPBiH’s portfolio. Banovići, Đurđevik and Kreka mines supply the Tuzla TPP (715 MW), while the other five mines supply the Kakanj TPP (450 MW). In RS, the state-owned utility Elektroprivreda Republika Srpska (ERS) owns and operates the Gacko and Ugljevik mines, which serve the nearby TPPs of the same names (300 MW each). As with FBiH, one further mine operates independent of ERS—Stanari—which supplies its own TPP (300 MW). Map 1: Coal Mines in BiH 5. Private ownership in power generation in BiH is limited to approximately 15 percent of the total installed capacity. Power transmission and distribution (T&D) are 100 percent owned by public utilities. The vast majority of large generation facilities as well as the T&D grids were constructed decades ago during the period of exclusive public sector ownership. Nevertheless, even for very mature technologies like wind and solar, the private sector plays a very limited role. The main reasons for this include: (i) the overall political instability and geopolitical tensions in the country; (ii) failure of BiH to implement the EU’s Third Energy Package; (iii) absence of the liquid wholesale power market; (iv) lengthy permitting processes; and (v) an incomplete legal framework. Important strides have been made since October 2020 when the State-level Ministry of Foreign Trade and Economic Relations (MOFTER) initiated broad coal transition strategic planning. In December 2023 a Just Transition in Coal Regions of BiH Road Map 6 was released, setting out the broad parameters and framework for coal transition, in line with the Sofia Declaration and other international obligations. Work is now required at the entity levels to develop and adopt policies and programs for coal transition. In May 2024 the FBiH government adopted an Information with Conclusion on “Actions taken towards Just Transition activities in FBiH in areas where active coal production is carried out” and its accompanying decision on an Inter-Ministerial Committee on Just Transition.7 Furthermore, a law has been introduced to the FBiH government detailing further obligations relating to the full closure of the Zenica mine. Amongst other things, the Committee is charged with defining tasks and responsibilities of individual stakeholders to implement the strategic documents, programs, and policies related to Just Transition process. It is composed of key ministries with competencies and responsibilities related to sectors involved in Just Transition (mining, labor, environment, spatial planning, to name a few). Important will be the capacity of the Federation Ministry of Mining, Energy and Industry (FMERI)—who regulates the coal sector—to provide strong inter-ministerial coordination and effective leadership with EPBIH who maintains almost 90 percent of the Federation’s coal production. RS will now need to initiate similar efforts at the entity level, with the Ministry of Energy and Mining (MEM) assuming a lead coordination function with relevant ministries concerned with Just Transition, together with ERS. At the State level, MOFTER will continue to play a lead coordinating function bringing actors from both entities together to discuss and learn best practices related to a Just Transition. 8 7. Transitioning communities and gradually closing coal mining operations is a complex process affecting multiple sectors, involving several layers of government, and implementing interventions that have yet to be fully tested in BiH. Beyond typical liabilities and risks associated with mine closure, underground mines in BiH are at significant risk of methane explosions, self-ignited coal fires, and atmospheric methane leakages. Capacity building is required to help government institutions and agencies establish and supervise closure processes in line with international best practice. Downsizing the workforce is unpopular and disruptive to the local economy, which complicates the political economy of decarbonization. Even the technical design of effective labor transition policies is challenging given the difficult labor market in terms of limited local labor demand. Few alternative employment opportunities match the profiles and expectations of mine workers, and this will contribute to a prolonged transition period of lower employment levels and uncertainty in the communities. Additional capacity within the mining companies and public employment services (PES) is required to manage appropriately retrenchments and the transition of workers into alternative work, yet PES has limited capacity to provide adequate counselling and there are no measures or arrangements in place to support re-skilling or upskilling. Opportunities to repurpose the country’s 200-400 km2 of post-mining land require clear ownership and land use regulations will need to be clarified beforehand. The demand for land will be driven by the spatial requirements for renewable energy with investments such as photovoltaics (PV), energy crops, and energy storage facilities, and by demand for quality lands for high-value economic investments. Closing inoperable mines (or sections of active mines that are no longer in use) is a priority to lay foundations for a Just Transition in BiH’s coal regions. Progressive mine closure can ensure that environmental and social liabilities and risks are proactively managed and minimized across the entire portfolio of BiH coal mines, create transition models for replication, and seek to progressively mitigate the scale of socio-economic impacts facing local coal mining communities. In FBiH, EPBiH and RMU Banovići face major operational challenges. EPBiH cross-subsidizes the poor financial performance of its mines through profits made from electricity sales. EPBiH’s coal mines have incurred substantial losses due to, amongst other reasons, technical challenges requiring high operating budgets and high labor costs for disabled workers. Both EPBiH and RMU Banovici face liquidity risks due to liabilities associated with taxes, social arrears, and concession fee arrears. 9 Equally, both face significant social and environmental challenges due to inefficient mine practices leading to unusually high numbers of disabled workers in the mines, and environmental and safety hazards noted on sites. EPBIH is the largest coal mine employer in the Federation with 6,371 workers between its seven mines and RMU Banovici employing another 2,640 workers. EPBiH has implemented a first three-year mine reform plan (2021-2023) focused on restructuring, capital investment, and workforce reductions. EPBiH has also drafted and approved a decarbonization plan applicable to the period, up until 2050. Their indebtedness and limited resources only increase the potential for further liability and harm. Moreover, responsible closure is complicated by the lack of a mine closure fund or remediation bond that would guarantee funds were made available to do the works in a timely manner. 9. The coal mines for intervention in this lending operation are at different stages of their life-of-mine cycle. Banovici and Zenica (the two mines with most project activities) have contrasting operating circumstances, each requiring fit- for-mine closure activities and safeguard measures. Zenica is an underground hard coal mine with most of its working footprint in subsurface domain. It is also at the end of its life-of-mine cycle. Coal production was suspended in 2023, but due to existing debts and lack of financial resources required to properly close the operation, the mine continues to operate under care and maintenance arrangements. Full closure according to FBiH environmental laws will require substantial financial resources. Most costs related to the closure of Zenica are labor, with only roughly one-third related to the removal of equipment, environmental mitigation of water and methane, and demolition of surface buildings. Banovici’s mine footprint is the opposite of Zenica, with the majority in surface domain and life-of-mine cycle not at its immediate end. Given the sizeable surface area inhabited by the mining operation, a sequential closure of inactive surface areas followed by investment in new economic activities on the post-mining lands could serve as part of a larger transition strategy for this heavily dependent coal mining town. Referred to as progressive or contemporaneous closure, this type of closure is highly effective for open pit lignite operations as it enables less significant financial outlays at the end of the mine’s life cycle. If managed properly, progressive mine closure can help mitigate sudden economic disruptions in coal-dependent economies by planning labor and economic alternatives over a longer timeframe. The remaining two mines subject to this operation (Kreka and Kakanj) have some closed surface pits and subsurface underground workings with potential for clean energy and other types of repurposing while the mines continue to operate for the coming 5-years. There is, therefore, an opportunity to apply progressive closure principles in these two latter cases, similar to that envisaged for Banovici. C. BiH’s CPF (FY23-FY27, Report No 172387-BA) recognizes that high emissions from the country’s coal-dominated energy sector leaves it vulnerable to external shocks (particularly with reference to the European Union Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism to come onstream in January 2026). The proposed project, with a focus on Just Transition in BiH coal regions, will directly contribute to High Level Objective 1 (increased inclusive private sector employment), High Level Objective 3 (improved environmental outcomes and climate change resilience), and the cross-cutting theme of closing gender gaps by: (1) piloting reskilling programs for disproportionately affected male workers and community members in coal regions to qualify for jobs in others sectors; (2) supporting the decarbonization of the BiH economy by enabling “just transitions” in coal producing regions through former mining land reclamation and repurposing, and renewable power generation; (3) improving BiH’s air quality by promoting a modern approach to coal phase down and repurposing of thermal power plants and associated coal mines. 11. Gender. Men currently employed in the coal sector will be disproportionately affected as coal mines start to phase down. For mine employees, their livelihoods are at risk because of mine downsizing and closures, and this in turn threatens household consumption and welfare. Transitioning to alternative jobs will be key to smoothing the potentially disruptive economic and social impacts. Most workers will not be able to navigate the impact of job loss welfare costs, given that wages in mines are above market-levels. Under sub-component 4.2 (Paragraph 27), project activities will include labor transition support (job search counselling, outplacement services, upskilling opportunities) to displaced mine employees to facilitate transition to alternative jobs and decrease the risk of long-term unemployment.10 This transition assistance is especially important in BiH’s coal regions, where few alternative job vacancies are likely to attract mine employees, and the risks of prolonged unemployment increase the vulnerability of mining households. To complement this transition support, the project also considers international evidence on potential increases in negative social behaviors such as substance abuse and gender-based violence following large economic shocks like job loss. As such, under sub-component 4.3, psychosocial support will be offered to affected mine employees and their partners to mitigate the increased risk of domestic violence. 12. Citizen Engagement. The proposed operation has a clearly defined scope in line with the World Bank’s Just Transition in Coal Regions framework. As per the framework, a Just Transition is an approach towards coal phase down that addresses the needs of workers and surrounding communities affected directly or indirectly by the shift away from coal; provides clean energy access to millions of people; repurposes mining lands and other assets for new economic use. The framework can be useful guidance for countries to organize planning for transition but is not meant to be prescriptive in its use. The framework draws attention to the need to treat governance, people and communities, and land and asset repurposing in equal measure. The framework draws together nearly 10 years of stock-taking through collaboration across the World Bank to understand how coal transition has happened in the past and how it can be better designed and managed in the future. The proposed operation will be the first World Bank IPF operation to apply this framework. 17. The proposed operation supports reinforcing a strong institutional framework for a Just Transition, progressive closing of mines, reclaiming and repurposing mining lands for new use, introducing clean energy, as well as supporting mine workers and communities in economic transition. The project prioritizes mines most advanced in closure readiness. Zenica mine will be the focus with immediate need for support to responsibly close one underground working (Raspotocje) and support redeployment of mine workers. This first operation will also initiate preliminary interventions in Banovici mine such as PV installation, a pilot voluntary separation program, and a community-focused pilot for early economic diversification measures. PV installation will also be targeted in Kreka, given the investment’s readiness. 18. The proposed operation’s scope is designed to be both adaptable and scalable. Coal transition requires a long-term commitment with interventions to address significant structural and legacy issues across a portfolio of mines in BIH. F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 35. Since 2016, the World Bank has taken stock of lessons from prior investments in coal sector adjustments— supported both by the World Bank and other institutions. Lessons span over four decades of coal mine closures in multiple countries across the globe. Lessons have been well documented through Support to Energy Transition in Coal Regions (P171194) and catalogued on the World Bank’s Just Transition landing page (https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/extractiveindustries/justtransition). These various knowledge products provide clarity on why certain approaches and measures to transition in coal mining regions have failed to produce sustainable results for communities. Some flagship reports include Managing Coal Mine Closure: Achieving a Just Transition for All, Global Perspectives on Coal Jobs and Managing Labor Transition Out of Coal, Just Transition for All: A Feminist Approach for the Coal Sector, Repurposing Coal Lands for a Just Transition, and ECA Coal Regions Just Transition Approaches Note (forthcoming in Open Knowledge Repository). 36. As outlined in Managing Coal Mine Closure: Achieving a Just Transition for All, a Just Transition can be conceptualized broadly in three (3) stages : (1) pre-closure planning, (2) mine closure and repurposing, and (3) regional economic transition. The report further estimates that at a bare minimum these phases require fifteen (15) to twenty (20) years to complete. Even then, as evidence shows in countries such as Canada, USA and Germany, regional economic transformation can continue for many decades after actual mine closure. BiH is in stage 1 (pre-closure planning), with piloting to commence on certain approaches to achieve a just coal sector transition. These new approaches are informed by World Bank lessons above and include: (i) innovative models for labor dismissals, reskilling and retraining, and general interventions in host mining communities; (ii) prioritizing land repurposing as an entry point at the outset of the planning process; (iii) ensuring that governance structures are inclusive and hybrid in nature; and (iv) mitigating major mine closure risks to communities, such as methane leakages. 37. The proposed project has adopted several novel approaches based on past lessons to improve outcomes and ensure greater chance of sustainability of interventions. Areas include (i) anticipated and adequate support to labor dismissals, reskilling and retraining, and general interventions in host mining communities; (ii) prioritizing land repurposing as an entry point at the outset of planning processes; (iii) undertaking methane abatement; and (iv) ensuring that governance structures are inclusive and hybrid in nature. The intention would be to test approaches particularly in this first operation for adaptation and replication in subsequence support (whether as additional financing or a new standalone operation). Specific thematic peer-to-peer learning groups will be established between beneficiaries to ensure cross-fertilization and sharing of lessons from mine to mine. These will be the subject of focus for the following learning agenda: (i) Closure of underground workings according to international best practice and standards for coal mines; (ii) Land repurposing; (iii) Labor and community transition; and (iv) Governance. 38. Lessons from the project will be shared – both internally in the World Bank and externally with global actors— through a variety of established learning and exchange platforms. Within the institutions, there are a few channels to promote learning on Just Transition for a variety of operations that are under upstream preparations. Notably, Page 12 The World Bank Just Transition in Select Coal Regions of BiH (P179990) within the World Bank, the “Energy & Extractives” Global Department (EEX) leads internal quarterly meetings on Just Transition with other units such as the “Social Sustainability and Inclusion”, “Social Protection and Jobs” and “Macroeconomic, Trade and Investment” Global Departments. It emphasizes governance, people and communities, and land and other asset repurposing. The design is also informed by upstream ASA performed by the World Bank from 2020-2022. 44. The economic viability of the proposed project is evaluated using a standard cost-benefit analysis (CBA), which measures the Economic Net Present Value (ENPV) based on quantifiable economic costs and benefits attributable to the project. This analysis adheres to World Bank guidelines and compares scenarios with and without the project for each targeted mine. It assesses the costs and benefits related to: (i) the installation of solar PV plants on repurposed post-mining land; and (ii) the closure of selected underground workings. To present costs and benefits at a common point in time, a country-specific economic discount rate of 7 percent is used 13. Per World Bank guidelines, results of the CBA are reported first without greenhouse (GHG) emissions, then with GHG emissions by applying shadow prices of carbon (SPCs) from the low and high price trajectories defined in the World Bank's updated guidance note on “The Shadow Price of Carbon in Economic Analysis.” 14 The input data and assumptions for this analysis are based on the best information available at the time of project appraisal. 45. Results for Banovici and Kreka Mines: Given the difficulty of quantifying the multiple benefits, this project falls within the category of projects that are economically viable only when accounting for GHG emission reductions and applying the SPC, as established in the 2024 Guidance Note on Shadow Price of Carbon in Economic Analysis. In accordance with this guidance, the economic analysis results demonstrate that land repurposing and solar PV installations in Banovici and Kreka become economically viable once the benefits of avoided GHG emissions are considered. 15 Without these benefits, the projects yield a slightly negative ENPV (Table 1). However, when factoring in the avoided emissions, the ENPV increases to US$11.9 million (low SPC trajectory) and US$28.2 million (high SPC trajectory) for Banovici, and US$9.2 million (low SPC trajectory) and US$22.2 million (high SPC trajectory) for Kreka (Table 2), confirming their overall economic viability under decarbonization-driven valuation approaches. 46. Results for Zenica Mine: Although the closure of the Raspotočje section in Zenica yields a negative ENPV of US$4.5 million in the low SPC trajectory, it improves to negative US$1.1 million in the high SPC trajectory. It is important to note that the economic analysis of Raspotočje considers only the atmospheric benefits of reduced methane emissions. Due to data limitations, additional social and economic benefits—such as improved health outcomes, labor transition benefits, and reduced environmental risks—are not captured, despite their significant value. Furthermore, the analysis assumes a flaring scenario for methane abatement rather than a utilization scenario, taking a conservative approach. This scenario accounts for emissions from both combusted and un-combusted methane, as well as the energy used for methane capture. Additionally, the flaring rate is expected to decrease over time, yet the analysis conservatively evaluates only the first 25 years of the methane abatement facility, even though such facilities can operate much longer.
