Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Angola is characterized by limited economic diversification and low labor productivity, which, combined with its poverty levels and weak human capital outcomes, further constrain its growth prospects. As the fifth-largest economy and second-largest oil producer in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), Angola's growth was sustained by oil revenue until recent years, resulting in volatile growth and limited economic diversification.1 The share of wage jobs remains limited, with more than 73 percent of new jobs in the non-wage sector, mainly in low-productivity micro-businesses. These trends contribute to high levels of poverty, and inequality: 31.1 percent of Angolans were considered "extremely poor," (i.e., below the international poverty line US$2.15/day) in 2018, significantly above the lower middle-income country (LMIC) average of 11.3 percent) and its Gini coefficient of 0.51 is one of the highest globally. 2 Angola’s Human Capital Index (HCI) of 0.36 is among the lowest in SSA ( below SSA, and LMIC averages of 0.40 and 0.48), hindering current and future productivity and growth. Poor households are often larger (with female headed households more likely to live in poverty) and girls have a lower HCI than boys. Angola's climate vulnerability threatens agricultural and fisheries productivity, 3,4, impacting food security and livelihoods of millions; meanwhile, the poor, five times more vulnerable to riverine and coastal flooding, risk losing 70 percent of their assets. Limited structural transformation and low labor productivity limit Angola’s prospects for increasing earnings and job quality, thereby reducing its opportunities for sustained and inclusive economic growth. 1 International Monetary Fund, World Economic Outlook Database, April 2024. 2 World Bank Development Indicators, 2024 3 The agriculture sector is the basis of food security for hundreds of communities, many of them indigenous, who live along coasts and river basins (Around 150,000 people earn a living from the sector). Angola created 3.5 million jobs in the last decade, but most new jobs were non-wage jobs in low productivity sectors. More than 72 percent of these jobs were in the non-wage sector (e.g., micro-businesses in the form of self- employment, unpaid family labor, etc.) and most were in unskilled occupations in the agriculture and commerce sectors. Large, modern firms create jobs slowly and employ relatively few people, while microenterprises dominate the private sector, with 55 percent being sole proprietorships. Agriculture remains the primary sector of employment (46.3 percent of employed), followed by the commerce and hospitality sector (22.3 percent). The lucrative extractives sector only employs 0.8 percent of workers. 3. Youth employment is a pressing challenge, driven by a rapidly growing young population that faces multiple constraints and struggles to secure the limited quality jobs5. Youth, aged 15-34, constitute 30 percent of the total population6 and comprise over half of the labor force, with 6 million out of 8.9 million residing in urban areas. Most (76 percent), work in poor quality jobs, predominantly in low-productivity self-employment in urban areas. Youth face higher unemployment rates (22 compared to the national average of 15 percent) and represent 83 percent of the total unemployed in Angola. 4. Angola’s youth lack relevant skills and experience for the labor market. Youth show major deficiencies in technical skills and workplace behavior.7 Soft skills8 pose a hiring constraint,9 and limit youth’s entrepreneurial capacity and management efficiency. A mismatch between the technical and professional training curricula and labor market needs, along with limited courses and spots, exacerbate the skills gap.10 Youth often lack work experience, with over 70 percent of private sector employers in Angola requiring at least three years of experience when hiring new workers. 5. Young women and persons with disabilities face more challenges integrating in the labor market. Young women participate less in the labor force than men (67 vs. 71 percent) and are more likely to work in low-quality jobs (92 vs. 78 percent), and in less profitable sectors.11 Only 26 percent of women hold management positions in private firms.12 Restrictive social norms and traditional gender roles discourage women from pursuing male-dominated fields of study, and occupations.13 In 2014, 2.5 percent of the population had disabilities, mainly due to prolonged civil war.14 Persons with disabilities face barriers such as information gaps, stigma, inaccessible physical environment, low education levels, and ineffective legislation. The Angola government prioritizes youth employment through coordinated efforts by multiple government institutions, as outlined in its key development strategies. The Long-Term Development Strategy (LTDS) 2025, National Development Plan (NDP) (2023-2027), and National Agenda for Employment (2023-2027), engage several government institutions to promote productive employment, job creation, and skills development. The NDP also prioritizes digitization to create new work opportunities and fostering skills development of youth. The Ministry of Public Administration, Labor, and Social Security (Ministério da Administração Publica Trabalho e Segurança Social - MAPTSS) is responsible for overseeing policies related to public administration, labor, and social security. The National Institute of Employment and Professional Training (Instituto Nacional de Emprego e Formação Profissional- INEFOP), mapped to MAPTSS, is responsible for providing and coordinating short-term vocational and non-formal professional training, implementing employment programs, and offering intermediation services. Technical education is led by the Ministry of Education (MED), and tertiary education by the Ministry of Tertiary Education, Science, Technology, and Innovation (MESCTI). The National Institute of Qualifications (INQ), under MAPTSS, coordinates the new National Qualifications System (SNQ). 7. Despite this prioritization, Angola’s policies and institutional capacity limit effective outreach to provide employment support the large youth population. Despite significant spending on active labor market programs (ALMPs) in Angola, restrictive eligibility criteria, limited scale, and overlapping objectives hinder efficient fund use and restrict access for youth.16 Angola also lacks robust information and monitoring and evaluation systems for labor programs, limiting its ability to assess their effectiveness. The NDP prioritizes the Employment Market Observatory, under MAPTSS, to produce and analyze labor market, employment, and training statistics, which is yet to be operationalized. INEFOP implements short-term vocational training, entrepreneurship, internship, and intermediation programs. However, they remain small-scale and lack a comprehensive approach. 8. Creating employment opportunities at scale in the short-term while making longer-term foundational investments for structural transformation is critical for sustainability of Angola’s growth path. In this context, the GoA has requested World Bank support to improve youth’s access to employment opportunities and increase productivity. The proposed project aligns with the government's long-term strategic priorities outlined in the NDP (2023-2027), as well as the Systematic Country Diagnostic (Streamlined SCD, 2024), and the forthcoming Country Partnership Framework (CPF) focusing on economic diversification, by building resilience at the individual, economy, and environment levels. By supporting interventions that enhance youth employment, productivity, income, and work experience, the project will help youth access better opportunities and empower them to become innovators and job creators over time. Strengthening the institutional capacity will also be critical to ensure impact at scale and sustainability. C. D. Project Description 10. The project is structured around three main components: Component 1: Promoting entrepreneurship opportunities for targeted youth (US$110 million); Component 2: Supporting transition of targeted youth to better employment opportunities (US$130 million); and Component 3: Strengthening systems, institutional and implementation capacity, and project management (US$10 million). 11. INEFOP will manage the outreach, and beneficiary selection for all components, through a unified intake process, except for subcomponent 1.2. Outreach will occur through INEFOP-led campaigns with support from contracted firms, collaborating ministries (Ministry of Youth and Sports (MINJUD) and Ministry of Social Action, Family, and Promotion of Women (MASFAMU), and local actors, using a clear communication strategy. Applicants can register online or at INEFOP centers18. The project will promote women's participation by addressing barriers like household responsibilities and social norms, and it will also consider specific needs of persons with disabilities to ensure their inclusion. Simple tools will assess eligibility based on criteria outlined in the Project Operational Manual (POM). The project will promote women's participation by addressing barriers like household responsibilities and social norms, and it will also consider specific needs of persons with disabilities to ensure their inclusion. 12. All beneficiaries (except those in component 1.2) will receive core soft skills training and will be directed to component 1.1 if they demonstrate interest and aptitude for entrepreneurship, or to component 2.1 for wage employment. Soft skills training will build attributes that are transferable across various job types aiming to build job readiness skills like self-awareness, emotional intelligence, communication, leadership, and conflict resolution. A module on gender roles and empowerment will be included, with a preference for female trainers and the incorporation of female role models to inspire participants. The training delivery (location, timing) and content will be tailored as feasible to meet the specific needs and priorities of women. Upon completion of the soft skills training, beneficiaries will be channeled to available employment opportunities or further support (e.g., technical training, internship, job search assistance, etc.) based on their assessed needs and potential identified in the screening process. 18 Vulnerable youth that are already in INEFOP databases will be encouraged to apply. This component aims to improve productivity of young entrepreneurs as innovators and employment generators by addressing key constraints such as limited access to business skills, finance, markets and networks, while making use of innovative digital tools to harness productivity, increase process efficiency and develop digital skills. It comprises the following two subcomponents. 14. Subcomponent 1.1: Improving productivity for self-employed youth. This subcomponent aims to increase productivity in self-employment for targeted youth by providing a support package tailored to the specific needs of subsistence type of entrepreneurs. To this end, this subcomponent will support: (i) entrepreneurship skills training; (iii) business development support; (iv) digital skills and technology adoption; (v) financial support through small grants or professional kits for organized youth groups, cooperatives and social enterprises; (vi) market assessments; and (vii) market access and mentoring. The outreach and communication will be part of the common approach financed under component 2.1. It will target youth who are subsistence entrepreneurs, self-employed in low- productivity sectors and activities —primarily in services—across urban areas and few targeted rural regions. It is expected that this subcomponent will benefit approximately 100,000 beneficiaries. 15. Subcomponent 1.2: Empowering young entrepreneurs with innovation and jobs creation potential. This subcomponent aims to identify and support high-growth potential young entrepreneurs nationwide looking to start or expand their business, creating employment opportunities, through a Business Plan Competition (BPC). This subcomponent will support: (i) outreach and communication campaigns (ii) pre-selection of potential candidates and screening of the initial business ideas; (iii) training and assistance to submit a complete business plan proposal; (iv) selection of winning business plans; (v) coaching and business development support; (vi) financial support through grants. Beneficiaries of this subcomponent may have relatively higher levels of education19 but no collateral nor assets or experience to access commercial financing. They might be innovative start-ups in need of early-stage financing. It is expected that this subcomponent will benefit approximately 5,000 beneficiaries. 16. Component 2: Supporting transition of targeted youth to better employment opportunities (US$130 million). This component aims to support targeted youth to improve access to employment opportunities, including in digital jobs, by simultaneously addressing the many barriers faced by youth, including limited/no work experience; lack of relevant technical and soft skills; skills employment mismatch etc. It also supports expansion and modernization of the professional training services network to facilitate expanded access to and delivery of interventions. It includes the following two subcomponents. 17. Subcomponent 2.1: Modernizing apprenticeships and enhancing employability. This subcomponent aims to provide targeted youth opportunities to obtain work experience and job-relevant skills, utilizing a comprehensive approach with a differentiated set of interventions, which will support youth along their path to employment. To this end, this subcomponent will support: (i) the design and implementation of outreach and communication campaigns; (ii) provision of core soft skills training; (iii) design of screening tools to refer youth to appropriate interventions; (iv) provision of job-relevant skills training (i.e. technical and/or digital; (v) certification of previously acquired skills/learning; (vi) on the job training in formal and informal sector; (vii) mentoring, job search assistance, and labor intermediation; and (viii) stipends for young women and people with disabilities to support their attendance at technical and digital training.20 The target group is youth unemployed or inactive without the work experience and 19It may include the TEST and PAT II beneficiaries. 20This subcomponent aims to strengthen and scale up existing INEFOP services, such as short-term technical training, internships, and job intermediation, integrating them into a cohesive package to maximize impact for youth while minimizing complexity. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 21. The project will generate sustainable results by utilizing a systems approach and operating at scale. Building on its solid technical capacity, INEFOP21 will serve as the project implementation agency, responsible for the Program's overall governance, technical execution, and fiduciary management. INEFOP is supervised by MAPTSS and is institutionally responsible for coordinating and implementing the employment and vocational training strategy. With a nationwide physical footprint, it provides short-term vocational (professional) training, implements employment program including entrepreneurship initiatives and internships, and offers intermediation services INEFOP manages and accredits over 1,600 training centers and operates 74 employment centers,22 with a track record of technical capacity. The project design facilitates implementation readiness by leveraging existing interventions and capacities, scaling them up and integrating international best practices. Building on this capacity, INEFOP will technically and administratively lead project implementation. It will be responsible for (i) planning and coordinating the implementation of all project activities; (ii) financial management and procurement for all project activities; (iii) addressing environmental and social aspects; (iv) recruiting and managing human resources for the project; (v) preparing annual work programs and budgets (AWPBs); and (vi) establishing a functional project monitoring and evaluation (M&E) system, ensuring timely preparation of progress reports, and disseminating project results. 22. The Project implementation will be centrally coordinated, with INEFOP supported by a technical team to enhance management capacity and ensure compliance with World Bank procedures. The overall Project will be housed at central level of INEFOP, with the Director General of INEFOP serving as Project Coordinator. Relevant central INEFOP departments (e.g., Professional Training; Employment Services Management; Administration and General Services, etc.) will coordinate project activities in their respective areas. Provincial services will oversee and implement activities at the local level. The technical support team will be hired or appointed with terms of reference reviewed and approved by the World Bank. The team will include fiduciary staff and environmental and social project specialists to ensure compliance with World Bank procedures. The POM will include a detailed description of their roles. Additional experts may be contracted on a short-term basis for specific technical issues, as needed. 23.
