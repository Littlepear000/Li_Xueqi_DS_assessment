Page ix The World Bank FSM and RMI: Implementation Capacity for Transformative Results Project (P506422) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. The Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) and the Federated States of Micronesia (FSM) are defined by their extreme remoteness, small size, and geographic dispersion. The RMI consists of 29 coral atolls and five isolated islands in the Western and Central Pacific Ocean (WCPO), with a total land area of 181 square kilometers (km²) and an Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) of just under 2 million km², making it the 19th largest EEZ in the world. The FSM is an even more widely dispersed, and overall larger, archipelagic nation in the WCPO, comprising over 600 islands stretching 2,700 km² from west to east, and organized into four semiautonomous states: Chuuk, Kosrae, Pohnpei, and Yap. The FSM’s total land area is 702 km² and its EEZ is the 14th largest in the world, covering approximately 3 million km², including some of the most productive tuna fishing grounds in the WCPO. The geographic dispersion and remoteness contribute to the high costs of public administration and an uneven availability of quality public services. 2. Both countries are classified as fragile due to limited state capacity and exposure to natural disasters and climate change. Fragility in the RMI and FSM arises from their extreme geographic dispersion and limited economic viability, thin institutional capacity, unplanned urbanization, youth unemployment, poor land governance, and gender- based violence. There is also high exposure to frequent and damaging natural hazards, with both countries having low capacity to manage the ensuing risks. Their vulnerability is exacerbated by the lack of technical capacity for adequate disaster preparedness and mitigation, and by climate change, which is predicted to amplify the magnitude of cyclones, droughts, and flooding. Addressing these challenges is essential for building resilience and supporting sustainable development in both countries. 3. Negative net migration has driven a decline in the population of both countries in the past decade, resulting in acute shortages of skilled labor. Between 2012 and 2022, FSM’s population (currently 115,224) declined by about 10 percent, with outbound migration playing a central role.1 RMI’s population of 41,996 has declined from 51,352 in 2013 and is concentrated mainly in the capital, Majuro, and the island of Ebeye. Citizens of FSM and RMI have visa-free access to live, work, and study in the United States under the Compacts of Free Association (COFA), easing emigration. The resulting shortage of skilled labor creates dependencies on foreign experts and undermines domestic revenue generation and economic growth. 4. The countries’ labor markets are characterized by gender disparities, high levels of informality, and reliance on public sector employment. Women are deeply underrepresented in paid employment and are more likely to be employed below their education levels and earn less than men. FSM youth (aged 15–24) have high Not in Education, Employment, or Training (NEET) and youth unemployment rates, while gender disparities are even stronger within this age group than the national averages.2 In RMI, public sector jobs have grown steadily, with the increase in sovereign rents since 2014, and now account for approximately 60 percent of jobs, while private sector job growth has been anemic. 5. RMI and FSM face significant economic challenges characterized by low growth rates, external vulnerabilities, and dependence on foreign aid. FSM is a lower middle-income country with a gross national income (GNI) of US$4,250 per capita in 2023, and RMI is an upper middle-income country with a GNI of US$7,860 per capita in 2023. RMI and FSM are focused on building human capital and strengthening governance. RMI’s National Strategic Plan 2020–2030 and FSM’s Strategic Development Plan 2025–2045 recognize that human capital is essential for ensuring sustainability of economic growth. RMI’s key policy objectives include: (i) enhanced skills and attributes necessary for a dynamic workforce and resilient economy; and (ii) enhanced capacity of public servants and employees of public agencies, including those in the outer islands. FSM’s desired outcomes include: (i) building a strong and diverse private sector workforce, and (ii) developing an efficient and effective public service workforce. Both countries are committed to strengthening transparency, efficiency, and institutional performance as a means to improve overall governance. 7. The activities under this project are consistent with the Strengthening of the Operating Model component of the World Bank Group (WBG) Evolution Roadmap, which aims to strengthen client support and capacity. The Project is providing support to RMI and FSM to build institutional and human capacity to achieve improved project portfolio quality, stronger results, and greater impact. The activities will seek to: (i) strengthen and improve the efficiency and capacity of key ministries and agencies to prepare and implement projects; and (ii) support the enhancement of the technical skills of public sector workers and consultants responsible for World Bank (WB)-funded operations and to attract, train, and retain local talent. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. RMI and FSM score below 3.0 on the Country Policy and Institutional Assessment (CPIA) and are consistently ranked below other Pacific Island Countries (PICs) (Table 1). They also score below 3.0 for indicators measuring the quality of public sector management and institutions. The countries’ scores are particularly low for dimensions related to the preparation and implementation of projects, namely: (i) Quality of Public Administration, which measures the extent to which the structure of civilian central government staff supports the effective design and implementation of government policy and delivery of services; and (ii) Quality of Budgetary and Financial Management, which measures, in part, the extent to which there are effective financial management systems in place and timely and accurate accounting and fiscal reporting, including timely and audited public accounts. COFA agreements approved by the United States on March 9, 2024, will deliver a total of US$5.6 billion in assistance to the two countries over a 20- year period starting in FY24. FSM will receive US$3.3 billion and RMI US$2.3 billion. The WB portfolio has grown to 11 projects in RMI, with a total commitment of US$298 million, and 10 projects in FSM, with a total commitment of US$344.45 million. In addition, other development partners also provide technical and financial resources. This increases the governments’ responsibility to use these resources efficiently and effectively through improved preparation and implementation of development projects. To achieve results, RMI and FSM must develop local human capacity to manage projects. In addition, strengthening institutional capacity and monitoring systems will lay a strong foundation for managing projects and support broader efforts to enhance governance. 10. There is a shortage of skilled workers to support development projects. There is a small pool of skilled workers available in the labor market, given the high migration and low level of education outcomes. Limited technical expertise and a high turnover of employees in public institutions are among the most frequent problems encountered across both countries. Capacity building efforts are often undermined as staff with newly acquired skills and qualifications move to new roles in or outside the public service, including with other development partners. The response is to hire international consultants, but this leads to high transaction and overhead costs (given the remoteness and fragility of the countries), and challenges in supervising consultants’ performance. Remote islands have more challenges hiring international consultants, and many of whom do not complete their full contract term. Experience has shown that the reliance on international expertise has not built national capacity, which is required for sustainable implementation of development projects. 11. The governments have begun to address capacity constraints. The governments of RMI and FSM are acutely aware that the lack of capacity is limiting RMI’s and FSM’s growth. They have established scholarship programs for local and overseas tertiary education. The Marshall Islands Scholarship Grant and Loan Board Program and FSM National Scholarship and Financial Assistance Program raise and administer financial assistance for qualified applicants. There are also scholarships offered by bilateral partners including: Australia, China, Japan, and New Zealand. Most scholarship recipients are required to return to their country of citizenship, after completion of their studies, to contribute to national development. There is no systematic linkage of scholarships and training programs with each country’s strategic workforce needs, and permanent jobs in the recipients’ areas of studies are very limited.3 Therefore, most participants migrate to the United States for better opportunities after they complete their required work obligations. 12. The WB has a track record of capacity development activities to improve operational efficiency. The IDA18 scale- up and the compressed IDA19 period led to a faster-than-expected expansion of the WB-financed portfolio, especially in RMI, both in the number of projects and value of commitments. Given the unique context and capacity challenges faced by RMI and FSM, in 2019 the WB financed Central Implementation Units (CIUs) in the Ministry of Finance, Banking, and Postal Services (MoFBPS) of RMI, and the Department of Finance and Administration (DOFA) in FSM. 4 CIUs were also implemented in other capacity challenged countries in the Pacific (the Republic of Kiribati, Independent State of Samoa, Kingdom of Tonga, and Tuvalu) and in the Caribbean (Dominica and Sint Maarten). The CIUs respond to the need for a differentiated approach for faster and transformative impact in small island states. Since 2023, Procurement Hands-on Expanded Implementation Support (HEIS) has been undertaken, resulting in a more than 60 percent reduction in procurement delays across RMI and FSM, which increased the number and value of contracts processed by 129 and 132 percent, respectively. Shared services units offer a meaningful way to improve government capacity.5 The CIUs provide support and capacity building for pipeline delivery and the implementation of projects in the RMI and FSM portfolios. The objectives are to: (i) to strengthen institutional capacity, build systems, and develop policies and processes; (ii) develop a cadre of local staff to operate these systems over the longer term; and (iii) enhance quality and timeliness in the preparation and implementation of development financed operations, initially focused on WB-financed projects. CIUs comprise local and international staff, under individual consultant contracts. These staff provide support in common function areas including procurement, financial management (FM), environmental and social (E&S) aspects, and monitoring and evaluation (M&E) – coordinated by a Project/Program Manager. Project Implementation Units (PIUs) typically have a technical Project Manager, a Project Officer, administrative staff, and technical consultants. 14. The recent Joint Country Portfolio Performance Reviews (CPPRs) for RMI (December 5, 2024) and FSM (December 12, 2024) highlighted the impacts of the CIUs – and the challenges they face. Between 2022 and 2024, the RMI CIU, with the majority of local staff, doubled the disbursement rate (from 7 to 14 percent). The FSM CIU, with a majority of international staff, had a marginal increase in the disbursement rate over the same period (6 percent to 7 percent). The governments are committed to key actions to improve portfolio quality and results. However, there are challenges that undermine the CIUs’ effectiveness, including: (i) lack of clarity about the mandate of the CIU, which encompasses both implementation support and capacity building; (ii) inefficient policies, procedures, and systems (for example, the absence of standard operating procedures (SOP)); (iii) a lack of clear performance metrics; and (iv) lack of incentives for capacity building (for example, performance and compensation are not linked to capacity building outcomes). During the CPPR exercise, the governments of RMI and FSM requested support from the WB to address these challenges. 15. The relationship between CIUs and PIUs requires streamlining to improve operational efficiency. Currently, PIUs are responsible for project implementation, including the preparation of FM and procurement documentation and work plans and budgets. CIUs provide final approvals for FM functions, oversight, and advice to PIUs in common function areas and have a mandate to provide capacity building, though not formally implemented. PIUs typically do not have specialist consultants in common function areas such as E&S Officers, therefore relying on the CIU to support these functions. However, there is a lack of clarity in the roles and responsibilities of PIUs, CIUs, and implementing agencies, such as an overlapping division of responsibilities (for example, the Project Officer and CIU doing the same function). At times, the division of responsibility between the finance and implementing agencies (ministries) with respect to the CIUs has been unclear, as has the line between the strategic role required of the government and the hands-on implementation required of the CIUs. 16. The project will focus on transitioning the CIUs to more effective successor arrangements, with sustainable local capacity to drive results. The CIU successor arrangements (CSAs) will resolve the existing ambiguities in institutional mandates and formalize increased capacity building support to WB portfolio PIUs. The institutional review to be conducted under the project will guide the structure and arrangements of the CSA and their role in project implementation. The consolidation of CIU/CSA operational support and capacity building activities under this stand-alone Investment Project Financing (IPF), with a designated results framework, is expected to increase accountability for the CIU/CSA performance. The project is expected to deliver several key benefits, primarily through enhanced efficiency of project management resources. Both the Federated States of Micronesia (FSM) and the Republic of the Marshall Islands (RMI) have laid the foundation for advancing gender equality through national strategies, yet women continue to face pronounced gaps in economic participation, technical roles, and leadership positions. 59. In FSM, the National Gender Policy (2018) identifies five priorities: improving women’s representation in decision- making, eliminating gender-based violence, ensuring equitable education, reducing barriers in the workforce, and improving health and reproductive rights. However, labor force data shows persistent disparities: while 67.8% of men participate in the workforce, only 46.2% of women do. Women’s unemployment rate (13.9%) is more than double that of men (5.4%). Although women make up 39% of formal employment, they remain concentrated in lower-level roles, holding just 13% of leadership positions (UN Women, 2022) Access to skilled and technical jobs remains especially limited. A gender stocktake found that women in FSM are significantly underrepresented in professional and technical occupations, in part due to traditional gender roles and limited access to relevant training pathways (Pacific Community, 2021). 60. In RMI, the Gender Mainstreaming Policy (2015) and Gender Inequality Act (2019) provide a policy platform for women’s empowerment, yet implementation challenges persist. Women’s labor force participation is just 27.9%, compared to 60% for men. While more women are entering public service, men continue to dominate senior and technical roles. A 2021 review of 13 organizations found that only 33% of senior management roles were held by women, and participation in technical fields remains low (SPC, 2015) Regionally, the gender gap in technical sectors is stark. A recent review of 14 Pacific energy utilities found that only 18% of employees were women, and just 10% of engineers were female—highlighting the systemic barriers women face in entering and advancing in technical professions (World Bank, 2024). 61. The project is strategically designed to reduce gender disparities by expanding women’s participation in technical and leadership roles. It will implement gender-responsive human resource frameworks that institutionalize equitable recruitment, retention, and promotion practices within project agencies. Women will be specifically targeted through inclusive training, certification, and internship programs, with a goal of achieving at least 50% female participation in technical areas such as procurement, financial management, environmental and social safeguards, and project delivery. In parallel, mentorship and coaching initiatives will support women’s career progression into decision-making roles, Page 15 The World Bank FSM and RMI: Implementation Capacity for Transformative Results Project (P506422) addressing both individual and institutional barriers. As a result, the project is expected to increase the number of women with certified technical qualifications and expand their representation in national employment. By coupling individual capacity-building with systemic reform, the project will help institutionalize inclusive practices and advance gender equality in alignment with national priorities in FSM and RMI.
