Introduction and Context Project Strategic Context and Rationale for Regional Approach Caribbean countries face significant challenges in building sustainable energy sectors due to their heavy reliance on imported fossil fuels. This dependence leads to high energy costs, averaging 6% of regional GDP, and some of the highest electricity tariffs in the world, reaching up to US$0.458 per kWh. The development of renewable energy in the Caribbean has been slow due to uncertain policies, small project scales, underprepared grids, climate risks, and weak implementation capacity. As a result, the actual share of renewable energy in electricity production was only 11% in 2019. The Caribbean Resilient Renewable Energy Infrastructure Investment Facility (RREIIF) Project aims to accelerate renewable energy development through a regional approach. This includes pooling project developers, creating a risk mitigation mechanism, and supporting grid infrastructure modernization Regional Sectoral and Institutional Context Caribbean countries, particularly those in the Eastern Caribbean Currency Union (ECCU), have abundant renewable energy (RE) resources and ambitious plans for development. The region receives about 200 days of sun annually, and there is significant potential for offshore wind and geothermal energy. The current installed electricity generating capacity from RE in the Eastern Caribbean is 38 MW, with plans to install 227 MW of RE capacity by 2030, requiring an estimated investment of EC$2.2 billion (approximately US$807 million) with the private sector taking the lead. Despite sufficient capital and liquidity in Caribbean banks to finance RE development projects, several barriers must be addressed. These include the small size of individual RE projects, lack of local supply chains, vulnerability to hurricanes, and limited administrative capacity for handling regulatory aspects. The Caribbean Resilient Renewable Energy Infrastructure Investment Facility (RREIIF) aims to overcome these barriers by addressing project size, preparation, risk- mitigation instruments, and implementation capacity. Additionally, countries are advancing on policy and regulatory reforms. Key regulatory frameworks are being developed or are already in place to facilitate private RE investment in participating countries. For example, Grenada enacted the Electricity Supply Act and the Public Utilities Regulatory Commission (PURC) Act, with the PURC becoming operational in July 2019. Saint Lucia amended its Electricity Act in 2016 to open opportunities for independent power producers (IPP) and establish a regulatory body, the National Utilities Regulatory Commission (NURC). Saint Vincent and the Grenadines is updating its National Energy Policy and Electricity Supply Act, with the regulatory and policy update process requiring stakeholder engagement and strong planning. Project Description The RREIIF project aims to address regional market, administrative, and technical barriers to the expansion of resilient, utility-scale RE by private developers in the Eastern Caribbean. The project will establish a Regional Coordination Unit to aggregate small RE projects, create a clear regulatory framework for private power generation, and implement risk- mitigation mechanisms to incentivize favorable lending terms by private banks. Additionally, the project will provide technical assistance and training for project preparation and negotiation, as well as financing for the modernization and upgrading of power grids for RE integration, including battery energy storage systems (BESS). The long-term goal of the project is to increase energy security at lower costs, enhance economic competitiveness, and create resilient energy infrastructure. The initial phase involves three countries, Grenada, Saint Lucia, and Saint Vincent and the Grenadines, and is designed to be expandable with more countries joining and additional investments being added to the project pipeline. The successful implementation of the first phase is expected to encourage more countries to participate, leading to more projects and advancing towards the long-term outcome. Overall, the Project is expected to have positive environmental and social impacts. Limited client capacity, creation of a risk mitigation facility requiring E&S risk management through an Environmental and Social Management System (ESMS), challenges in coordinating regional and national activities by a newly set up Regional Coordinating Unit; and implementation of multiple sub-projects at the national levels contributes to the Substantial risk rating. Though the location and scale of all the investments under both components is not known, site specific impacts such as those related to pollution, waste, fire and life safety, community as well as occupational health and safety are likely and would be short-term, localized and reversible that could be addressed through standard mitigation measures and good international industry practice (GIIP) delineated in the WB Group General Environmental, Health and Safety (EHS) Guidelines. The Project’s key social risks relate to land acquisition and resettlement (under Components 3 and possibly 2), labor and working conditions, OHS (e.g., safety hazards for workers such as risks of accidents due to working at heights, life and fire safety risks), community health and safety, and mitigating the risk of forced labor in the global supply chain for solar panels and solar components. Given the size of the installation and maintenance service contracts expected under the project, contracted firms are expected to hire local labor, and thus the risks related to labor influx are expected to be limited. In terms of OHS risks, while St. Lucia, Grenada and SVG have OHS legal frameworks, potential issues may arise during implementation due to weak enforcement. The risks of sexual exploitation and abuse and sexual harassment have been assessed as low. To mitigate this risk of forced labor, the Bank requires the borrowers to strengthen solar related procurement processes by including forced labor bidder declarations, qualification requirements and strengthened contractual provision in procurements involving financing of solar panels/solar components. It is expected that the majority of subprojects are expected to be implemented within the existing footprints of public investments or on public lands, minimizing the need for land acquisition impacts. A Resettlement Framework (RF) will be prepared as a precautionary measure in case land acquisition and/or involuntary physical/economic displacement is required in one or more subproject sites. Resettlement Plans, if needed, will be prepared and implemented prior to carrying out the relevant works. For private sector investments, the private developer is expected to buy land, if needed, through a willing buyer-willing seller open market transaction. Finally, insufficient engagement and coordination with different project stakeholders and poor-quality stakeholder consultations or the lack of involvement of local communities in decision-making may lead to public resentment. This risk will be mitigated by stakeholder engagement activities specified in the Stakeholder Engagement Plans. Nine Environmental and Social Standards are currently relevant (ESS1-6, ESS8-10). To manage the environmental and social risks, draft country-specific Stakeholder Engagement Plans (SEPs) and a draft Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF) including the Labor Management Procedures (LMP) have been prepared and disclosed by the clients; A draft Resettlement Framework will be prepared for the Project within 90 days of the Project Effective Date. The apex FI will also develop an ESMS within 240 days of the Project Effective Date. In addition, client specific Environmental and Social Commitment Plans (ESCPs) have been prepared and disclosed by the World Bank and the clients prior to Appraisal and will be finalized during Negotiations. Implementation Page 5 The World Bank Caribbean Resilient Renewable Energy Infrastructure Investment Facility (P180831) Institutional and Implementation Arrangements The Resilient Renewable Energy Infrastructure Investment Facility (RREIIF) will be anchored within the Eastern Caribbean Partial Credit Guarantee Corporation (ECPCGC), with a Regional Steering Committee (RSC) overseeing project implementation. The Regional Renewable Energy Coordinating Unit (RCU), hosted within ECPCGC, will coordinate overall project activities, support national implementation units, and serve as a transaction advisor responsible for project development, aggregation, and tender processes across participating countries. Each participating country—Grenada, Saint Lucia, and St. Vincent and the Grenadines—will establish or enhance national Project Implementation Units (PIUs) under their respective energy ministries. These national PIUs will have fiduciary responsibilities for specific project components, with dedicated staff including project coordinators, environmental and social specialists, and procurement experts. Project Steering Committees will be created in each country to include representatives from various ministries, financial departments, and regulatory commissions, ensuring coordinated oversight and strategic alignment of renewable energy development efforts.
