Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Bosnia and Herzegovina (BiH) has enjoyed macroeconomic stability over the last decade. Real GDP growth was driven by a surge in exports, and robust growth in private consumption. However, its economy is heavily reliant on sources of growth that are not sustainable in the long-term—most notably high levels of consumption, exacerbated by low investment levels and export volumes, especially compared to BiH’s Western Balkan neighbors. Furthermore, the country’s economic growth over the last decade failed to generate enough jobs to improve employment outcomes for much of the population, resulting in still-high unemployment around 16 percent (Labor Force Survey, 2019) and steady emigration rate, most prevalent among the youth. Between 2011 and 2019, BiH lost approximately 20,000 jobs, spread across many sectors. The largest losses were in agriculture, public administration, wholesale and retail, transport and health services, disproportionately in the public sector.1 Of note for this proposed project, coal mining employment contracted by over 13 percent in this period. 2. The labor market improved, but the unemployment rate remains elevated, contributing to already significant levels of poverty, especially in rural areas. The unemployment rate increased by 1.5 percent to 18 percent in 2021 compared to the year before. Job gains in agriculture and construction drove up the employment rate to 40.4 percent in 2021, which nevertheless remains at low levels compared to other Western Balkan countries. In parallel to the tightening of the labor market, net nominal salaries rose about 4.3 percent in 2021, however the swift rise in consumer prices in the fourth quarter of 2021 has eaten into households’ real incomes. Moreover, according to the Household Budget Survey (HBS)2 poverty rose slightly from 15 percent to 16 percent between 2011 and 2015. The poor are disproportionately concentrated in rural areas, where poverty rates are much higher than in urban areas (19 percent and 12 percent, respectively), and where jobs tend to be scarce, informal, and salaries are low. Women and younger workers face significant challenges accessing economic opportunities and are more likely to remain outside the labor force or migrate for work. 3. BiH has taken various steps to address climate change imperatives. Energy production in BiH accounts for over 60 percent of greenhouse gas emissions, equivalent of 7.1 metric tons of CO2 emissions per capita. Coal dependence of the country is such that in 2020 a total of 67.9 percent of electricity in BiH was produced by coal- fired thermal power plants. Hence, the phasing down of coal-based energy production is the first, and, possibly, the most important step towards achieving BiH’s decarbonization targets, and is therefore the subject of this proposed operation. Bosnia and Herzegovina (BiH) is a country of about 3.5 million people in the Western Balkans. BiH comprises of two entities: the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina (FBiH) and Republika Srpska (RS). The District of Br�?ko was added as self-governing administrative unit in 2000. The FBiH is subdivided into 10 cantons, each with its own executive, legislative, and judicial branches of government. The cantons are further subdivided into municipalities. The RS is subdivided into 64 municipalities. Each entity has a high degree of autonomy. The authorities in BiH are, however, pursuing a joint development strategy that centers on macroeconomic stability and export-led growth, employment and social cohesion, and sustainable development. The strategy’s overarching goal is the accession to the European Union (EU) with BiH already being a candidate for EU membership. Sectoral and Institutional Context Overall Energy Context 5. At present, approximately two-thirds of the power generated in BiH comes from coal with the remaining one- third from hydropower. Though BiH’s power sector has been unbundled over the past few years, certain operations remaining integrated within state-owned utilities: large hydropower generation, coal mining and coal- based power generation, including district heat supply in several urban centers. In fact, except for small hydropower plants, one privately owned coal-fired plant Stanari, and some few emerging wind and photovoltaic projects, the power sector of BiH is mainly dependent on the entity-owned utilities: EPBiH and EPHZHB in FBiH and ERS in RS. As of 2021, BiH has an installed capacity of around 4,610 Megawatt (MW), with 2,065 MW coal (45 percent), 2,077 MW large hydropower (45 percent), 180 MW of small hydro (4 percent), 135 MW wind (3 percent) and 57 MW solar (1 percent); industrial power plants add 93 MW to the system (2 percent). Over the period 2015- 2020, annual electricity demand in BiH averaged 12,637 GWh. Peak demand registered in 2021 was 1,909 MW. Historically, BiH has been a net exporter of electricity. The average net exports between 2015 and 2020 were 3,562 GWh, close to the net exports during 2020 (4,061 GWh). 6. Overview of Coal Mines and Thermal Power Plants (TPP) in BiH 7. BiH has 11 active coal mines, eight of which are located in FBiH3 and three in RS4 (Figure 3). In recent years, BiH produced approximately 14 million metric tons of coal per year, split between FBiH and RS, and almost exclusively used to fire the country’s coal power plant fleet. Domestic production and generation ensures reliable supply of base power and has contributed significantly to BiH’s regional power export position. In FBiH, coal mines are largely operated by the state-owned power utility Elektroprivreda Bosne i Hercegovine (EPBiH), with the exception of Banovići mine which is owned and operated by RMU Banovići, with 69.3 percent of its shares state-owned and a group of shareholders controlling the rest. EPBiH accounts for an annual production of 5.2 million metric tons, while the Banovići coal mine has an annual production of 1.9 million metric tons. In RS, two mines are operated by the state-owned power utility Elektroprivreda Republike Srpske (ERS), accounting for 4.7 million metric tons of coal production annually. The remaining RS mine, Stanari, is owned and operated by the UK-based energy trading and investment firm, Energy Financing Team (EFT) Group, with an annual production of 2.5 million metric tons. Overall, the RS’s three coal mines produce similar amount of coal as eight mines in FBiH; an indicator of a broader geographical distribution of coal fields in FBiH, making the Just Transition process somewhat more complex in this entity of BiH. 8. BiH’s mine-to-power sector is vertically integrated. Most domestic coal production is used to power the country’s five thermal power plants (TPP). Tuzla-5 is expected to be retired in 2025 when Tuzla-7 (a new coal unit) was expected to commence operation. Labor Trends in the Coal Sector 10. Most coal sector jobs are in the public sector, pay high wages, and provide good non-wage benefits. This means that most coal sector workers—and especially for lower-skilled coal workers—would likely to struggle to find comparable compensation working in other sectors. In addition to “direct�? coal sector workers, thousands of workers are employed upstream in the coal value chain in firms dependent on mines and TPPs, who are also at risk of losing their jobs in the event of mine and power plant closures. 11. Although the coal sector is not a major employer in the broader BiH economy, it is a significant player at the local level, especially around Gacko, Banovici, Ugljevik, Breza and Kakanj. These municipalities are vulnerable to economic fall-out from mine closure, not only due to their dependence on the coal sector, but also because of already high unemployment. In a scenario in which all coal-related jobs are eventually lost, the influx of displaced coal-sector workers into local labor markets would massively increase the pool of registered unemployed and likely exacerbate existing structural skills mismatch. Coal sector workers may find it difficult to access jobs that are aligned with their skills or sector-related specializations, given that occupations in highest demand in the current labor market are for low-skilled workers. Social protection system in place in each entity will not be sufficient to cushion the socio-economic consequences in communities of any coal phase down. Former Mining Lands and Their Potential for Repurposing and Future Investment 12. BiH has abundant natural resources, including land. Conservative estimates indicate that at least 42 km2 of lands are currently occupied by coal mining activities, while recent empirical evidence suggests five to ten times this amount. As such, between 200-400 km2 of post-mining lands could likely be made available for reclamation and future redevelopment and repurposing. The demand for lands that already have clear ownership, flexibility regarding spatial zoning designation and are not under environmental protection status, is expected to rise significantly over the coming years. This demand will be driven by the spatial requirements for renewable energy (RE) with investments such as photovoltaics, energy crops, energy and storage facilities and, on the other hand, by demand for quality lands for high-value economic investments. If the economic activity currently provided by the coal mining sector is to be replaced with a diversified, sustainable, modern economic fabric, former coal mining lands will play a critical enabling role. 13. The ability to provide abundant lands for a variety of productive purposes could have a significant impact on the pace and depth of economic diversification. Relationship to CPF 14. Country Partnership Framework. The Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for BiH for the period (FY23-FY27) recognizes that BiH’s high emissions from its coal-dominated energy sector leaves it vulnerable to external shocks. Coal dependence is such that in 2020 a total of 67.9 percent of electricity in BiH was produced by thermal power plants. Hence, to maintain its energy security and achieve its 2050 decarbonization goal, BiH will need to expand lower carbon energy alternatives including utility-scale hydropower, a major shift to solar and wind, and prioritize the support for a “just transition�? in coal regions. Meanwhile, the CPF stresses that the energy transition in coal regions will impact not only workers directly engaged in mining operations and along the coal supply chain, but also workers with indirect linkages to coal activities, such as retail, restaurants, and recreation service providers to coal miners and their families. The proposed project, with a focus on Just Transition in BiH coal regions, will directly contribute to High Level Objective 1 (increased inclusive private sector employment), High Level Objective 3 (improved environmental outcomes and climate change resilience), and the cross-cutting theme of closing gender gaps. 15. Gender. Although women comprise only a small proportion of the formal mining workforce, they are present in large numbers in the informal, artisanal, and small-scale mining sectors. Irrespective of the size or nature of the coal mining operation, women generally are poorly paid and typically engaged as low-level staff, casual, informal or daily-wage labor within the coal sector. Gender roles and relations in coal mining communities are at root of gender disparity. The important role of informal labor in coal-based economies further exaggerates the challenge to achieve gender equality during the transition. Rather than perpetuating existing gender inequalities, a transition away from coal needs to be seen as an opportunity to transform current gender relations, creating more gender equal conditions. Through this project, women that are formally employed by the mining sector will benefit from the labor transition programs, while women in the coal communities will benefit from the awareness campaigns as well as the creation of new employment opportunities that will be piloted. 16. Concept Description 17. In 2019 the World Bank launched a global initiative entitled Support to a Just Transition in Coal Regions (P171194), with the objective of supporting client countries to plan for a Just Transition of their coal sectors. An entry point of support is the development of a roadmap which serves as the basis for detailed Just Transition programming in a given country. The roadmap is organized around three pillars of intervention: (i) governing systems; (ii) people and communities; and (iii) repurposing of mining lands and other assets. The three pillars are then set against three phases of transition: (i) pre-closure planning; (ii) closure; and (iii) regional transition. These pillars underpin the World Bank’s approach to providing programmatic assistance aimed at easing the transition to a new energy economy—the World Bank “Just Transition�? in Coal Regions Framework (3x3 Matrix) (see Figure 1). Pillar 1: Institutional Governance. Responsibility for planning, preparing, and implementing transition rests with national and regional governments. Clearly defined policy and government participation is indispensable, but actions taken by the government should not be unilaterally determined. The World Bank has found that successfully planning for regional transition hinges on input from a broad range of diverse stakeholders, often concentrated at the local / regional level where they otherwise are disadvantaged in terms of voice and influence in decision-making processes. National, subnational, regional, and local leadership play important and varying roles in the evolving energy economy and should lead a Just Transition which satisfies local needs and respects the community’s vision of the future. In this way, governments manage transition by developing policy tailored to fit regional requirements and by providing needed support through a robust and flexible framework for economic assistance. Agile government actions that are highly coordinated and underpinned by clarity on various roles and responsibilities for each agency, ensure that resources are applied when and where they are needed for community redevelopment so that communities are enabled to drive the change and build the future. 19. Pillar 2: People and Communities. People and the mining communities in which they live and work are at the epicenter of any transition. Their well-being is paramount as the impacts of mine closure on employment and health are most intensely felt in communities surrounding mines. Beyond job losses, the social impacts of mine closures stemming from disrupted livelihoods can lead to intrahousehold stress, substance abuse, gender-based violence, and social tension, among others. The extent to which affected communities recover is the metric by which successful transition is measured. While physical closure of a coal mine may be the end of active mining in a community, it marks the abrupt and often difficult beginning of social and labor transition. It is critical to have a clear understanding of the poverty and social impacts of the mine closure, and to engage communities in the preparation, management, and repurposing of closed facilities. Pillar 3: Repurposing Mining Lands and Thermal Power Plants and Other and Assets. Coal extraction and processing at a mine and associated facilities may have occurred over many years or even decades, so it is not surprising that closing a mine, reclaiming the land, and protecting the remaining assets and natural resources for beneficial future use is a complicated and often a lengthy and expensive process. Corporate governance plays an important role in determining the early outcomes of closure and mined land repurposing. Managers of mining corporations confront and manage the early social and economic impacts of mine closure, but mine management, who may have the best firsthand knowledge of the closing mine and its impact on the natural resources and surrounding communities, will withdraw once the mine is closed and all titles and permits to operate are relinquished. 21. Under the overarching guidance of the World Bank “Just Transition�? in Coal Regions Framework, the design of this proposed operation is the result of recently completed World Bank Advisory Services and Analytics (ASA) — which proposed a roadmap for Just Transition in BiH coal regions to BiH authorities (P171194)—and dialogue with the Government of BiH. Additionally, the proposed operation is also informed by on-going ASAs: (i) the work of the Least Cost Planning (LCP) (P169389), and (ii) the Country Economic Memorandum (P178645). Each component of the operation reflects an agreed design with the beneficiaries (Banovici mine, EPBiH and ERS) and builds on prior work and findings from the Just Transitions ASA. 22. The ambition of BiH authorities and their utilities and mines to pilot approaches to a Just Transition in their coal regions currently outstrips IBRD resources available. At present, IBRD financing can comfortably accommodate preliminary pilots in Banovici, Zenica and Gacko. All three sites were pre-identified and chosen in close coordination with the state and entity level authorities and the respective utilities in June 2022. However, RS has recently expressed interest in having a second pilot area included in this operation, Ugljevik. The project design will include six technical components, four of which will be location and mine site-based, allowing the project to operate more efficiently and effectively within BiH’s complex government and institutional structure (see Para. 4 above for more details). Subcomponents of the four location and mine site-based components are guided by the World Bank 3x3 matrix described above, and have been agreed upon by BiH authorities and the respective utilities in November 2022 after multiple World Bank technical mission. Activities proposed for each mine site will focus on providing support on (i) preparing mining lands for future use and investment, (ii) developing renewable power generation; and iii) preparing for a labor transition. A fifth technical component is proposed transversal in nature, as it aims to address several policy areas requiring attention to ensure sustainability of BiH’s “just transition�? efforts in the longer term. The last technical component will support project management and coordination at the state level and the entity level, by financing the core project implementation teams and operating costs that are essential for effective project implementation. The environmental and social risks associated with project are rated substantial. There is a limited Borrower's capacity in addressing and managing the Environmental and Social (ES) issues in accordance with Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) requirements. While the project will not finance actual mine closure, it will support pre-closure planning. The project will support remediation and land repurposing of surface coal mines, using the Just Transition for All approach which inbuilds ES sustainability objectives. The four mines have been identified (Banovici, Zenica, Gacko and Ugljevik) and the exact geographical scope of the impacts is yet unknown, but is not expected to exceed the existing footprint of mines and their associated facilities (waste tips, tailings deposits, workshops, etc.). Preparation for mine closure works include civil works such as reshaping and grading mine lands, geotechnical stabilization works, repair, repurposing and preservation of existing assets and infrastructure; expansion of infrastructure; re-vegetation and afforestation; drainage and hydrotechnical works. Depending on the specific situation there may be special tasks such as decontamination, water treatment or mine methane management. Civil works may have to be carried out above and below ground, may be complex, potentially of a significant scale, and associated with tangible environmental impacts. However, it is important to note that current environmental baseline conditions of the mines considered for future closure is very poor, thus the net environmental effects of sub-components related to lands repurposing of selected mines are expected to be significantly positive. 25. The planned technical assistance (TA) related to renewable power generation on former mining lands is not expected to cause direct environmental risks or impacts, where activities such as placement and operation of the photovoltaic systems may require small-scale construction works, these would entail anticipated and common environmental impacts that can be readily mitigated using adequate Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs) or ESMP checklists (dust, noise, land use, small-scale waste management, occupational, health and safety). Key identified existing risks and ongoing impacts the project seeks to mitigate are related to the current conditions and operation of the open pit and underground mines. They include: (i) water management and water quality issues; (ii) soil stability issues; (iii) soil contamination with chemicals or contaminated water limiting availability of land and impacting health of people; (iv) management of large quantities of mine waste which can potentially be contaminated and may be prone to combustion causing significant fire hazards. These risks would continue to be present with ongoing mines operation, and if mines are not closed properly become long term ES legacies. To capture and describe the risks of remediation and repurposing and to scope out the planned mine closure processes and instruments for their management, the Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF) will be prepared as a guiding document for the management of ES aspects. It will include a roadmap for ES review of closure, remediation, and repurposing of existing and abandoned mines. 26. The social risk rated is substantial due to risks and impacts associated with stakeholder engagement and managing public perceptions on Just Transition, land use impacts, and social inclusion issues. While the project will not support actual mine closures, it will support pre-closure planning and preparatory activities including feasibility studies, mine land repurposing scenarios, options for renewable energy (RE) generation and installation (e.g., solar PVs, biomass). Risks associated with TA under sub-components associated with RE options are assessed as Dec 06, 2022 Page 11 of 13 The World Bank Pilot Approaches To a Just Transition in Select Coal Regions of BiH (P179990) moderate due to potential downstream social implications that may arise when the results of the TA may lead to future investments in the RE installation. 27. Social risks and impacts of mine pre- closure planning phase include: (i) stakeholder engagement: it will be important to proactively sensitize workers and affected broader communities on upcoming employment and livelihood impacts, and to seek their views and inputs on alternative livelihood options; managing public perceptions on Just Transition and avoiding perceptions of greenwashing; and potential resistance to mine closures. (ii) land use impacts: new land acquisition and involuntary resettlement are not expected within the scope of the project, however there is a risk of informal land use on existing lands within mine sites, which may likely result in economic displacement of affected persons. In addition, during project preparation it will be assessed if there are any risks associated with informal waste pickers. (iii) social inclusion: a particular concern under Just Transition for All (JTA) approach is the impact of transition on already poor and vulnerable groups. In BiH, women and younger workers face significant challenges in the access to economic opportunities and are more likely to remain outside the labor force or migrate for work. In 2021, roughly 41 percent of potential workers were inactive, and 25 percent of youth 15-29 were not in education, employment, or training (NEET). Employment activities are substantially lower for women than for men, and the country has the second lowest female labor force participations in the Western Balkans. Working-age women accounted for two-thirds of the inactive population in 2021. The population in BiH is shrinking and ageing, while out-migration is an urgent challenge, representing a loss of young people and skilled workers. A particularly marginalized group is the Roma, who is the largest ethnic minority group, living in chronic poverty. Eventual mine closures and energy transition may also cause risks of increase in domestic violence, mental health issues and substance abuse as a consequence of job losses and change in living patterns. It will be important to address these risks during pre-closure planning phase. There are OHS and community health and safety risks related to land reclamation and repurposing works; and technical works associated with installation of RE power generation. Significant labor influx risk is not expected to be associated with activities supported by the project. The sexual exploitation and abuse/sexual harassment (SEA/SH) risks are assessed as moderate and discussed in a dedicated section. 28. The abovementioned risks and impacts will be mitigated and managed by measures included in project design and in ESF instruments. To address risks associated with stakeholder engagement and labor transition, the project design include establishment of multilevel policy dialogue mechanisms on JT; awareness campaign; and assessment technical assistance that will inform the design of labor transition support to affected workers as well and training, reskilling and labor activation programs for workers.
