BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING 1. This project paper seeks approval by the Division Director for an Additional Credit of US$100.4 million from the International Development Association (IDA) to the Burkina Faso Human Capital Protection Project (HCPP, P506528). The additional financing (AF) will scale up the HCPP, which was approved on September 19, 2024 and became effective on November 27, 2024. The parent project is financed by a US$100.4 million equivalent IDA credit and its project development objective (PDO) is “to support the Recipient’s efforts to ensure the provision of essential social services.” To meet this objective, the HCPP finances essential services in the health and education sectors for 2024 and 2025. As of October 16, 2025, the project has disbursed a total of US$97.6 million, or 97 percent of total World Bank financing following four phases of ex-ante audit reports that provided fiduciary assurance on the eligibility of expenditures for reimbursement. 2. The project is being implemented in Burkina Faso’s volatile security environment and challenging humanitarian context, highlighting the urgent need to maintain essential social services and prevent both short and long-term For Official Use Only declines in human capital. Ongoing security challenges have displaced over 2 million people 1 and disrupted thousands of health and education facilities, while severe food insecurity affects millions. Despite these challenges, the government has increased health and education budgets, enabling free healthcare consultations and immunizations for children as well as teacher training and the organization of exams. However, risks of service disruption remain high, especially in conflict- affected areas. Sustaining essential social services is crucial to prevent setbacks in poverty reduction and safeguard the country’s future productivity, making the HCPP a key investment in Burkina Faso’s resilience and development. A. Project Components 3. The project has two components: (a) ensuring the provision of essential health and education services (US$100 million equivalent); and (b) project management (US$400,000 equivalent). The criteria used to select essential services include: (i) emergency needs; (ii) impacts on service delivery; and (iii) capacity to manage fiduciary, environmental, and social risks. 4. Component 1: Ensuring the provision of essential health and education services (US$100 million equivalent). The operation finances essential health and education services included in the national budget. The component is divided into two subcomponents. (a) Subcomponent 1.1: Ensuring the provision of essential health services (US$60 million). The selected services in the health sector relate to the implementation of the free healthcare program, hospitals and health centers services, and routine vaccines. The operating costs include medical supplies (e.g., gloves, cotton, oxygen bottles, bands, essential drugs); nutrition products; routine vaccines; utilities (e.g., water and electricity); insurance; telecommunications including internet and phone communications; communications; office rent; fuel; transport and mission-related expenses; maintenance cost of vehicles, including cars, motorcycles, and ambulances; small office equipment (e.g., tables, chairs, shelves, office closets, computers, photocopiers, printers); and office supplies and consumables (e.g., paper, pads, and writing material). Eligible activities for climate-related activities are captured in Annex 2 of the parent project PAD (P506528)2 to address climate risks associated with malnutrition and the rise in malaria, meningitis, and dengue. (b) Subcomponent 1.2: Ensuring the provision of essential education services (US$40 million). The selected services relate to organizing exams and competitions; training and supervising teachers; and equipping teachers with materials. This includes pedagogical kits; reproduction and copying; transportation of exam- related material and personnel; expenses related to the correction of exam papers; consulting services of 1 According to the March 2023 census conducted by the National Council for Emergency Relief and Rehabilitation (Conseil National des Secours d’Urgence et de Réhabilitation, CONASUR, Government of Burkina Faso). 2 https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099091024150518697/pdf/BOSIB-c6acfda1-08d9-4b94-b4ed-daedef808879.pdf Official Use Only Page 1 The World Bank Additional Financing to Burkina Faso Human Capital Protection Project (P506528) examiners and correctors; and related expenses, including transport, support for information systems, translation costs, bank charges, and travel, transportation, per diem, and accommodation costs; training courses, seminars, workshops, and other training activities, including inspectors visits, space, and equipment rental; and local travel. Component 2: Project management (US$400,000 equivalent). This component finances the operating costs required to implement the project, including the management of fiduciary risk. Specifically, it covers : (a) ex-ante audit costs for the selected services to be conducted by the General Inspectorate of Finance (Inspection Générale des Finances, IGF); (b) ex-post annual independent audits to provide adequate fiduciary assurance on the use of project funds; (c) costs related to implementing the actions identified in the environmental and social assessments; and (d) other implementation-related costs. B. Implementation Performance For Official Use Only 6. Based on the May 2025 mid-term review, the Project ratings for Progress towards the achievement of PDO and Overall Implementation Progress (IP) are both satisfactory. This positive assessment is attributed to strong results under Component 1, where all four PDO indicators in the education and health sectors have been surpassed, and nine out of twelve intermediate results indicators have been achieved. 3 As of June 2025, 4 in the education sector, 98 percent of students benefited from annual exams, exceeding the target of 94 percent, 99.8 percent of teachers were certified by national training institutions, surpassing the target of 94 percent, and 69,000 teachers were provided with pedagogical kits, also exceeding the target. In the health sector, the project has facilitated 16.2 million free healthcare consultations for women and children under five years old, surpassing the target of 16 million, and 92.6 percent of children received the Pentavalent vaccine, against a target of 92 percent. A total of 1.13 million children were immunized. In addition, the payment rate for free care expenses increased from 75 percent in 2023 to 82 percent by June 2025. These efforts underscore the country’s commitment to protecting and enhancing human capital. 7. Under Component 2, which focuses on project management, the implementation of core governance arrangements as outlined in the Project Appraisal Document (PAD) represents a significant achievement. A functional steering committee is in place, providing strategic oversight and ensuring alignment with project objectives. Ex-ante audit arrangements, led by the IGF have been effectively implemented to strengthen financial oversight. The Project Coordination Unit (PCU) is supported by a well-established fiduciary platform staffed with qualified personnel whose roles now include day-to-day management of the project activities, in line with effectiveness requirements. A manual of procedures and an information management system have been established to guide implementation and improve coordination and accountability. In addition, key environment and social instruments have been publicly disclosed, eight of the thirteen have been completed, with the remaining actions under active follow-up. 8. Financial Management (FM) Performance is rated “Satisfactory,” based on several key achievements. A customized accounting system has been successfully set up; all operations are accurately recorded; and the first Interim Financial Report (IFR) has been prepared and submitted. Additional achievements include recruitment of the external auditor, setting up of the accounting system, preparation of bank reconciliations, and allocation of additional resources to the IGF to conduct the ex-ante audits. The Procurement rating is also “Satisfactory,” reflecting progress in small-scale procurement activities. The procurement plan has been approved and uploaded into the Systematic Tracking of Exchanges in Procurement (STEP) system. Finally, the grievance mechanism is accessible to the population, and as of October 7, 2025, no complaints have been received. C. Government Request and Rationale for Additional Financing 9. The Government of Burkina Faso has requested AF to scale up the project, aiming to support the delivery of essential health and education services for an additional year. Given the parent project’s strong results and the ongoing country situation, the proposed AF aims to support the government’s efforts to keep essential health and education services running in a still-fragile environment and challenging humanitarian context. The need for bridging financing between the already closed IDA-funded education and health projects and new ones remains due to delays in effectiveness and operationalization of these projects. The AF is proposed to continue the financing of identified critical recurrent expenditures in the health and education sectors with strong fiduciary controls (including tracking of expenditures) to ensure service delivery for populations. Disruption in these services will negatively impact citizens, particularly women and children in affected communities, leading to long-term losses in human capital. For Official Use Only 10. The proposed AF has been prepared under paragraph 12 of Section III of the World Bank IPF Policy, which allows for exceptions to standard requirements in situations of urgent need for assistance or capacity constraints. This provision has been triggered due to the ongoing fragility in Burkina Faso, where, in 2024, the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data base (ACLED) recorded approximately 7,500 security-related deaths, and more than two million citizens have been displaced, with widespread closure of health centers and schools and disruption of supply routes. By applying paragraph 12, the AF will enable the government to mitigate the impacts of conflict and fragility, maintain core functions, and deliver fast visible results to support essential service delivery. 11. The AF will conclude the cycle of recurrent-cost financing that has supported the government’s planned increases in health and education spending. The AF will also smooth the transition to other financing instruments and sources, including government resources and two new IDA-funded projects in the health and education sectors. The Burkina Faso Strengthening Health System Performance and Resilience Project (P180539) became effective in October 2025, while the Restoring Education System Performance and Improving Resilience Project (P502472) is expected to become effective by December 2025. Both operations include reform actions, some supported through Performance- based Conditions, that will further strengthen governance and service delivery of the health and education sectors. Adoption of the work plan for both projects is scheduled for December 2025, after which disbursements typically begin only after several months. The AF can therefore serve as bridge financing, as it can start implementing shortly after approval as there are no effectiveness conditions, and ex-ante audits are already being prepared by the IGF to enable a first disbursement shortly after effectiveness. 12. The government has demonstrated commitment to reforms in recent years. Specifically, the government has: (a) successfully implemented all the Performance and Policy Actions of the Sustainable Development Financing Policy (SDFP); (b) maintained progress under the IMF-supported program; and (c) made significant strides in mobilizing domestic revenues, and reducing energy subsidies and the wage bill, leading to a trend of fiscal consolidation, in line with the West Africa Economic and Monetary Union’s fiscal rules. The HCPP, together with the leveraged parallel financing, is critical to fully finance the government’s IMF-supported program, which remains on track and runs through 2027. 13. The operation remains fully aligned with the West and Central Africa (AFW) regional priority to strengthen core institutions for service delivery 2021-2025, 5 as well as selected priorities of the Regional Sahel Prevention and Resilience Allocation. D. Recent Developments in Support of Additional Financing 14. Burkina Faso continues to face significant challenges due to ongoing conflict, violence, and fragility, prompting a security-first approach and increased defense spending, which are already showing some positive results. Insecurity has displaced over 2.1 million people 8 and strained social cohesion. Despite these difficulties, key government initiatives such as the Presidential Initiative for Agriculture and “Faso Meebo” have garnered public support. Reforms to strengthen state presence, improve service delivery, and enhance justice and governance have enabled the securitization of nearly 700 localities, the return of more than one million internally displaced persons, and the redeployment of 5,800 civil servants. 9 Schools and health facilities are gradually reopening, with over a million displaced children resuming their education. The reopening of courts has improved access to justice for 1.3 million people, and mobile courts have issued identity documents to over 400,000 citizens 10 Additional measures, including prosecuting security abuses, training For Official Use Only Volunteers for the Defense of the Homeland in humanitarian law, and digitalizing payments, aim to rebuild trust and strengthen resilience. 15. Independent data from ACLED corroborate the improvement in security conditions in 2024 and 2025. Between 2023 and 2024, fatalities fell by 11.5 percent and security incidents decreased by 26.1 percent (Figure 1). This trend accelerated in the first eight months of 2025, with 4,159 security-related deaths recorded—1,867 fewer than over the same period in 2024. The increased government budget allocations for security and defense (Figure 2) appear to have contributed to this gradual improvement in security. Nevertheless, the overall security context remains challenging, still disrupting some supply routes and forcing the closure of health centers and schools. The government faces the complex task of stabilization amid limited budgetary resources. Domestically, the political situation remains unchanged since September 2022. The transition charter provides for a transition period of 60 months starting in July 2024, but states that elections marking the end of the transition may be held before this deadline if the security situation allows. 11 Figure 1. The government is working towards recalibrating the composition of expenditure by reducing defense and security spending to generate fiscal space for social spending. Due to the fast-spreading insecurity, the government was required to dramatically increase defense and security spending, with a spike in 2023. Overall, security and defense spending has become more transparent with bringing the Homeland Support Fund on budget per requirement under the IMF-supported program. The program sets structural benchmarks and prior actions to improve fiscal transparency and governance, including continuous structural benchmarks on transparency of public procurement contracts and budget execution that have so far been met. Defense and security spending declined in 2024 and is budgeted to fall further in 2025. The AF will give an additional year of support to manage the scale-back in defense spending, without compromising health and education spending. Over time, lower defense spending will create fiscal space to exit from the HCPP and to finance essential social services with the government’s own resources. 17. IV. APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis 34. The assessment from the parent project still holds; project implementation will lead to the continued provision of selected basic health and education services in an environment of insecurity and tight fiscal space. The project supports the government’s efforts in overcoming service disruptions and fostering a positive state presence, particularly in fragile areas. The two project components comprise: (a) the provision of essential health and education services; and Official Use Only Page 11 The World Bank Additional Financing to Burkina Faso Human Capital Protection Project (P506528) (b) financing of project implementation. This is expected to increase the provision of healthcare, specifically for women and children, through the implementation of the free health strategy, the operation of hospitals and health centers, and support of vaccination campaigns. In the education sector, the project will benefit students and teachers by supporting the organization of exams and the provision of teacher training and materials. The absence of this support could result in increased vulnerability to shocks and significant adverse economic, human capital, and poverty developments—both at the country level and for the poorest and most vulnerable populations. 35. Investing in Burkina Faso’s human capital promises substantial economic returns expected to surpass the projected operational costs. The focus on enhancing essential health services and education services comes at a critical time, as disruptions of health services since the onset of COVID-19 have led to increased illness and mortality. By bolstering essential health services, the project could significantly improve the health outcomes for women and children, with many of the proposed interventions being highly cost-effective and lifesaving. The cost-effectiveness analysis conducted for the HCPP still holds and the early achievement of PDO indicators suggests that the AF represents a valuable investment in the country’s future, with benefits far outweighing costs.
