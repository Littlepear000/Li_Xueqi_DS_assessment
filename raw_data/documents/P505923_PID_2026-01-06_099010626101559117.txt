Introduction and Context Country Context 1. The Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (SCBFE) Program is an ambitious and transformational commitment to create jobs through sustainable management of the Congo Basin Forests (CBF). The world’s second- largest tropical forest system yields significant local, national, and global benefits, absorbing around six times more carbon annually than the Amazon. The Program’s dual focus on jobs and sustainability is especially important in CBF countries (Cameroon, Central African Republic (CAR), Republic of Congo (RoC), Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Gabon, and Equatorial Guinea) given the potential of their natural resources and growing young population. This Multi-Phase Page 2 Official Use Only The World Bank Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (P505923) Programmatic Approach (MPA) proposes an indicative financing envelope of US$1.03 billion. Phase 1 is expected to create 220,000 jobs and reduce annual regional greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions on average by 17.6 million tCO2e. 2. The Congo Basin region is characterized by low economic growth, extreme poverty, and high fragility. Gross domestic product (GDP) growth in the Central African Economic and Monetary Community (Communauté Économique et Monétaire de l'Afrique Centrale, CEMAC)1 region, decelerated to 1.8 percent in 2023, down from 3.1 percent in 2022.2 Nearly 13 percent of the world’s extreme poor live in the CBF countries,3 with the majority of extreme poor located around forested landscapes, where they depend heavily on subsistence agriculture and forest resources, with limited access to basic services and economic opportunities. Ongoing conflicts and insecurity in the region continue to displace thousands and exacerbate disputes over control of natural resources.4 3. The region is missing opportunities to leverage natural resources for economic and structural transformation, a critical prerequisite for job creation. The contribution of the forest industry to GDP (ranging from 2 to 10 percent in CBF countries in 2021) has stagnated, largely due to a focus on exporting logs rather than developing local wood processing. Unreported extraction and fragmented domestic markets undermine price discovery, traceability, and reliability of legal supply for downstream processors. 4. Concurrently, job-intensive domestic and export activities are stagnant, and most businesses are informal and characterized by low-productivity.5 The need for more and better jobs is exacerbated by high population growth rates and increases in working-age adults. Estimates project that the population Central African countries will increase by a third _For Official Use Only_ by 2035 and double by 20506. Population and demographic shifts will increase the demand for jobs and urbanization, with a concomitant increase in demand for cooking energy, food production, and raw materials7,8. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. The CBF cover about 200 million ha, an area slightly larger than Indonesia or Mexico, and account for between 35-90 percent of the total national land areas in each of the six CBF countries. The CBF is the only tropical forest system that still serves as a global carbon sink.9, These forests sustain an estimated 40 million people in nearby urban centers and directly provide livelihoods for an estimated 60 million people who live in or near the forest areas, including local communities and Indigenous Peoples, comprising at least 150 different ethnic groups.10 Critically, about 100 million ha (or about half of the CBF), are not under active management11 and are mostly unallocated forests that could be converted to other uses. About 54 million ha of the CBF are under commercial forest concessions, while Protected Areas of high conservation value constitute 30 million ha. 6. Forest loss is rising in the CBF, driven by subsistence-scale fuelwood and charcoal production, mining, small- scale agriculture, and informal extraction of timber, particularly in unallocated or unmanaged forest areas. Studies show that nearly half (48 percent) of CBF wood production takes place informally, mainly from these unallocated forests. For example, the informal charcoal value chain is valued at millions of dollars and contributes thousands of jobs. However, high levels of informality raise due diligence costs for investors and undermines creditworthiness. Unmanaged, informal logging also leads to social problems such as conflicts and labor abuse. 7. Community co-management in unallocated forests could improve sustainability and expand economic opportunities for communities. This approach has been effective in other tropical regions, such as in Latin America and Asia, when combined with clear tenure security and institutional and technical support. CBF countries have robust forest laws, and many have recently updated their national forest laws with provisions for community forest management; but implementation has been limited (see Box 1). For instance, Cameroon has designated 1.5 million ha as community forests, but most communities do not have technical or financial means to prepare and implement effective management plans. 8. Despite being the source of 80 percent of Africa’s wood exports, CBF countries account for only half a percent of the global wood value chain12. Wood is one of the top export earners – first most valuable export for CAR, second for Gabon and RoC, and third for Cameroon. Most wood exports are subject to minimal processing, and are shipped as sawn wood or logs, in order to meet the requirements of the CEMAC log export ban13. However, enforcement of this ban has been uneven throughout the region, with Gabon being the only country to fully implement it. The absence of structured internal wood supply chains and contract-based relationships between large logging concessionaires and wood processing Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) holds back the sector's potential. Regulatory inconsistencies across the CEMAC _For Official Use Only_ region, including differing processing mandates, tax regimes, and product definitions and standards, also limit the sector’s competitiveness and integration. 9. Development of the wood value chains in the region is also limited by the lack of robust timber legality verification systems, skilled labor, and accessible financing. Only 24 percent of forest concessions in the RoC have third- party certification for legality/sustainability. Community forest institutions and SMEs face high fixed costs for legality verification, limiting their access to premium international markets.14 National systems like Cameroon’s SIGIF 2.0 (Système Informatique de Gestion des Informations Forestières) Management System are being developed with support from the European Union’s (EU) Forest Law Enforcement, Governance, and Trade (FLEGT) Action Plan and Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) of the United Kingdom. SIGIF 2.0 issues automatic barcodes across the timber value chain but lacks full compliance with EU Timber Regulations (EUTR). Most countries in the region lack specialized vocational institutions aligned with modern industry needs, while existing training centers are often under-resourced. Banks view the forest sector as high-risk, limiting operators’ ability to invest in processing.15 10. To fully unlock the potential of CBF for driving economic growth, creating jobs, and reducing poverty, it is essential to implement targeted interventions across both upstream and downstream segments of the wood value- chain. This approach should include: (i) supporting the effective implementation of regulations and procedures, with a particular focus on empowering local and community institutions by strengthening their capacity to oversee forest management, enforce compliance, and participate in decision-making processes. Project Description 14. The Program follows the World Bank Group approach to job creation, with three mutually reinforcing Components. The Program is designed to remove binding constraints and enable private sector solutions and job creation across activities. Component 1 Policies and Institutions supports regulatory and fiscal reforms to reduce disincentives for private investment and harmonization of trade barriers, thereby reducing market distortions and improving predictability for private actors. Interventions also support enhanced wood traceability and certification systems, directly addressing constraints such as illegality, which hinder private sector entry into forestry businesses and access to premium markets. Component 2 Forest Resources Development invests in the foundational infrastructure facilitating smallholders and private investment in agroforestry and plantations through seed banks and technical support, as well as investments in community forest economies, protected areas management, improving opportunities for nature-based tourism, production capacity, processing equipment, and access to infrastructure to enable SMEs and community enterprises to grow commercially. Component 2 also enhances smallholder entry into the wood value chain through buy-back schemes, and PPPs. Component 3 Industry and Enterprise enhances private sector access to finance and infrastructure, with private Page 5 Official Use Only The World Bank Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (P505923) capital enabling (PCE) potential through the creation of SME wood service parks and Special Economic Zones (SEZs), directly enabling private processing firms to establish manufacturing operations in the CBF countries. 15. The Program operationalizes a One World Bank Group approach that sequences World Bank supported reforms and public investments with IFC advisory/investment and MIGA guarantees to unlock private capital across the CBF wood value chain. IFC and MIGA have been actively involved in the preparation of the Program and will continue to coordinate closely with the World Bank during implementation to align strategies, identify entry points for private sector engagement, investigate country-specific constraints for Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) and private investment, as well as for local content development, and pursue the complementarity of public and private interventions. Component 1. Policies and Institutions 16. Component 1.1. Policies and Regulations. This subcomponent will cover the development and enactment of regulations to operationalize national Forest Codes, emphasizing the assignment of forest management duties to local communities, simplifying procedures for the approval of community forest management plans, and establishing permit protocols for small-scale forestry operations. 17. Component 1.2. Traceability, Certification, and Information Systems. National traceability and legality verification systems will be improved to facilitate the entry of legally sourced timber into domestic and export markets and to update forest inventories for more accurate management planning. These systems will be applied nationwide to help prevent unauthorized logging outside project areas. Activities will include capacity building for forest officials, civil _For Official Use Only_ society, and private sector stakeholders regarding system use. Financial support will be allocated for (i) hardware, software, and technical expertise for the development of forest inventories and data on forest carbon, where feasible, using digital Monitoring, Reporting and Verification (d-MRV); and (ii) improved traceability and legality verification systems, including equipment, connectivity, hardware, software, training, and support for SMEs. Component 2. Forest Resource Development 18. Sub-component 2.1. Foundational Infrastructure for Forest Resource Development. Support will include: (i) preparation of plantation strategies and plans; (ii) establishment of forest seed collection and storage centers; (iii) conducting silviculture trials and research on lesser-known timber species; (iv) planting on government and private lands, including agroforestry initiatives led by smallholders and communities; (v) forest regeneration on degraded lands managed by government agencies; (vi) provision of essential equipment such as Information Technology (IT), GPS devices, and internet connectivity; and (vii) supply of fire management equipment. 19. Sub-component 2.2. Community Forest Economies and Local Development. This subcomponent supports Community Forests and Communal Forest institutions. Key activities will include: (i) capacity building to empower and strengthen community forest committees; (ii) delivery of technical and professional assistance for developing forest management plans, replanting in logged or deforested areas; (iii) procurement of equipment for timber logging and small or mobile sawmills; (iv) development of storage and processing infrastructure for NTFP processing such as oil presses and charcoal kilns; and (v) rehabilitation, upgrading, or expansion of critical small-scale infrastructure to improve market access and essential services for remote communities, including feeder roads, drinking water boreholes, structures for community meetings and markets, solar panels, and enhanced digital connectivity where possible. Investment decisions will be made through participatory processes, aligned with local development plans, and communities will enter subsidiary agreements with service provider organizations to prepare management plans and subproject proposals for economic activities. Project Implementing Units (PIUs) will select professional organizations and NGOs to deliver these services. 20. Sub-component 2.3. Management of Protected Areas. Activities under this subcomponent will strengthen the management of protected areas and provide targeted support to Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities (IPLCs) residing in or near buffer zones. Protected area selection will be guided by national priorities and, in the case of Page 6 Official Use Only The World Bank Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (P505923) transboundary sites, by intergovernmental agreements. Specific locations for activities will be determined according to the selection criteria outlined in Annex 7 (Table 7.1 and Box 7.1). Key activities include: (i) preparation and/or implementation of management plans for prioritized national or transboundary protected areas, excluding law enforcement and the redrawing of boundaries. Investments will be aligned with the needs identified in each management plan and may encompass habitat restoration, biodiversity and forest status monitoring using geospatial tools and community engagement; (ii) construction and upgrading of essential park infrastructure, especially in areas at risk from climate change impacts (such as firebreaks, guard stations, and park boundary markers); and (iii) enhancement of management effectiveness tools for protected areas, transitioning from the Management Effectiveness Tracking Tool (METT) to the Integrated Management Effectiveness Tool (IMET). It is important to note that law enforcement activities and support for the establishment or redrawing of existing protected area boundaries will not be financed. 21. Support for IPLCs will comprise: (i) participatory development of culturally appropriate management plans for communities in or near protected areas; (ii) initiatives to promote alternative livelihoods and improve food security; (iii) efforts to preserve and strengthen Traditional Ecological Knowledge Systems, particularly those related to native seeds and crops, to bolster climate resilience; and (iv) measures to mitigate human-wildlife conflicts and safeguard crops and human settlements. Additionally, COMIFAC will facilitate regional dialogue and cooperation among countries managing transboundary protected areas under the MPA. Component 3. The environmental and social risks are rated as High. These risks are viewed at two levels: the project-specific risks, which are relatively contained and technical in nature, and the contextual and sector-wide risks, which are more complex, interlinked, and influenced by external drivers beyond the project’s direct control. While the former can be addressed through well-established mitigation measures, the implementation context in the Congo Basin significantly elevates overall risk due to its ecological sensitivity, biodiversity, carbon value, and systemic risks in the wood sector including illegal logging, deforestation, weak governance, fragility and conflict-affected (FCV) conditions, and limited oversight. The most significant risk is that project-supported wood processing enterprises could inadvertently increase Page 8 Official Use Only The World Bank Sustainable Congo Basin Forest Economies (P505923) demand for illegally harvested timber, which may be further exacerbated by weak traceability, while transboundary pressures such as wildlife trafficking, uneven regulatory enforcement, and artisanal mining could exacerbate deforestation and conflict. Amplified by weak institutional capacity and the FCV context of several participating countries, these systemic, multi-layered, and interlinked risks—ranging from localized impacts to broader cumulative governance and ecosystem threats—justify a high environmental and social risk classification for the MPA. 27. To manage Phase 1 environmental and social risks, participating countries have prepared framework environmental and social instruments—including Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF), Resettlement Framework (RF), Indigenous Peoples Planning Framework (IPPF), Security Management Plan (SMP) in CAR, Labor Management Plan (LMP) and Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP) which have been widely consulted and will be disclosed before appraisal. The 3 ESMFs have been disclosed in the respective countries and on the World Bank website17. While the physical relocation of households is not expected, the project may still affect livelihoods by restricting access to grazing areas, firewood collection, hunting, and fishing. To address such livelihood impacts, draft Process Frameworks (PF) have been developed as a precautionary measure since the participating countries have not selected the final sites to be financed by the project and will be updated to reflect site specific aspects once identified. The PF ensures a structured transparent and participatory approach, incorporating Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) and details the approach for mitigation, compensation, and benefit-sharing measures, with particular focus on vulnerable and marginalized groups. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 28. Each country operation will be independently implemented with one project implementation unit (PIU) under the oversight of the lead line Ministry. Implementation will be led by the respective Ministries responsible for forests as the lead Ministry for this operation. The PIUs will be staffed with experts on all three pillars of the Program. Staff could be seconded from within the government or contracted through competitive selection. In each country, the lead Ministry could enter into a cooperation agreement with relevant ministries (i.e., industry, trade and commerce, SMEs, Planning, lands and construction, finance, etc.) responsible for providing technical advice and participating in Steering Committees and Technical Committees for the Program, especially on Pillar 3 activities, outside of their own area of responsibility (i.e. SME Service Park approval and oversight, support to private sector processing activities, financial sector regulation, etc.). Furthermore, the ministries of forests would enter into a subsidiary agreement with apex institutions that will select the financial intermediaries to manage credit facilities to support eligible private sector enterprises and companies. 29. At the Regional level CEMAC and COMIFAC will be responsible for implementation of the regional activities under Program. CEMAC will convene a Regional Steering Committee of Ministers of Economy & Ministers of Forests to carry out annual review of progress on implementation of the Program. An independent panel of experts will be enlisted by COMIFAC to advise and facilitate transparent and continuous stakeholder engagement during implementation. Detailed descriptions of implementation arrangements for Phase 1 are presented in Annexes 1 -4. The PIU in CEMAC will be housed within the Department for Sustainable Development and Industry. Both CEMCAC and COMIFAC will have PIUs with fiduciary capacity for implementation and monitoring and reporting on the Regional Project activities. B.
