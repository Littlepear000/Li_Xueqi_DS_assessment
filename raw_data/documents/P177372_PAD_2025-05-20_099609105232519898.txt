STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Gabon is one of Africa’s few upper-middle-income countries, with good economic growth and recovery but heavily reliant on oil and other extractive sectors. The gross domestic product (GDP) per capita is estimated at US$8,238 (2024),1 supported mainly by oil, timber, manganese and other minerals. Rich in natural resources and the fifth largest oil producer in Africa, Gabon experienced strong economic growth over the past decade, and its post-pandemic recovery held up well in the face of recent global shocks.2 Despite efforts to promote economic diversification, the oil sector is still the country’s main engine of growth.3 Oil production is expected to decline from 2025 onwards as oil fields reach maturity. However, economic growth is set to continue in the medium term, propelled by the non-oil economy (the mining, timber and agricultural sectors),4 which is the focus of the government’s diversification plans. As for Gabon’s fiscal situation, prospects of diminishing oil revenues and rising expenditures pose important risks to the country's public finances. 2. Despite its economic performance and mid-level ranking on the Human Development Index, about a third of all Gabonese still live in poverty, and spatial inequalities are large. Using the international poverty line for upper middle- income countries 5, the urban poor make up about 26 percent of Gabon’s total population. Although the percentage of rural population living in poverty (60 percent) is still twice as high as in urban areas, with 29.4 percent, a significant part of the urban population is considered poor. Urban poverty is slightly more pronounced in secondary cities (21.2 percent in Libreville and Port-Gentil compared to 38.2 percent in other urban centers). Although poverty has declined over the past decades, the pace of decline has been slower than population growth, resulting in an absolute increase in the number of poor, mostly in urban areas. 3. High expectations generated after the regime change in 2023 are putting pressure on public spending, through an ambitious infrastructure and political reform program and the ‘National Development Plan for the Transition (Plan National de Développement pour la Transition, PNDT) 2024-2026’6. Elevated social expectations, new programs and the planned investments under the PNDT 2024-2026 have increased expenditures, particularly on infrastructure projects. Large-scale projects include electricity and water system expansions, water treatment centers and the rehabilitation and expansion of the national road network, including the Transgabonese highway connecting the capital to Franceville, Gabon’s third-largest city. In addition, public works on urban roads have been launched in the country’s main cities, including cities targeted by the project. 4. Gabon is highly vulnerable to the impacts of natural hazards and climate change, particularly to flooding, sea- level rise, landslides, storms and changes in rainfall patterns. In 2022, Gabon ranked 126th out of 187 countries on the ND-GAIN Index,7 which measures a country’s vulnerability to climate change and its readiness to improve resilience. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. Although sparsely populated, with 2.4 million (2023)13 inhabitants on a heavily forested area of 268,000 sq km, Gabon has one of Africa’s highest urbanization levels. More than four-fifths of the population lives in cities. The urbanization level increased from 20 percent in the early 1960s to 91 percent in 2023 and is expected to rise to 95 percent by 2050.14 The capital, Libreville, and the second largest city, Port Gentil, which is the country’s economic capital, are home to 45 percent of the total population. Apart from Libreville (703,940 inhabitants), Port-Gentil (136,462 inhabitants) and Franceville (110,568 inhabitants), the remaining 14 cities are relatively small, with populations between 10,000 and 80,000, and there are several towns of less than 5,000 inhabitants. Gabon‘s secondary cities are generally sparsely populated, spatially expansive and poorly connected. They are of strategic importance given their natural resources and demographic dynamics, but lack of connectivity hampers the realization of their economic potential. Gabon’s urban development is characterized by people migrating from rural areas to the economic hubs of Libreville and Port-Gentil (where the oil industry and ports are based), and to a certain extent towards Moanda and Franceville15 (where the mining industry is located). Secondary cities have a key role to play in the country’s territorial development by connecting sparsely populated regions and acting as economic centers for the wood and mining sectors, which are based in the interior of the country and have the potential to reduce spatial and economic inequalities. Furthermore, with investments in proper planning and the expansion of infrastructure networks and services, secondary cities have the potential to grow into intermediate hubs to employ Gabonese from the rural hinterland, particularly the youth, thereby supporting the demographic transition.16 6. Gabon’s cities face several livability and resilience challenges that limit their potential to contribute to the country’s sustainable economic and social development. Over the past two decades, the lack of national spending on secondary cities17 resulted in substantial deficits in basic infrastructure and services. Limited investments and the absence of urban planning have led to rapid and uncontrolled urbanization in secondary cities, including the proliferation of informal settlements, which now account for 36.6 percent of the urban population. These settlements are often located in areas prone to flooding and landslides. The country is at high risk of hydrometeorological hazards and disasters, affecting its urban centers and resulting in damage to its assets and disrupting economic activities.23 Gabon has a tropical climate, receiving 1,500 mm to 3,500 mm of precipitation per year,24 and an extremely dense hydrological network feeding various rivers. Most cities are prone to pluvial and fluvial flooding, and some are at risk of sea-level rise and coastal flooding. Direct flood losses and damage in the housing sector are estimated at US$135 million25 AAL and are expected to increase 1.7 times to US$230 million under future climate conditions (2050-2095).26 Infrastructure in Gabon is facing an increasing risk of damage due to higher precipitation and more intense flooding events. Without adaptation measures, it is estimated that damage to roads could result in significant annual costs ranging from US$14.4 to US$15.9 million per year by 2040.27 Lack of or inappropriate flood protection infrastructure is one reason for such high damages. Urban drainage systems are generally underdeveloped and/or poorly maintained, and flow is impeded by the built environment and waste-clogged drains,28 which exacerbates flood risk and contributes to environmental degradation. Rising temperatures and rainfall variability may lead to prolonged warm and dry spells, intensifying urban heat effects in several cities. Urban heat poses health risks to residents and negatively affects labor productivity. Surface temperature in Gabonese cities during the hottest months of the year exceed 45 degrees Celsius. A continued increase in built surfaces could further push urban temperatures beyond climate-related increases.29 To control these heat impacts, efforts are urgently needed to (i) mitigate the carbon footprint of urban areas through, e.g., green buildings and transport; and (ii) implement adaptation measures such as neighborhood greening, improved ventilation and cooling. As hydrometeorological hazards are likely to intensify with climate change and impact more residents, it is essential to invest in cities’ resilience to climate shocks and protect urban infrastructure and assets. 8. The decentralization process has been slow to transfer the responsibilities and resources that municipalities need to implement their urban development plans. They also lack financial autonomy for capital investments. The Gabonese Decentralization Law (Organic Law No. 001/2014 of June 14, 2015), provides multiple sources of funding for municipal development, including own-source revenues and state transfers, loans and other resources. The new Constitution aims to strengthen local governments through the decentralization of responsibilities and the transfer of powers, competencies and resources needed to exercise those responsibilities. However, Law and other decentralization provisions, including fiscal transfers, have yet to be implemented, and the national government continues to play a key role in urban planning and infrastructure investment in secondary cities. Municipalities lack the necessary resources and capacities to manage urbanization and deliver services according to their mandate. Some of the challenges include limited finances and own-source revenue capacity; lack of qualified personnel; and limited hiring autonomy. Furthermore, the transition regime has placed local governments under the administration of “special delegates” appointed by the government. The staffing and financing authority of the local government is limited, however, and decision making is guided by the supervisory (national) authorities. This situation has delayed the development and implementation of several urban and land-use plans.32 The capacities of municipalities need to be strengthened to enable them to take on a greater role in urban management and assume responsibilities when decentralization further progresses. 10. The proposed Gabon Urban Development Project (Projet d’Aménagement et de Développement des Infrastructures du Gabon, PADIG) aims to support the Government of Gabon’s efforts to enhance urban livability and climate resilience in seven secondary cities. The investments will focus mainly on connective infrastructure, flood protection infrastructure and rehabilitation of basic public facilities and open spaces. The seven cities – Franceville, Oyem, Lambaréné, Koulamoutou, Mouila, Lébamba and Ndende33 – together account for about 13 percent of Gabon’s population34. They are connected and crossed by national (interurban) roads, along which relatively dense commercial activities have developed. Climate impacts and the poor condition of the urban road system, however, make it difficult for residents to access schools, health facilities and economic opportunities. F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 32. The project’s design reflects lessons learned from the previous urban development projects in Gabon. The proposed project is a follow-on to the Local Infrastructure Development Projects 1 (P082812) and 2 (P151077), and focuses on secondary cities where investments need to be completed.58 The ILDP1 (US$25 million), which closed in 2011, supported the provision of urban infrastructure and services in six provincial capitals: Libreville, Lambaréné, Oyem, Mouila, Port-Gentil and Franceville. It also established basic technical capacity in those provincial capitals where capacity was lacking. The ILDP2 (US$100 million), was a continuation of the ILDP1. It aimed at improving access to urban infrastructure and services in underserviced neighborhoods, and building capacities for municipal management in the target cities. The main lessons from the ILDP projects are as follows: (i) to facilitate implementation, project design should be based on in-depth assessments of the Borrower’s implementation capacity and technical readiness, and consider only a limited number of components and targeted activities; (ii) to ensure the quality of implementation, it is necessary to place the implementation responsibility inside a technical ministry; (iii) a key reason for the underperformance of ILDP2 was the lack of adequate capacity in project management of the PIU; and (iv) to ensure the quality of the infrastructure built and of project implementation, independent supervision engineering firms, which supervises works and ensure quality, are required. As a result of past experience with limited infrastructure implementation capacity, the proposed project focuses on roads, public spaces and facilities, school rehabilitation and drainage. Additional lessons from ILDP2 suggested strengthening stakeholder buy-in and community support for project investments. The previous project highlighted the need to: (i) fully align with the government’s priorities and establish formal mechanisms for collaboration, such as a Project Steering Committee at a high political level to ensure political commitment and stakeholder coordination; and (ii) involve communities and municipalities in the selection and implementation of project activities to foster community buy-in and social cohesion around the project. 34. This project also builds on the spatially coordinated approach from other secondary cities projects financed by the World Bank in West and Central Africa. Past secondary cities projects in West Africa have suffered from scattered and spatially uncoordinated investments, limiting impact at scale and agglomeration effects. More recent secondary cities projects, such as Mauritania Decentralization and Productive Intermediary Cities (P169332), have instead concentrated high-impact investments (e.g. urban roads, drainage systems, electricity network extension, markets, waste management, public spaces and parks) in a few dynamic cities. For example, the Mauritania Decentralization project has completed an initial rapid urban investment program in its seven target cities, and more than 47,000 people have already benefited from improved access to services. The proposed project design also embeds experience from recent World Bank projects in Niger and Chad (e.g., ‘Niger Refugees and Host Communities Support Project - PARCA (P164563)’, and ‘Chad Territorial Development and Resilience Project- RESICHAD (P177163)’), which demonstrated that a spatially driven, multisectoral approach combining investments in infrastructure, basic services, economic development and community engagement is key to achieving inclusive and transformative development. Part of this integrated approach is to strengthen the capacity of local institutions to plan and manage public services and respond to community needs, which helps to foster a social contract in a low-capacity environment. The proposed project’s city selection is derived from the cities’ hierarchy and their level of importance as provincial capitals, as well as their proximity to projected development corridors. Franceville, Koulamoutou, Oyem, Lambarene and Mouila were selected as the main provincial capitals to promote balanced territorial development. The first four cities (Franceville, Koulamoutou, Oyem and Lambarene), benefited from urban infrastructure and service investments under ILDP2 – some of which remained incomplete at project closure. These cities were included in the proposed project to secure the benefits intended under ILDP2. Mouila was added as a fifth provincial capital due to its proximity to a major road to the Republic of Congo, which will multiply its spillover effects. Given the importance of regional corridor development, the cities of Lebamba and Ndende, both located in the vicinity of Mouila, have also been selected to strengthen the development of Ngounié Province. This prioritization strategy aligns with Gabon’s national development strategy. Other secondary cities will be considered in subsequent phases of the project in alignment with the government’s strategic objectives. The budget allocation per city is based on a composite criterion including demographics, service rates, poverty, and exposure to natural hazards, inter alia. 45. Priority urban roads and public facilities have been identified in all seven cities (Component 1). A series of urban roads and public facilities have been identified in all seven cities. The selection process was conducted by the government in coordination with the PIU and local authorities and further informed by community consultations during preparation. The selection was based on four criteria: technical complexity,63 flood exposure, readiness level, and alignment with cities’ plans to address the main challenges identified in the urban diagnostics. 46. Drainage and flood protection infrastructure investments have been identified in the first four cities to protect vulnerable populations (Component 2). Three main criteria (hazard exposure, vulnerability level, and urban land use & population characteristics), as well as alignment with national and local strategies (e.g. urban development plans) were used to identify priority investments in drainage and flood protection infrastructure. The selection was informed by quantitative analysis (i.e., flood modeling and risk assessments) and qualitative data, gathered during local consultations and field visits, in the first four cities – Lambaréné, Oyem, Koulamoutou and Franceville. Once validated with stakeholders and the MTP, further consultation was conducted with the local authorities. Gabon is located at the equator, with a hot and humid tropical climate. Forests covering most of its territory and a mangrove ecosystem add to a diverse and largely intact ecosystems which plays an integral role in absorbing greenhouse gases and supporting the country’s extensive biodiversity. Precipitation is generally uniform throughout the country, though areas in the northeast, near the country’s capital, can experience wetter conditions. Temperature is variable across the country and is generally higher in central and western areas, with average annual values peaking at about 27˚C. Between 1990-2020, monthly average temperatures ranged between 26˚C in March and 23˚C in July, while monthly average precipitation varied from around 13 mm in July to 290 mm in October. Climate change projections indicate more variable rainfall in the future with a high likelihood of increased mean precipitation and more intense and heavy rainfall events, particularly along the cost and northwestern zones.88 Average annual temperature has increased by 0.6°C from 1960-2019, with the rate of warming increasing in the last decade and predicted to continue increasing through 2050 by at least 0.9° to 2.5°C.89 Relative mean sea level in Gabon has increased by ca. 70 mm (ca 3 mm per year) between 1995- 2015. Coastal erosion is estimated at 100-250 m of shoreline since 1950, and mangrove systems have disappeared at a rate of 50 hectares per year between 1960-199090. 2. The country is highly vulnerable to the impacts of natural hazards and climate change, particularly to flooding, heat, landslides, and precipitation changes. According to the 2025 Inform Risk Index, Gabon’s socio-economic vulnerabilities (significant poverty and inequality) and limited institutional capacities to address climate and disaster risk, combined with the infrastructural deficit, are mainly responsible for its significant risk ranking91.Flooding, including coastal, urban and riverine floods, is a particular high hazard for Gabon and potentially damaging and life-threatening river floods are expected to occur at least once every 10 years. With raising temperatures and more variable but uncertain rainfall patterns, more extreme and prolonged hot and dry spells may also be likely in Gabon. The number of days of extreme heat (above 35°C) is expected to increase 25–75 percent annually by the 2060. This will impact health and labor- productivity but also sensitives sectors such as agriculture and energy through diminishing water availability. Heatwaves occurring since early 2024 have already led to increased energy demand, and lower river levels affected hydropower generation, causing frequent outages over the year. 3. Climate change is significantly impacting Gabon's urban population and infrastructures. The country faces increased urban flooding risks due to changes in precipitation patterns, high urbanization and degradation of natural protective systems (e.g. mangroves) which are crucial as a buffer during flood events. An estimated 37 percent of the population in Libreville is directly threatened by floods92. Libreville and Port Gentil are projected to experience increased annual rainfall as well as extreme rainfall, in addition to longer dry spells. Lack of budgeting and public spending in the urban sector resulted in substantial deficits in infrastructure and services in most cities. Weak urban infrastructure in the capital city, Libreville, has led to substantial monetary losses. By 2050, climate change is expected to result in annual infrastructure damages of $64 million in Libreville only. Without adaptation measures, it is estimated that damages to roads nationwide could result in significant annual costs ranging from US$14.4 million to US$15.9 million by the 2040s. Therefore, investments in climate proofing of urban infrastructure and services are a must, alongside the implementation of adequate spatial planning and urban management. Thus, strategic, integrated spatial planning and urban management, including the enforcement of zoning laws or building codes, will become even more important under future climate change scenarios. 4. Sub-component 1.2: Strengthening the capacity of cities, communities and the national government for resilient urban planning and management (US$10 million IBRD equivalent) Adaptation activities: • Integration of climate resilience aspects into POSs and SDAUs – The integration of climate risks will guide resilient infrastructure planning and facilitate risk-informed land use planning, promotion of NBS for flood and heat adaptation, development of solid waste management plans (to reduce drain clogging as a factor for flood risk), drainage master plans (reduce flood risk). • Capacity building for community engagement: Key government officials and communities will be sensitized and trained on climate change aspect, resilience and NBS so they better operate and maintain investments for longer term sustainability and climate benefits of those investments. • Technical assistance on the preparation of climate-resilient investments (preparatory studies). The TA will finance preparatory studies for investments such as intercity roads, markets and public buildings that will be prepared with a focus on climate- resilient design. This will build capacity within the GoG to further and independently prepare urban investments integrating climate resilience in the design. Component 2: Flood Risk Reduction Infrastructure (US$55 million equivalent) Adaptation activities: • Climate-informed flood risk modelling will inform the design of urban drainage and flood risk reduction infrastructure. • Drainage will be designed based on climate future projections to reduce the impact of flooding in flood-prone areas. Prioritized urban drainage systems will be constructed to build flood resilience in dense urban areas and vulnerable settlements • Increasing the capacity of existing stormwater systems to accommodate a 1 in 10-year design solution, informed by future climate change scenarios, contributes to enhanced resilience against more frequent and intense storm events, thereby reducing flood risk and associated damages. • Incorporating initial cleaning and the improvement of infiltration systems enhances the capacity of the urban drainage system to manage stormwater effectively. This contributes to increased resilience against flooding during heavy precipitation events. • River flood protection infrastructure will significantly reduce flood damage and interruption of economic and livelihood activities. It will improve residents' access to schools, health centers and public facilities. Flood protection infrastructures will also safeguard the projects urban investments, in addition to the population living flood hot spot areas 95. • Nature-based Solution will improve infiltration and may have added cooling effects, as well as support the integrity of natural infiltration systems. Slope stabilization measures near rivers and drains will reduce the risk of erosion and potential landslides. • Drainage and flood risk studies for the three cities will inform the design of climate-resilient drainage infrastructure, improving stormwater management, and reducing flood risk. • Ancillary work on adjacent infrastructure will ensure a flood resilient design and flood risk reduction of the wider city area.
