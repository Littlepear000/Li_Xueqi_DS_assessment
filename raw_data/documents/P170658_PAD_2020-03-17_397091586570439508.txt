Page 6 of 77 The World Bank CENTRALIZED EMERGENCY RESPONSE SYSTEM PROJECT (P170658) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Peru is an upper-middle-income country with remarkable economic growth over the last decade. Despite somewhat slower economic activity in the past couple of years, Peru has been one of the fastest growing economies in the Latin America and the Caribbean region in the last two decades. Between 2002 and 2018, real gross domestic product (GDP) grew at an average rate of 5.3 percent, far exceeding the 2.7 percent average growth rate for the region. Such a high rate of growth enabled Peru to double its per capita income and reach upper-middle-income status in 2008. Macroeconomic stability coupled with open financial and capital markets and a sustained policy of trade liberalization amid a favorable external environment for commodity exporters were the key factors behind Peru’s success. However, after expanding 4 percent in 2018, economic growth decelerated to 2.6 percent in 2019. 1 2. Peru made substantial gains in poverty reduction since the 2000s. Prosperity was widely shared, as growth in services, agriculture, and mining, coupled with well-targeted social policies, boosted income growth among the bottom 40 percent. Poverty decreased from 21.7 percent in 2017 to 20.5 percent in 2018, recovering after increasing in 2017 with faster decreasing rates in rural areas and stagnating poverty levels in Lima. Projections based on the upper-middle-income country poverty line of US$5.5 per day (2011 purchasing power parity) show that the poverty rate is expected to fall by about 1.5 percentage points between 2018 and 2020. 3. Peru’s geography, natural endowments, and diverse population have shaped its unbalanced economic development and service delivery based on capital-intensive growth that resulted in spatially unbalanced outcomes for the population. The population in the Lima Metropolitan area and Callao reached 10,479,899 in 2017, accounting for one-third of the total population of the country. 2 The high cost of service delivery and digital and physical connectivity in the vast Sierra and Selva regions is the result of an uneven spatial and economic development pattern with concentration in Lima and the coastal region. 4. Peru is a country highly vulnerable to multiple natural hazards that affect its development, especially its efforts to reduce poverty and promote inclusive growth. Peru is in a region of major seismic activity, with as many as 200 minor earthquakes occurring on average each year. Climate change impacts in Peru include increasing temperatures and extreme precipitation, as well as more frequent and severe occurrences of natural disasters, including flooding and droughts linked to the recurrent ‘El Niño’ and ‘El Niño Costero’ phenomena. 5. Gender-based violence (GBV) in Peru is at one of the highest levels in Latin America and is present in all regions and across all socioeconomic strata. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 6. There are three main First Response Units in Peru with competencies to respond in case of an emergency: (a) the National Police of Peru (NPP) under the Ministry of Interior (MoI); (b) the Mobile Emergency Care System (Sistema de Atención Móvil de Urgencia, SAMU: health care provider) under the Ministry of Health (MoH); and (c) the Peruvian Corps of Voluntary Firefighters (PCVF) under the MoI. The proposed project will seek to integrate into the 911 system the three key emergency response numbers (for NPP, PCVF, and SAMU) as they are the ones receiving approximately 90 percent of the calls. The NPP responds mainly to emergencies and urgencies5 related to (a) harassments, (b) traffic accidents, (c) thefts, and (d) fires. SAMU responds mainly to emergencies and urgencies related to (a) labor emergencies, (b) traffic accidents, (c) burns and head injuries, and (d) loss of consciousness. According to the PCVF’s data, medical emergencies and urgencies account for 50.7 percent of the emergencies and urgencies responded to—fires account for 12.3 percent, car accidents for 13.7 percent, rescues for 3.8 percent, and other incidents (such as gas leakages, chemical spills, and special services, among others) for the remaining 19.5 percent. 7. Each First Response Unit has a different emergency response number and their own communications systems and infrastructure to respond to emergencies and urgencies. This creates confusion among the citizens regarding which is the most suitable number to call for a given emergency, reduces coordination among entities involved, and duplicates response efforts to an emergency or urgency. In addition, the lack of a unique number leads to delays in answering the call and delivering an efficient emergency service response. 8. The average response time for the NPP is very slow. The NPP receives 77.36 percent of the emergency and urgency calls. The average response time—defined as the time between responding to an emergency or urgency call and arriving at the place of the incident—in the Lima Metropolitan area and Callao is 45 minutes. This time is much higher than the average in other countries in Latin America and the Caribbean such as Ecuador, where the average response time is less than 13 minutes.6 A response to an emergency or urgency call encompasses a first- and second-line response. In parallel to the NPP, SAMU-health, and the PCVF, the MoWVP operates a free GBV hotline (Line 100) dedicated to orientation, information, counseling, and emotional support for people affected by domestic violence and sexual assault or who are aware of a situation of abuse in their environment. According to the social assessment of the project carried out between June and September 2019, women are the primary users of Line 100. The operation of the line has several challenges, including (a) long wait for the operator to pick up the calls; (b) lack of procedure protocols and lack of coordination protocols with other emergency response entities such as the police; (c) high percentage of malicious calls (79 percent); (d) lack of coordination with GBV service providers for case referrals; (e) lack of updated software and interoperable platforms; (f) lack of adequate equipment; (g) lack of data analysis on the GBV cases to improve GBV prevention and response, among others; and (h) lack of knowledge among GBV survivors of the existence of Line 100, with approximately 15 percent of GBV survivors not knowing where to seek help.15 16. In early 2019, the Ministry of Transport and Communications (MTC) was given the urgent mandate to integrate the existing emergency response numbers into a well-coordinated and integrated system. Within that context, the MTC requested World Bank Group support to establish the proposed integrated 911 Emergency Response System and delegated implementation to PRONATEL, a government agency housed in the MTC. The proposed project will seek to integrate the three key emergency response numbers mentioned above (NPP, PCVF, and SAMU) as they are the ones receiving approximately 90 percent of the calls and will progressively include the remaining emergency numbers as described in annex 2. The existing emergency response numbers (105, 106, and 116) will be phased out gradually, and all calls made to the First Response Units will be automatically transferred to the 911 system. Lines 100 and 911 will coexist, and all calls made to Line 100 will access the 911 system to benefit from receiving (a) alerts of previous malicious calls, (b) automatic information of previous emergency calls, and (c) the geolocation of calls. The GoP’s National Plan identifies public security as a strategic area. By maximizing finance for development through private sector investments and participation in the sustainable and efficient operation and maintenance (O&M) of the 911 Emergency Response System and subsequently paving the way for a private sector-based expansion of the system to the rest of the country, the proposed project would contribute to Objective 3 of CPF Pillar I (Enhance the environment for sustainable private sector investments). Following international best practices, PRONATEL, the implementing agency, will outsource the O&M of the 911 Platform and the operation of the service to be provided by the 911 operators to one or two national or international firms. By improving access to emergency medical services, particularly benefitting the lower income groups and women in the Lima Metropolitan area and Callao, the proposed project would also support Objective 5 of CPF Pillar II (Modernize delivery of health and nutrition services for the poor). Finally, the emphasis on disaster risk management and emergency response is in line with the focus of CPF Pillar III (Natural resources and climate change risk management). The proposed project will support the adaptation to climate change through improved planning, coordination for, and in response to natural, climate, and anthropic hazards and other emergencies. This will be done by (a) establishing an integrated communications platform, (b) adopting specific and well-coordinated emergency response protocols and procedures, (c) promoting the linking of the recently implemented Early Warning Messaging System ( Sistema de Mensajería de Alerta Temprana de Emergencia, SISMATE) with the 911 system, and (d) providing much-needed capacity building. Following international best practices, the O&M of the 911 Center will be outsourced. Given the limited experience in Peru, and more specifically within PRONATEL, in the implementation of this type of service, the PIU will contract national or international private firms to (a) operate and maintain the 911 Platform and (b) operate, manage, and handle the 911 emergency response calls. The MTC will continuously supervise the 911 service and establish the necessary mechanisms to ensure the proper operation of the 911 Center. 61. The O&M cost of the 911 emergency and urgency response service will be financed with MTC’s own funds. The MTC will contract a private firm that will be in charge of the O&M, including the staff. According to the regulatory framework in Peru, these costs cannot be covered by external financing. However, given the importance of these contracts for the sustainability of the project, the World Bank, through technical assistance under Component 6 and project implementation support, will support the PIU during the design of the technical specifications for contracting the firm or firms that will operate and maintain the 911 Platform. The project design includes measures to better respond to GBV emergencies in the Lima Metropolitan area and Callao. These measures were identified during preparation based on an assessment of the main challenges of Line 100. Line 100 receives 104,207 calls per month, out of which 79 percent are malicious. This leads to long response times to the survivors seeking help. Furthermore, as stated earlier, only 15 percent of the survivors know where to seek help and around 40 percent think reporting is not useful. The following activities will improve the delivery of services offered by Line 100, as well as a more coordinated and efficient response in case of GBV emergencies or urgencies: (a) the integration of the Line 100 digital system to the 911 Platform, aiming at reducing malicious calls and response times; (b) the elaboration of a specific protocol on how to respond to GBV emergencies, which will improve coordination between all First Response Units in the event of an emergency deriving from GBV; and (c) capacity building of the 911 operators and first emergency response units on GBV emergencies management (including psychosocial support services, guidance, and counseling). 65. The proposed project is economically viable and expected to improve emergency and urgency services in the Lima Metropolitan area and Callao, by reducing the response time. The economic analysis was carried out using a Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) framework to assess the benefits to be derived from the centralized emergency system in Peru between 2020 and 2030. The analysis calculated the net present value (NPV) of estimated costs and benefits and the economic internal rate of return (EIRR). Economic costs include (a) capital cost during the design and construction of the 911 Center, (b) the design and Page 26 of 77 The World Bank CENTRALIZED EMERGENCY RESPONSE SYSTEM PROJECT (P170658) deployment of all the digital components, (c) the cost of the design and implementation of the procedural protocols, (d) capacity-building and awareness-building activities, and (e) O&M costs during the operational phase. The main project’s quantifiable economic benefit is the savings in health care costs due to reduction in response time. The proposed project is also expected to yield other non-quantified economic benefits such as lives saved from life-threatening situations and reduced property damage. Under a conservative approach, the project shows an NPV of US$16.29 million at a 6 percent discount rate and an EIRR of 22.39 percent over a 10-year evaluation horizon, and a cost-benefit ratio of 1.21. The World Bank has considered additional scenarios based on external factors such as an increase of traffic and its impacts on NPV and EIRR, as detailed in annex 5. 67. The improved emergency and urgency response service will reduce health costs for the GoP. Based on international studies,33 a health saving cost of US$2 per person per minute in the response time to emergency and urgency situations is assumed. With an estimated average number of people assisted by the emergency response services of 950,000, due to the improvement of the service, the total cost savings are estimated to be, on average, over the time horizon 2020–2030, US$13 million per year. The project will be housed in PRONATEL, in line with its mandate to manage the centralized emergency response system. Legislative Decree 1277,34 which sanctions the execution of malicious communications to the emergency or information centers, establishes that the Vice Ministry of Communications is the responsible entity in charge of gradually implementing and operating the integrated communications system through a unique emergency number. The Vice Ministry of Communications has issued a memorandum which assigns project implementation to PRONATEL. PRONATEL will prepare an annual work plan and act as the main coordinating body and convening authority among the project stakeholders. PRONATEL will systematically monitor and evaluate project implementation progress, as well as identify and address implementation shortcomings, challenges, and possible disputes. 3. The PIU will be formally established under PRONATEL’s Studies and Projects Directorate and will be created through a legal resolution from PRONATEL’s Executive Director. The PIU will coordinate the technical, logistical, implementation, and M&E aspects of the project. The Executive Director will appoint a dedicated team from the staff already working at PRONATEL’s Studies and Projects Directorate, with the qualified competencies for implementation of the project. It will include the following staff: (a) a project coordinator, (b) an architect or civil engineer, (c) a telecommunications engineer, (d) an environmental specialist, (e) a social specialist, (f) a contract manager, and (g) an M&E specialist. In addition, for all the fiduciary aspects of the project the PIU will be supported by PRONATEL’s Administrative Office, as well as by at least a (a) procurement specialist, an (b) FM specialist, and (c) an accountant. 4. The SC will meet once every three months to monitor implementation progress, evaluate recommendations and requests that have policy and institutional implications, provide guidance, identify project issues, and discuss and agree on corrective measures. The 911 Center will include the following areas needed for the delivery of the 911 emergency and urgency response service: (a) Area for senior executives and managers. This area will be approximately 159.52 m2 and will include (i) manager’s office, (ii) assistants’ offices, (iii) meeting rooms, and (iv) complementary services. In addition, a total of 816.13 m 2 will be allocated to the manager of each area, such as operations, accounting, or human resources. (b) Operators’ room. Each operator will have a minimum work space area of 9 m2 (following International Organization for Standardization [ISO] standard 11064), and each operator will have approximately three monitors. The total area for this room is approximately 790 m 2. Page 59 of 77 The World Bank CENTRALIZED EMERGENCY RESPONSE SYSTEM PROJECT (P170658) (c) Specialists’ room. This space will be occupied by highly trained emergency management staff from the NPP, PCVF, and SAMU units who possess extensive knowledge in critical emergency and urgency response. They will oversee the coordination measures needed for high-complexity emergencies or for those that need response from more than one specialized unit. The specialists’ room is similar to the operators’ room with an area of 116 m2 for 15 people. (d) Crisis room. This space will house various agencies from the 911 emergency response service and outside to coordinate efforts during a crisis situation. It will include a video wall to be able to view crisis incidents taking place. This room will be used for large-scale incidents that require close coordination and analysis and dissemination of emergency information to facilitate coordination and decision making. The access and dynamics of use of the crisis room must be protocolized and private access will be enforced. The room will have an area of at least 66 m2 for 30 people. (e) Data room and equipment. This is the room where the computer equipment and communications systems that are required for the operation of the 911 Platform will be housed. The average dimension for this kind of space is 66 m². It will house the necessary computer equipment and network systems and be equipped with energy, cooling, and communications systems appropriate for the proper functioning of a 911 emergency response service. Access to this room will be restricted exclusively to technical staff. The 911 Center will be connected through fiber-optic cable with two different physical paths, promoting redundancy and continuity of the service 24/7. (f) Meeting room. A meeting room will be set up to deal with the day-to-day issues of the service. (g) Training room. This is the area designed for the training of staff to promote the right use and efficient operation of the 911 Platform. The training room will have a maximum capacity of 30 people with an estimated area of 134.87 m2. 9. At least 80 percent of the space of the 911 Center will be dedicated to the main and critical areas described above. The final design of the 911 Center will include flexible spaces that are adaptable to demographic, functional, hierarchical, and technological aspects of organizations.
