Program Strategic Context 1. The Government of Mauritania (GoM) has prepared the Learning Support Priority Plan1 (PPSA) aimed at improving the quality of preprimary and primary education and increasing access to secondary education, particularly for girls and children in rural areas, as well as in communities affected by forced displacement. This operation will directly support the PPSA in accelerating the transformation of the education system while addressing growing service delivery pressures on an already strained system. 2. These delivery pressures are largely driven by demographic growth, persistent system constraints, and sustained refugee inflows resulting from the deteriorating security situation across the Sahel. In December 2025, the GoM adopted a National Strategy for Peace Consolidation and Social Cohesion2 (SNCPCS), reflecting a broader commitment to a humanitarian-development-peace nexus approach to managing displacement. By early 2026, Mauritania was hosting over 300,000 refugees and asylum-seekers (representing approximately 6 percent of the total population)—predominantly in southeastern border regions such as Hodh El Charqi and Guidimakha, and increasingly present in urban areas such as Nouakchott and Nouadhibou—making it one of the largest refugee-hosting countries in West Africa3. The continued deterioration of security conditions in neighboring Sahel countries in 2024 and 2025 continued to drive new arrivals in the second half of 2025, the majority being women, children, and unaccompanied minors, significantly increasing demand for social services. 3. The GoM has adopted a refugee protection framework to address these challenges. Based on the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR)’s Refugee Protection Assessment update for July–December 20254, refugees continue to access core national services, including documentation, alongside ongoing efforts to strengthen asylum governance, notably through improved documentation processes, the development of a national asylum law, and increased inclusion in national systems. The World Bank, following consultation with UNHCR, confirms that the protection framework for refugees continues to be adequate in Mauritania. Nonetheless, significant pressures remain—the influx has placed considerable strain on health, education, and other social services in already underserved hosting areas, while fueling tensions between refugee and host communities and leaving key challenges unresolved, including documentation backlogs and substantial protection risks. 4. The Mauritania Improving Education Quality, Results, and Access for All Operation (IQRAA)—financed by the International Development Association (IDA) in the amount of US$40 million (including US$25 million from the Window for Host Communities and Refugees [WHR]) and the Global Partnership for Education (GPE) in the amount of US$17.535 million—will support the GoM in the delivery of results under its program. The operation will directly contribute to the objective of the new Country Partnership Framework (CPF)6 (FY2026-FY2030) under Outcome 1 for more and better jobs. The operation is fully aligned with the Sahel Education White Paper’s7 recommendations and aims to reduce learning poverty and improve girls’ education. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. Mauritania's growing young population presents both a challenge and an opportunity for its education system. With 41 percent of the population under the age of 15 as of 2024, the demand for schooling is increasing rapidly. Between 2013 and 2024, the school-age population (3–18 years) grew by 17.5 percent13, further pressuring the education system. Coupled with this demographic pressure, Mauritania’s Human Capital Index (HCI) of 0.3814 remains one of the lowest globally, reflecting persistent gaps in learning quality and access. Children entering school at age four can expect 7.7 years of schooling, but when adjusted for learning quality, this translates to only 4.2 effective years, highlighting a learning loss of 3.5 years. Without effective interventions, many young Mauritanians will struggle to acquire the foundational skills needed for employment and economic participation, further exacerbating long-term inequalities. 9. Persistent challenges in education: quality remains a key hurdle 10. Standing at 94 percent in 2019, learning poverty is a major challenge in Mauritania. Both supply- and demandside factors contribute to this learning crisis. On the supply side, key issues include inadequate school readiness, lack of teacher qualifications and effectiveness, high absenteeism, limited availability of adequate pedagogical inputs, and the language of instruction, as highlighted in the 2022 West Africa Mapping Learning Poverty report15 and confirmed by the Education Service Delivery Indicators (SDI 2024) survey. On the demand side, household income and parental education significantly influence enrollment and learning outcomes. In addition, the lack of accessible schools within reasonable distance exacerbates disparities in educational opportunities. Addressing these systemic challenges is essential to improving learning and equipping children with the requisite knowledge and skills to reach their full potential. 11. One of the most critical factors contributing to learning poverty in Mauritania is the lack of school readiness due to limited access to preprimary education. Early Childhood Education (ECE) is essential for building foundational cognitive and socio-emotional skills. However, in Mauritania, less than 10 percent16 of children have access to structured preprimary programs—one of the lowest rates in West and Central Africa—leaving most children unprepared for primary school. Without adequate exposure to quality early learning, many children start school with significant developmental gaps, making it harder for them to acquire basic literacy and numeracy skills. In 2020, the average Gross Enrollment Rate (GER) for preprimary education in Sub-Saharan Africa stood at 28 percent17, highlighting the stark disparity and the need for targeted efforts to expand quality ECE in Mauritania. 12. Compounding this challenge is the lack of adequately qualified teachers, particularly in core subjects such as Arabic, French, and Mathematics. Significant efforts have been made related to both the flow and existing stock of teachers who often lack essential subject knowledge and pedagogical skills. Studies indicate that many teachers do not meet national competency standards. The latest teacher evaluation in 202118 highlighted this issue, revealing that 34 percent of Arabic speaking teachers struggle with Arabic and 57 percent with mathematics; 52 percent of French speaking teachers are unable to teach in French and 41 percent of them struggle with mathematics. The SDI19 survey revealed that no teachers met the required standard in French, only 6.6 percent in Arabic, and 11.2 percent in mathematics, with 23 percent absent from classrooms. To improve the flow of teachers, reforms in Teacher Training Institutions (Ecole Normale d’Instituteurs, ENI), including the implementation of performance-based contracts, led to higher success rates among new ENI graduates in 2022 compared to previous cohorts. For the stock of teachers, various teacher training methods have been tested, some yielding positive outcomes while also highlighting challenges in sustainability and scalability. Drawing from these experiences and considering the country's geographical expanse, a new multisectoral hybrid model is being developed by the GoM to create a more equitable, efficient, and sustainable system for teacher professional development. While Mauritania is on track to achieve universal access to primary education, transition from the primary to the upper cycles remains a significant bottleneck. Mauritania has expanded education access over the past decade, with the GER at the primary level increasing from 95 percent in 2010 to 127 percent in 202320, and with girls participating at a slightly higher rate than boys, 128 and 126 percent, respectively. However, the 2023 Education Sector Diagnostic 21 shows that only 61 percent of primary school graduates progress to lower secondary education, and 35 percent to upper secondary education. Despite notable progress made in access to lower secondary education (collège) and upper secondary education (lycée), where the GER rose, respectively, from 24 to 48 percent and from 14 to 29 percent between 2010 and 202322, the low transition rates—rooted in poor learning at primary and coupled with enrollment capacity constraints—continue to impede government efforts to achieve its stated goal of ensuring that 39 percent of girls have access to upper secondary by 2030. Mauritania’s overall secondary school completion rate is relatively low at 32 percent23, trailing behind Senegal (36 percent) and Côte d’Ivoire (59 percent). Despite recent progress, access to secondary education remains limited, highlighting the need for continued efforts to address barriers, particularly for girls. 14. Gender parity in education has improved in Mauritania, but significant disparities persist in learning outcomes, retention, and completion rates. While girls have achieved equal access to lower secondary education since 2019, they continue to record lower test scores and completion rates at the upper secondary level (25 percent for girls compared to 30 percent for boys24). Many students drop out of school early due to economic constraints, distance to school, early marriage, and cultural expectations. Additionally, boys are increasingly disengaging from formal schooling, with lower primary completion rates and higher dropout rates in secondary education. Many boys opt for Quranic education (44 percent compared to 29 percent for girls25) or enter the workforce early (12 percent compared to 3 percent for girls), limiting their future employment prospects. A major challenge for both genders is underperformance in Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) education, where cultural biases, lack of exposure, and inadequate teacher training hinder girls, while boys’ disengagement from schooling exacerbates the issue. Overcoming governance hurdles: enhancing data-driven decision making, stakeholder engagement, and coordination 15. Mauritania’s education system faces three critical governance challenges: ineffective infrastructure planning, weak community participation, and lack of coordination among stakeholders. The Education Management Information System (EMIS, Système d'Information de Gestion de l'Éducation, renamed SIRAGE), deployed through the World Bankfinanced Basic Education Sector Support Project (P163143), is an important tool for managing the education system and informing data-driven decision-making. To date, 58 percent of schools remain incomplete,26 offering only two to four classrooms instead of the required six and lacking essential amenities such as water, sanitation, and electricity—severely limiting learning conditions and student retention. SIRAGE presents a valuable opportunity to address this issue, particularly by leveraging its data to revive school mapping (Carte Scolaire) and ensuring that infrastructure investments prioritize the completion of existing schools and the efficient deployment of teachers. Moreover, community involvement in school governance remains weak, despite the establishment of School Management Committees (SMCs) (Comite de Gestion des Etablissements Scolaires, COGES) in a select number of public primary schools nationwide (74027 out of 4,000), leaving most schools without structured local engagement in decision-making and oversight. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Program Design 43. The proposed operation is well informed by various analytical works on the critical need to invest in quality preprimary, basic, and upper secondary education, including the recent RESEN 2023. As detailed below, the operation incorporates key lessons learned from the ongoing education project in Mauritania, the Basic Education Sector Support Project (P163143), and builds upon the World Bank’s global experience in the implementation of PforR operations. 44. Focus and simplicity of Program design are essential to its effective implementation. As this is the first PforR operation for the education sector in Mauritania, it is crucial that the Program be designed with simplicity in mind, featuring a well-articulated and clearly defined PrDO. The Program is strategically focused on critical RAs, targeting highimpact activities that ensure the allocated funds benefit a large number of beneficiaries. By selecting specific subsectors, the Program aims to foster mutually supportive and interconnected RAs. The operation supports a number of critical, interrelated activities such as teacher training, curriculum, and data-driven decision-making, which are expected to positively affect learning outcomes and overall sector performance. These areas are centered on achieving specific milestones and conditions that are essential for enhancing education service delivery and outcomes. Experience from education operations implemented in refugee and displacement-affected contexts underscores the importance of maintaining realistic ambitions and carefully sequencing reforms, particularly where integration into national systems is required. In such contexts, progress depends not only on expanding access but also on addressing transition challenges related to curriculum alignment, language of instruction, and interrupted schooling. 45. Successful implementation of construction activities requires context-specific measures. In a vast territory such as Mauritania, where institutional capacity is limited, the timely completion of construction programs requires specific strategies. The Basic Education Sector Support Project (Projet d'Appui au Secteur de l'Éducation de Base, PASEB, P163143) has revealed significant delays in classroom construction activities, largely due to inefficiencies in procurement processes, the limited capabilities of construction firms and contractors, inadequate on-site monitoring, and ineffective quality control mechanisms. Despite these persistent challenges, the availability of quality school infrastructure remains a critical need for the sector. The GoM is committed to expanding school capacity and enhancing school construction quality. In this context, the PforR approach, which ties financing to tangible outcomes in quality and timely delivery of construction activities, emerges as a highly adequate route. Lessons learned from the Côte d'Ivoire Strengthening Basic Education System Operation (P177800) have informed the sequencing of yearly DLRs for secondary schools’ construction. 46. Strong government buy-in is crucial for the sustainability of any program. Despite some initial resistance to change, the GoM and key stakeholders have embraced the scripted lessons approach following the successful implementation of its pilot phase. To enhance the teaching and learning experience and support the nationwide scale-up of this approach, it is essential to improve student preparedness. This improvement can be achieved by expanding access to quality preprimary education, laying a solid foundation for learning. By combining the expansion of preprimary with the scaling-up of scripted lessons, the Program is expected to enhance basic literacy and numeracy skills among students. 47. Effective teachers are critical to learning, and incentives are key. Encouraging teachers to engage in continuous training can be challenging without sustainable incentives. To address this, a strategic approach involves implementing a continuous training digital platform, as successfully established by the ongoing Basic Education Sector Support Project (P163143). This platform facilitates ongoing professional development for teachers, making training more accessible and engaging. Additionally, leveraging teachers' participation and proficiency as DLIs can motivate the GoM to develop effective and sustainable incentive measures. This approach not only enhances teachers' skills but also ensures long-term commitment to professional growth, ultimately leading to improved educational outcomes. 48. Evidence-based decision-making is essential for ensuring strong education sector management and maximizing development impacts. The Basic Education Sector Support Project (P163143) has supported the establishment of a strong management system, known as SIRAGE, which the Government is committed to using as a singular management tool for Page 17 Official Use Only The World Bank Mauritania Improving Education Quality, Results, and Access for All Operation (IQRAA, P509243) the sector. Yet, quality yearbooks have not been produced effectively. The Program has incorporated the timely production of yearbooks as a DLI, emphasizing the importance of leveraging SIRAGE capabilities. This strategic action aims to ensure that SIRAGE is effectively utilized and that necessary data are collected timely, thereby reinforcing the system's capacity to support informed decision-making. This will further allow the full operationalization of SIRAGE while ensuring Government ownership, building on lessons learned from the ongoing Basic Education Support Project (P163143). III. The Program will ensure that both girls’ and boys’ needs are met at newly constructed secondary schools through gender-sensitive school infrastructure (separate sanitation facilities, safe spaces) to encourage girls to stay in school. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the World Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). Complaints may be submitted at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the World Bank's attention, and World Bank Management has been given an opportunity to respond. KEY RISKS 83. The overall risk of the Program is Substantial, consistent with the ratings for political and governance, macroeconomic, institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability, fiduciary, E&S, and other risks related to refugee inclusion, displacement and insecurity. 84. Political and governance. The Program faces substantial political and governance risks, particularly resistance to change within the administrative layers of the education and finance sectors. Despite strong political commitment to transition to performance-based management by 2026, with education being one of the selected pilot social sectors along with health and social protection, residual risks remain in adapting new budgeting and management practices. To mitigate these risks, the program includes an IPF component that provides essential TA for enhancing sectoral budget planning and execution. This component will provide capacity-building and operational support, including the development of a comprehensive M&E framework and the institutionalization of the PCU. By fostering stakeholder engagement and intersectoral coordination, the IPF component aims to address governance barriers and facilitate a smooth transition, ultimately supporting sustainable improvements in the education sector. 85. Macroeconomic. The Program faces substantial macroeconomic risks that could affect implementation. The government's ambitious education agenda, as translated into the PPSA, may face challenges from competing spending priorities in a resource-constrained fiscal space. The Program is designed to focus on education sector priorities in the highest Return on Investment (RoI) activities, rationalizing the public investment portfolio within the sector to ensure more effective allocation and utilization of resources. Despite these strategic efforts, residual risks persist, particularly concerning the potential for ongoing fiscal constraints and climate-induced challenges to disrupt the program's financing. The success of the Program in maintaining focus on high RoI activities is dependent on the government's ability to navigate competing spending priorities and manage fiscal pressures effectively. These prioritization efforts will need strong and sustained policy dialogue with the government to collectively bolster the Program's resilience against macroeconomic fluctuations, ensuring its sustainability and effectiveness in improving the education sector. 86. Institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability. The Program requires substantial reforms that demand strong institutional capacity and coordination for effective implementation and sustainability. However, the Government faces challenges in managing complex initiatives due to limited capacity, including difficulties in coordinating across ministries and IEs involved. Additionally, there are constraints in terms of skilled human resources, both in quantity and quality. This is further compounded by the first use of the PforR instrument in Mauritania's education sector, introducing additional fiduciary and results management requirements, while historical project performance in Mauritania has often shown delays and implementation issues, raising concerns about meeting the Program's objectives within the stipulated time frame. To mitigate these risks, the Program will strengthen institutional capacity through TA and capacitybuilding measures to enhance the skills and knowledge of IEs. The IVA recruitment will be streamlined before effectiveness to prevent implementation delays. Clear governance structures will be established to facilitate effective coordination, and a dedicated PCU will be established to ease communication and management processes. These measures aim to bolster institutional capacity, improve coordination, and ensure the Program's success. 87. Fiduciary. The Program faces several fiduciary risks that require careful management to ensure successful implementation. A primary concern is the timely availability of funds to IEs, which can be mitigated through the provision of advances to ensure smooth financial operations. Additionally, delays in aligning Procurement Plans with the budget may disrupt Program timelines and efficiency. The Program also grapples with limited familiarity with the PforR instrument Page 27 Official Use Only The World Bank Mauritania Improving Education Quality, Results, and Access for All Operation (IQRAA, P509243) among fiduciary staff in IEs posing a challenge to effective and timely Program execution. To address these risks, a comprehensive fiduciary systems assessment will be conducted to evaluate the residual risks and develop targeted mitigation measures supported by the IPF component, thereby strengthening fiduciary systems and enhancing the Program's capacity to achieve its objectives efficiently and effectively. 88. Environmental and social. The Program faces substantial E&S risks that will require management to ensure effective implementation. The primary risks relate to small-scale school construction and rehabilitation activities, which may generate temporary environmental impacts such as dust, noise, waste, and limited land disturbance, as well as social risks linked to labor and community health and safety. Additional concerns include possible land access issues and the risk of exclusion of vulnerable groups, particularly girls, children with disabilities, and refugee populations. 89. Other - Refugee inclusion, displacement, and insecurity. Refugee children in Mauritania face specific risks affecting enrollment, retention, and progression, including interrupted schooling, differences in curricula and language of instruction, limited documentation, and heightened vulnerability linked to displacement. These challenges are compounded in refugee-hosting communities by overcrowded classrooms, teacher shortages, and constrained service delivery, which also affect host-community students. The continued deterioration of the security situation in Central Sahel, with direct spillovers in Mauritania, increases uncertainty and insecurity risk in the Hodh El Charqi and further intensifies pressures on education services. To mitigate these risks, the Program prioritizes integration into the national education system rather than parallel provision, the most sustainable arrangement for refugee children, as enrollment in public schools ensures continuity of access beyond the life of the operation. Accordingly, the Program adopts a phased approach to scaling enrollment results, anchored in system readiness measures. To address language barriers specifically, the Program will incentivize the recruitment and deployment of Arabic-speaking teachers to support refugee children's pedagogical transition to the Mauritanian curriculum, with appointments formalized under MERSE administrative procedures. More broadly, by strengthening education service delivery in refugee-hosting communities, the Program aims to reduce exclusion risks for refugee children while preserving equitable access and quality for host-community learners. CONTRIBUTIONS TO CLIMATE CHANGE ADAPTATION AND MITIGATION Mauritania faces severe and escalating climate risks, including floods, droughts, heatwaves, and desertification, that threaten inclusive growth, disrupt education, and damage infrastructure. Nearly 80 percent of Nouakchott is at risk of inundation within 20 years, temperatures are projected to rise by +2.1°C by 2050, and annual gross domestic product could decline by 3.4 to 7.2 percent under various climate scenarios. In 2022 alone, droughts and flash floods affected 800,000 people, with disproportionate impacts on girls and vulnerable children in rural and peri-urban areas. In response, the Program mainstreams climate adaptation and mitigation across all levels of the education system, through infrastructure, pedagogy, data systems, and community engagement, to ensure school safety, education continuity, and the empowerment of the next generation with the skills to navigate environmental challenges, while contributing to Mauritania's NDC.
