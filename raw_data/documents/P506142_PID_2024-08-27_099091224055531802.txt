Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Brazil’s economic activity continued to recover after negative Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth in 2014-2019 and the drastic impact of COVID-19. GDP grew 2.9 percent in 2023, after having achieved the rates of 4.8 percent in 2021 and 3.0 percent in 2022, on the back of robust private consumption, still supported by a strong labor market, fiscal stimulus to social transfers, declining inflation, and the monetary policy easing. A record grain harvest and a favorable external environment also boosted exports in 2023. With economic activity slowing since the second half of 2023 and 2023’s unusually high agricultural output not being matched in 2024, GDP growth is expected to moderate to 2.0 percent in 2024. Medium-term growth projections remain at around 2 percent per year, constrained by structural factors limiting Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth in services and industry sectors. The successful implementation of growth-enhancing structural reforms, including those related to trade openness, market competition, and the business environment could provide a boost to potential growth. 2. In 2023, the poverty rate (US$ 6.85 per day) fell again to 21.3 percent, linked to improvements in economic conditions and social protection policies. Unemployment reached 7.1 percent, the lowest since 2014, reaching women, Page 2 The World Bank Santa Catarina Rural Development Project for Sustainability and Innovation (P506142) youth, and Afro-Brazilians. The Bolsa Familia Program1 helped reduce poverty: coverage expanded by 2 million families, reaching 21.3 million, with the average monthly transfer increasing from R$ 394.48 to R$ 670.36. Finally, the real minimum wage increased by 2.8 percent, boosting the incomes of about 24.5 percent of the households in the bottom 40 percent with at least one formal worker. With sustained job growth, the poverty rate is projected to further decrease to 20.5 percent by 2026. 3. Brazil will experience rapid change in climate conditions and its impacts will vary spatially. By the end of the century, the average temperature in Brazil is expected to rise, depending on the trajectory of global Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions, by 1.7 °C to 5.3 °C from the 1986–2005 average.2 All of Brazil’s biomes are vulnerable to the impact of changes in temperature and rainfall, though with significant regional differences.3 In rural areas, more frequent and severe climate events, such as droughts and floods, are likely to cause major impacts on agriculture productivity, logistics (i.e., worsening conditions of rural roads) and delivery of water and sanitation services due to dwindling water supply. 4. The Santa Catarina GDP saw a significant recovery of 6.8 percent in 2021 after the impact of the pandemic, reaching R$ 428.6 billion, the sixth largest in the country. The GDP per capita of R$ 58,401 was the third highest, marking the best placement in the historical series. In 2022, a GDP growth of 2.8 percent for the State was estimated, and projections for 2023 indicate a growth of 3.7 percent. In 2023, following the record value of the previous year, Santa Catarina exports reached US$ 11.578 billion, or 3.4 percent of the national total. The Santa Catarina strategic location and competitive tariff and port competitiveness position the State as the second-largest importer in Brazil, with 11.9 percent of the total in 2023, or US$ 28.771 billion.4 5. Despite the relevance and economic growth of Santa Catarina, inclusion remains a challenge, along with the development of public policies that improve the livelihoods of the most vulnerable populations. According to the Cadastro Unico5, in May 2023, 32 percent of the families registered in the Santa Catarina government system are in extreme poverty and are beneficiaries of the Bolsa Família Program. Of these, 15 percent (32,064 families) live in rural and fishing areas. 6. Climate risks are amongst the most important risks farmers of Santa Catarina have to face. 6 Climate risks in the State include drought, flooding, hail and frost that threaten rural and fishing lifestyles, agricultural sustainability, and the ability to meet rising demands for food, fibers, and biomass. The most immediate and tangible effects on Santa Catarina's production chains are related to erratic rainfall. Intense, prolonged droughts have negatively impacted water supply, leading to productivity losses, increased vulnerability to and outbreaks of pests and diseases, and higher production costs. Additionally, changes in rainfall patterns have resulted in more frequent short-duration, high-volume floods, causing crop damage, livestock and infrastructure losses, soil erosion and even deaths. Agriculture in Santa Catarina has the 10th highest added value in Brazil and contributes 6.7 percent of the value added by the local economy. The 2023 Value of Agricultural Production (VPA) reached USD 13.28 billion and in the last 10 years it has had an average real growth of more than 2.5 percent per year. Despite the great diversification of agriculture in Santa Catarina, almost 60 percent of VPA results from just four activities: swine, 20.2 percent; poultry, 16.4 percent; milk, 12.3 percent; and soybeans, 10.9 percent. In 2023, state agribusiness exports had the second-best performance in history, with US$7.49 billion exported, 65 percent of the total value of Santa Catarina's exports in 2023 and 4.5 percent of those exported by Brazilian agriculture. 9. In Santa Catarina, the productive structure of agriculture is characterized by the strength of family farming and the predominance of small rural properties. In the state, 183,000 agricultural establishments are registered, 78 percent of which are family farms, with a total area of 6.45 million hectares (ha) and an average area of 35.2 ha per establishment. The agrifood system employs more than 500,000 people.9 The State has a long history of collective action among farmers. As of 2017, 194 farmers organizations had been registered with a total of 15,759 associates (8 percent of farmers). Cooperatives represent 69 percent of farmers’ organizations and have on average 100 members.10 The World Bank-funded Microbacias projects (P006473 and P043869) supported in the 1990s through the 2000s, the consolidation and formalization of many cooperatives. 10. Despite its growth and contribution to the country and State economy, the agrifood system in Santa Catarina faces several challenges to ensure a more resilient and inclusive growth. 11. Challenge #1 – Delivery of last-mile agricultural public goods and services. The literature shows that agricultural public goods like rural infrastructure (rural roads, electrification, irrigation, information and communication technology, etc.) and services (land administration, food safety and agriculture health, agriculture innovation, etc.) are fundamental for addressing food security and sustainable development.11 The State of Santa Catarina invests only 0.7 percent of its agricultural GDP in agricultural public goods and services. This level is below the OECD average (more than 5 percent), and what was observed in São Paulo (1.2 percent). Of the total State’s investment in public goods in the sector, 24 percent corresponds to agricultural innovation (Research and Development, extension) and the rest is distributed in infrastructure, health inspection, control services, and others.12 Unlike many other States in Brazil, most farmers in the State receive some technical assistance and extension services. Between 55 and 60 percent of family farmers are attended by the State Company of Agricultural Research and Rural Extension (Empresa de Pesquisa Agropecuária e Extensão Rural de Santa Catarina – EPAGRI). Yet the State faces the need to maintain and strengthen these public services to the sector. The vast majority of rural communities are located in remote areas, leading to socioeconomic isolation due to deficient communication infrastructure for Internet access and mobile telephony. Challenge #2 - Building assets for an inclusive agrifood system. Many family farmers in the State still struggle with poverty, with household incomes falling below the USD 1.24 daily threshold. it is estimated that around 22 percent of family farmers are benefiting from the Bolsa Familia program.13 This struggle can be attributed, in part, to the lack of minimum asset endowment. There are also significant challenges in the inclusion of family farmers with limited land and impoverished rural populations, as well as Traditional People and Communities (TPCs). 20 percent (37,000) of farmers in Santa Catarina have farms primarily for subsistence production and there are 67,700 agricultural establishments (37 percent of the total) with less than 10 hectares.14 Current literature highlights the significance of minimum asset ownership for production.15 Also, recent research provides compelling evidence for the effectiveness of supporting rural households through various productive inclusion phases using a multifaceted "graduation approach" and "productive alliances�? programs.16 These approaches and programs, combining productive asset transfers, technical and business management assistance, and access to saving accounts, have demonstrably led to long-term sustained increases in income and consumption for participating family farmers, therefore, lifting them out of poverty. 13. Challenge #3 – Improving agrifood system sustainability. In Santa Catarina, the agricultural sector contributes around 36 percent of total emissions estimates in the state (System of Estimation of GHG Emissions - Sistema de Estimativa de Emissões de Gases Efeito Estufa- SEEG, 2021), a situation that places Santa Catarina in 15th place in the ranking of emissions by state with gross emissions of 59 MtCO2 equivalent per year. In addition, the use of outdated farm technologies and farming practices leads to soil degradation and water pollution. Farmers in the State also face several climate risks, such as drought, flooding, hail and frost whose occurrence is increasing with climate change. To increase farmers’ resilience in the face of these risks while mitigating the impact of agriculture on climate and the environment, a simultaneous approach is recommended: (a) promoting sustainable agriculture intensification of family farmers through the adoption of climate-smart agriculture (CSA)17 technologies and farming practices and (b) fostering the implementation of policies and programs mitigate impacts of the activity on the climate and environment, like improving the implementation of the Rural Environmental Cadaster (Cadastro Ambiental Rural - CAR), of Payments for Environmental Services (PSE)... 14. Challenge #4 - Agrifinancing for sustainable agrifood system development. In Santa Catarina, access to rural credit is higher than in the rest of Brazil18, but still quite low with 29 percent of farmers accessing some form of agrifinance. 19 Furthermore, the current available agrifinance instruments could be strengthened to provide incentives for the adoption of CSA practices and technologies, contributing towards improvement in agrifood productivity, environmental goals, and improved livelihoods. In Santa Catarina, access to subsidized credit is conditioned, like the federal level, to the farmer having a registered CAR. Challenge #5 – Enhancing women participation in agrifood system development. There are significant gender inequalities in family farming in Santa Catarina. Women run only 16.9 percent of the state family farms and their farms are significantly smaller than those run by men – the average area of female-run family farms corresponds to only 72.9 percent of the area of male-run family farms. There is also a relevant gender gap in terms of access to technical guidance. While 84 percent of female farmers and 90 percent of male farmers have access to technical information through different media, the female participation in technical meetings and seminars is very unbalanced: only 22 percent of female farmers participate in these events, while 36 percent of the male farmers do. Participation in cooperatives and community associations is also highly imbalanced by gender – a gap of 17 percent in terms of participation in cooperatives and 15 percent in community associations. Finally, data on access to credit and access to agricultural extension services also show huge gender gaps. Thus, data from the Center for Socioeconomy and Agricultural Planning from the Company of Agricultural Research and Rural Extension of Santa Catarina (Empresa de Pesquisa Agropecuária e Extensão Rural de Santa - EPAGRI) shows that, in 2023, female family farmers (a) obtained only 14.7 percent of rural credit contracts made in the state; and (b) contracted only 16.6 percent of the amounts financed. These gender gaps on access to land, knowledge- sharing networks, credit and social capital reverberate in relevant inequalities in terms of the relevance of the earnings from agricultural production, participation in the market between male and female family farmers in the state and income distribution. Hence, the share of female-run family farms dedicated to market production is significantly lower than male- run farms (a gap of 16 percentage points – 66 percent and 82 percent, respectively); the share of female family farmers whose income earned from agricultural production is greater than the family's other incomes is much smaller than that of male family farmers (25 percent and 37 percent, respectively); and female family farmers are overrepresented among family farmers with lower income levels – 57.2 percent of the farms they ran belong to this income group. D. Project Description 16. The proposed Project in Santa Catarina, which is phase 3 of the MPA, aims at increasing resilience of family farmers by lifting the bottlenecks that prevent their sustainable intensification and development. The Project will support family farmers in targeted areas to (a) improve the agrifood system sustainability, through the adoption of climate-smart Page 6 The World Bank Santa Catarina Rural Development Project for Sustainability and Innovation (P506142) agriculture (CSA) practices, sustainable management of natural resources and environmental regularization, (b) build minimum asset ownership for agrifood productivity, through productive assets transfers, access to finance and innovations, technical and managerial assistance, and (c) delivery of last-mile agriculture public goods and services by supporting farms connection to rural roads, on-farm electrification, digital connectivity, and access to information. The Project will also provide support to the State of Santa Catarina to integrate this approach into its agricultural policies and programs. 17. The Project will target areas where economic, environmental and inclusion challenges overlap and hinders sustainable development of the agrifood system. Economic indicators include poverty level, access to markets and agriculture public goods and services, numbers of people registered in the Cadastro Unico at municipality level, percent of agriculture in municipality’s economy; environmental indicators include frequency of droughts, most severe consequences of droughts, deforestation rates, existence of an Ecological Corridor; inclusion indicators include gender, identification as Traditional People and Communities (TPC). The selection of priority municipalities will be discussed with the Regional Development Councils at the beginning of the Project’s implementation. 18. The proposed Project is an Investment Project Financing (IPF) of US$120.0 million to be implemented over a six- year period. The total Project cost is estimated to be US$ 181.8 million, including US$30.00 million of counterpart funding from the State of Santa Catarina and US$ 31.8 million from beneficiaries. The Project will be implemented through 4 components: (a) Direct support to family farmers and TPCs to adopt CSA, (b) Strengthening agriculture public goods and services, (c) Scaling innovative agrifood policies and programs and project management; and (d) Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC). Component 1. Direct support to family farmers and TPC to adopt CSA (Total cost US$ 116 million, of which IBRD loan US$ 84.2 million and beneficiaries US$ 31.8 million). 19. This component's goal is to enhance socio-economic inclusion and climate resilience of family farmers and TPC by promoting CSA21 technologies and practices, supporting food and nutrition security, ensuring environmental compliance, increasing market access through the implementation of subprojects. Investments under this component will contribute to improve net carbon balances through emission reductions, will help protect native vegetation and restore degraded areas, increase carbon storage in soil and vegetation, and reduce on-farm GHG emissions. 20. The component has two main activities: (a) pre-investment activities such as identifying CSA opportunities and preparation of subprojects; and (b) co-financing CSA subprojects through matching grants. These subprojects focus on various improvements in farming systems with CSA and nutrition-smart agriculture22, agro-processing, and environmental recovery efforts, support to meet legal sanitary requirements, marketing and logistics, access to energy23, and digital connectivity. The component prioritizes inclusion, benefiting TPC, women, and youth, and requires all projects to incorporate climate-smart practices. Proposed subprojects will be disclosed and revised by the Municipal and Regional Rural Development Committees. Environmental and Social risk is rated Moderate. The risk rating determination of the Project has considered: (a) the magnitude and complexity of the works to be supported by the Project – these risks and impacts are predictable and expected to be temporary and reversible, low in magnitude, site-specific and with low probability of serious adverse effects to the environment and human health; (b) the regulatory and institutional framework in which they will be conducted and the prior experience and demonstrated capacity of the implementing agencies to manage environmental and social risks; and, (c) their positive track-record in implementing World Bank-funded projects under the safeguard operational policies. Technical Assistance activities included in the Project - technical support to farmers, infrastructure analysis, and institutional capacity support – are not expected to result in materially significant environmental and social downstream effects. 27. The Borrower is developing proportionate environmental and social risk management tools to address these risks. These instruments include (a) an Environmental and Social Management Framework, which will include inter alia E&S (i) specifications for Contractors hired for road rehabilitation, (ii) guidelines for evidencing voluntary land donation (if needed), (iii) land acquisition procedures (if needed), (iv) a chance finds procedure, labor management procedures to apply to community workers (if engaged in core functions of the Project) and (v) SEA/SH prevention measures in accordance with the requirements of the relevant ESSs and (b) a Stakeholder Engagement Plan, which includes special procedures for meaningful consultation with beneficiary Indigenous Peoples. The Project will also partially rely on the use of the Borrower E&S Framework that allow the achievement of outcomes materially consistent with the objectives of ESS 2, ESS 3, ESS 7 and ESS 10. E. Project Management. SAR will establish an Executive Secretariat (SAR/SEE) that will act as the Project Management Unit (PMU), in form and substance satisfactory to the Bank as a condition of effectiveness that will be responsible for overall management, planning, coordination, monitoring and evaluation of all Project’s activities, as well as for Project financial management, procurement, disbursements and accounting. The PMU will be composed at the minimum of a general coordinator under the responsibility of the Executive Secretary, one specialist for monitoring and evaluation, a financial specialist, a procurement specialist, and one social and one environmental specialists. 31.
