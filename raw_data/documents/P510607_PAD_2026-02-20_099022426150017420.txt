STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Macroeconomic resilience and persistent structural gaps. Over the past two decades, Peru has maintained macroeconomic resilience, emerging as one of the most stable economies in Latin America. Between 2002 and 2013, the country averaged 6.1 percent annual gross domestic product (GDP) growth, though this has slowed to around 3 percent per year since 2014. This rapid growth during 2002-2013 was triggered by the mining sector assisted by a commodities super cycle, which brought in foreign currency through foreign direct investment and export revenues. Strong fiscal discipline, inflation-targeting monetary policy, and sound debt management supported sustained growth and low public debt, with international reserves providing a solid buffer against external shocks. Despite this solid macroeconomic foundation, Peru faces deep structural challenges: persistent informality, inequality across regions, limited public investment execution, and recurring political instability continue to constrain inclusive and sustainable development. 2. Poverty rates in Peru have seen major improvements over the past two decades, but persistent inequalities and stark gaps in access to essential services continue to pose significant challenges. The national poverty rate decreased from 58.7 percent in 2004 to 20.2 percent in 2019, but it spiked during the COVID-19 crisis, reaching around 30 percent in 2020 before declining to 27.6 percent in 2024 (using the national poverty line). Using the international poverty line of US$8.30 per day (2021 PPP), poverty reached 38.3 percent in 2023 and declined to 36.2 percent in 2024. The urban–rural divide is stark: 39.3 percent of the rural population lives in poverty compared to 24.8 percent in urban areas. Around 70 percent of Peru’s poor now live in urban areas, reflecting a notable urbanization of poverty that accelerated during and after the pandemic. The poorest segments of Peru’s population also face deficits in access to basic services. Nearly 60 percent of households still lack at least one basic service such as running water, sanitation, electricity, or internet connection. Stark differences between Lima and mining-intensive regions contribute to social tensions and weaken institutional trust. As highlighted in the World Bank’s 2023 Poverty Assessment and the Systematic Country Diagnostic Update for Peru (2022), closing these gaps is essential for inclusive growth. Improving the management of mining revenues in the regions is essential to help reduce regional inequality, as these revenues can be translated into jobs and infrastructure. Poverty levels in mining jurisdictions are generally lower where state capacity has been stronger. 3. Mining remains a strategic pillar of Peru’s economy, contributing around 10 percent of GDP and more than 63 percent of export revenues, with copper alone generating exports around US$23 billion in 2024. The sector has consistently underpinned Peru’s trade surplus, foreign reserves, and fiscal performance, with mining-related transfers to subnational governments exceeding US$2.18 billion in 2024, financing infrastructure, health, and education in producing regions. Royalties and corporate income taxes from mining also reinforce macroeconomic stability and investor confidence, supporting Peru’s favorable credit ratings and regional competitiveness as an investment destination. Mining is one of Peru’s largest generators of formal employment in the producing regions, sustaining over 256,000 direct jobs in 2025—the highest level on record—and nearly 71 percent of these through contractors and service providers, which multiplies opportunities across engineering, logistics, construction, and community services. Each direct mining job in Peru generates an additional 6.25 jobs through indirect and induced effects, meaning that total mining-related employment reaches close to 1.5 million people across the economy. The impact is especially visible in regions such as Arequipa, Apurímac, and Moquegua, where projects like Cerro Verde, Las Bambas, and Quellaveco have at times employed 10,000–15,000 workers during peak construction. Moreover, the portfolio of exploration projects currently underway across 17 regions is the seedbed of future employment. 4. The global shift toward clean energy and digital infrastructure places the country at a strategic inflection point. As the world’s third-largest copper producer, Peru is well positioned to meet rising demand for minerals essential to renewable energy systems, electric vehicles, and transmission networks. Beyond copper, diversification into other critical minerals offers an opportunity to strengthen Peru’s role in regional and global value chains. To seize this Page 1 moment, investments must be leveraged not only for sustainable production but also to generate broader spillovers— shared infrastructure (roads, ports, energy), skills development, resilient local supply chains, and inclusive territorial development. A forecasted increase in copper production could raise Peru’s GDP growth rate by about 1.5 percentage points, underscoring copper’s central role in driving long-term growth prospects (World Bank, 2025). B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. Peru has historically been one of the leading destinations for mineral exploration 1, but its competitiveness has weakened in recent years. Between 2020 and 2024, the country attracted about US$1.76 billion in direct exploration investment, and in 2024 alone it recorded 75 active exploration projects with investments of roughly US$644 million, concentrated mainly in copper and gold. Yet Peru’s share of global exploration budgets has declined from 6.3 percent in 2017 to 3.8 percent in 2024, reflecting a loss of relative attractiveness. While the country’s geological potential remains strong, competitiveness is increasingly constrained by lengthy permitting processes and by the need to modernize the quality, coverage, and technological base of geological information and mapping to attract new entrants, including junior explorers. 6. Recent stagnation of private investment in mining driven by permitting delays, overlapping mandates, and rising social tensions risks eroding Peru’s hard-won gains. The last major mining sector reform was carried out over 25 years ago, with World Bank support. There is a need for second generation public investments and policy reforms centered on improved governance, institutional modernization, digitalization, streamlined permitting processes, improved access to high-quality precompetitive geoscientific data, stronger inter-agency coordination, and inclusive community engagement. By addressing these structural constraints in its enabling environment, Peru will be better positioned to enhance competitiveness, restore investor confidence that will lead to attracting investment and generating jobs, reduce conflict risks, and transform its mineral endowment into sustained prosperity. 7. Peru is considered an underexplored jurisdiction, and realizing its full geological potential will require regaining competitiveness. Historically ranked among the top 20 jurisdictions worldwide for exploration attractiveness, Peru had fallen to 40th place among 84 countries in the Fraser Institute ranking in 2024, underscoring the urgency of restoring investor confidence. A further concern is the shrinking role of junior companies, which before 2020 accounted for about 50 percent of exploration investment in Peru but today represent only 23 percent, limiting the pipeline of new discoveries. 2 The most critical constraint is the permitting and authorization process, which can take three to four years from the identification of an area of interest until drilling begins, with limited proportionality in requirements that apply almost equally to highly complex projects and to simpler exploration activities. 3 At the same time, Peru must strengthen its pre-competitive geological information capabilities, using modern technologies to drive innovation and reduce exploration risk. 8. High-quality geoscientific data is essential for responsible exploration and planning. The Geological, Mining and Metallurgical Institute (Instituto Geológico, Minero y Metalúrgico) (INGEMMET), Peru’s national geological service, plays a vital role in generating and disseminating precompetitive geoscientific information. Precompetitive geological information is high-quality geoscience data collected by governments—through mapping, geophysics, geochemistry, laboratories and drill core libraries—before private exploration begins. Funded as a public good, it is geoscience infrastructure that reduces uncertainty, attracts investment, and underpins sustainable sector development, unlike “soft” studies or consultancies. Since its conception 47 years ago, its mission has been to provide public and transparent geoscientific information. The country’s territory is organized across 20 metallogenic belts, and INGEMMET has made progress in collecting, field checking, geological mapping and digitizing geochemical information. It is important to note that geoscientific information acts as the catalyzer for igniting the mining value chain and, is considered as the “trigger” for early investment, without which no resource replacement will take place and, no other stages of the mining cycle may be properly sustained. Compared to countries like Canada, Australia, Chile and Brazil, Peru lags in quality-airborne geophysics and thematic data layers which are essential for derisking early-stage exploration. Improved geoscientific information would not only attract junior exploration mining companies, which are responsible for 75 percent of global discoveries, but also support land use and infrastructure planning, water resources assessments, and investments in climate risk management and resilience. Expanding and modernizing geodata acquisition tools, processing, interpretation and analysis methodologies such as mineral systems, is “not a luxury” but a prerequisite to increasing Peru’s competitiveness and sustainability of the mining sector and is therefore a cross- cutting enabler for attracting investment, enhancing exploration discovery success and an overall tool for informed territorial planning and governance. 9. Peru’s permitting system is widely regarded by stakeholders as fragmented and administratively burdensome. An inefficient Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) process, combined with the lack of integration among agencies responsible for water rights, land classification, and indigenous consultation, results in significant delays to project approvals. In 2025, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) noted that permitting inefficiencies were stalling critical mineral investments and constraining long-term growth. While some reforms have been introduced, such as concurrent processing of water and environmental permits, the system continues to lack digital integration, procedural clarity, and risk-based approaches. These inefficiencies increase transaction costs for investors, with a particular impact on junior companies, who depend on predictable timelines to secure financing. A modern, digital permitting system that strengthens inter-agency governance and shortens approval cycles is critical to improving the competitiveness of Peru’s mining sector. 10. Mobilizing investment for mine construction and production depends on streamlining regulation. Peru holds one of the world’s most attractive geological endowments, with large reserves of copper, silver, gold, zinc, and other critical minerals. The Ministry of Energy and Mines (MINEM) reports a pipeline of 66 mining projects with a combined CAPEX of US$63.4 billion. Yet this investment potential remains stalled due to systemic regulatory bottlenecks. The Instituto Peruano de Economía (IPE, 2023) estimates that a representative mining project experiencing a four-year permitting delay would forgo US$1.4 billion in investment, reduce GDP by about US$3.6 billion, cut fiscal revenues by US$0.6 billion, and delay the creation of nearly 8,500 jobs in construction and 7,400 jobs in production each year. With an average of 15 years required to bring a mine from discovery to production, such delays significantly diminish the economic benefits of Peru’s mineral endowment. 11. Peru's mining sector, a cornerstone of its economy, faces significant climate vulnerability challenges that threaten its sustainability and productivity. The country's diverse geography and climate variability exposes mining operations to a range of climate-related risks, including water scarcity, extreme weather events, and temperature fluctuations. Water scarcity is particularly critical, as mining activities are heavily dependent on water resources for mineral processing and dust control. Climate change exacerbates this issue by altering precipitation patterns and reducing water availability, which can lead to operational disruptions and increased costs. Additionally, extreme weather events such as heavy rainfall and landslides can damage infrastructure, delay production, and pose safety risks to workers. Temperature fluctuations also affect the efficiency of mining equipment and energy consumption. To mitigate these vulnerabilities, Peru's mining sector is increasingly focusing on climate adaptation strategies, such as improving water management practices, investing in resilient infrastructure, and adopting energy-efficient technologies. These efforts are essential to ensure the sector's resilience to climate impacts and to support Peru's broader goals of sustainable development and environmental stewardship. 12. Alignment with global environmental, social, and governance (ESG) standards will unlock responsible markets and finance environmentally sustainable opportunities. As global commodity buyers and investors demand stronger ESG performance, Peru must continue adapting its institutional frameworks to meet evolving expectations, Page 3 particularly in areas such as cumulative impact assessment, carbon footprint reporting, and gender inclusion. The Servicio Nacional de Certificación Ambiental para las Inversiones Sostenibles (SENACE) and the Organismo de Evaluación y Fiscalización Ambiental (OEFA) has strengthened their regulatory oversight in recent years, introducing improved mechanisms for impact evaluation and environmental monitoring. Some leading mining companies in Peru are already adopting voluntary standards such as The Copper Mark or the Initiative for Responsible Mining Assurance (IRMA). Enhancing ESG alignment is not only about reducing risk, but it also opens new opportunities. Responsible investors and downstream buyers increasingly require traceability, certification, and climate resilience as prerequisites for sourcing minerals, particularly those linked to the energy transition. Better ESG frameworks are thus vital to secure access to responsible markets and unlock sustainable finance for Peru’s mineral value chains. 13. Rebuilding trust through stronger territorial presence and credible community engagement is vital. More than half of all active social-environmental conflicts in Peru are linked to community perceptions of mining, particularly in regions with weak institutions and limited access to basic services. These conflicts frequently emerge from unfulfilled community expectations, weak implementation of community participatory processes, weak state capacity to translate mining revenues into development impact, and lack of transparency around benefits and agreements. Moreover, the absence of systematic follow-up commitments made to communities has undermined credibility. Many local stakeholders perceive the state as absent or reactive, while companies are left to fill governance gaps in complex social environments. Building trust by the national government will require a more proactive, professional, and transparent approach to community engagement, including improved mechanisms for prior consultation, participatory monitoring, and digital tracking of agreements. Strengthening the territorial presence of government agencies and enabling effective conflict prevention and resolution systems are essential to achieving socially legitimate mining and minimizing the risk of project delays. 14. Formalizing and supporting artisanal and small-scale miners (ASM) can enhance inclusion, productivity, and environmental performance. ASM plays a central but often informal role in Peru’s gold production, accounting for an estimated 40 percent of national gold output. Over 85,000 ASM continue to operate outside of formal legal and environmental frameworks, exposing them to unsafe conditions, legal uncertainty, and poor access to finance, training, or markets. Informal ASM is also associated with widespread use of mercury, deforestation, and revenue leakage. Despite repeated efforts at regularization, progress has been slow, due to overly complex procedures, limited institutional support, and lack of incentives for formalization. Integrating ASM more effectively into the formal system would reduce health risks exposure and environmental harm, boost rural employment, and increase state revenue while enhancing the social legitimacy of the mining sector. 15. A national consensus for mining reform underpins the government’s commitment to sectoral transformation. Over the past five years, Peru has built a cross-sectoral consensus on the need to modernize mining governance to restore competitiveness, sustainability, and legitimacy. Strategic studies including the “2019 Comisión para el Desarrollo Minero Sostenible” report, the “Visión de la Minería al 2030”, and the 2021 World Bank Mining Sector Diagnostic—have identified clear priorities: regulatory predictability, geodata acquisition and management, better interagency coordination, improved socio-environmental performance, and formalization of ASM to foster private investment and generate quality jobs. Building on this foundation, the Ministry of Energy and Mines launched the process to formulate the National Multisectoral Mining Policy to 2050 (Política Nacional Multisectorial Minera, PNMM, 2050), in alignment with Centro Nacional de Planeamiento Estratégico (CEPLAN)’s national planning framework. The proposed policy seeks to incorporate the voices of regional governments, civil society, indigenous organizations, and industry representatives, and will provide a long-term roadmap for sustainable mining development. The Competitiveness and Sustainability in the Strategic Minerals Sector (Programa de Mejoramiento de la Competitividad y la Sostenibilidad ‘COMPASS’) project is closely aligned with this national agenda and contributes to providing a concrete platform to begin translating this shared vision into institutional change and regulatory reform. 16. Comprehensive sector support program will be complemented by analytical and advisory services. The project’s comprehensive sector support will be complemented by analytical and advisory services aligned with the Page 4 World Bank Group Minerals & Metals Sector Strategy and the regional RISE initiative (Leveraging Critical Minerals for Job Creation in Latin America), including the LAC study “Catalyzing Resilient Mineral Value Chains for Job Creation” (P512876). While downstream manufacturing is outside the scope of this operation, the project’s upstream reforms are part of a broader sector agenda that will be reinforced through preparation of a Northern Copper Corridor Roadmap. This roadmap will identify priority investments and policy actions to unlock copper production in the northern Andes— particularly in Cajamarca—while promoting territorial development through integrated planning of infrastructure, energy, water, and social investments. In parallel, analytical work will support reduced material footprint, more efficient land and water use, expanded renewable energy access, and lower waste generation. Together, these efforts will strengthen production competitiveness, enable value chain upgrading, and ensure that mineral development contributes to quality job creation and inclusive regional growth. II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Development Objective (PDO) 17. The PDO is to improve the competitiveness and sustainability of Peru’s mining sector. 18. By generating cutting-edge geoscientific data, modernizing key institutions, and improving the way environmental and social risks are managed, the project will help position Peru’s mining sector as a more competitive, responsible, and attractive driver of private investment, job generation, and sustainable growth. 19. The project design targets the binding constraints to private mining investment in Peru by (i) upgrading public geoscience data—responding to demonstrated demand (≈19.7 million platform visits, 2020–2024, with cadaster, geology, geochemistry, geophysics, and resources among the most accessed)—and (ii) modernizing permitting to reduce costly delays (a four-year deferral of a US$1.4 billion copper project is associated with ≈US$3.3 billion lower GDP and ≈US$560 million less fiscal revenue). International experience shows that public investment in geoscientific data can significantly crowd in private exploration, with commonly cited leverage ratios of around 1:5 and documented cases exceeding 1:7 in leading jurisdictions, supporting the project’s leverage assumptions. These outcomes align with the World Bank Group Minerals & Metals Sector Strategy by strengthening sector governance and foundational infrastructure (including reliable geodata and streamlined approvals), while expected jobs outcomes are framed within Peru’s local and regional development agenda—emphasizing inclusive, place-based benefits for supplier ecosystems and gains in territorial competitiveness. This investment represents a strategic innovation in Peru’s mining sector, moving from conventional geological mapping toward a modern mineral systems approach that strengthens national geoscience capacity and supports diversification into critical minerals. This forward-looking shift positions Peru to meet rising global demand for minerals essential to the energy transition, while reinforcing its established strengths in copper. With World Bank support, INGEMMET and the Government will gain the tools and methodologies to build a stronger, investors´ oriented and more resilient geological knowledge infrastructure—placing Peru closer to the global leaders in geological prospectivity mapping, alongside Australia, Canada, and the United States. This will contribute positively to the investment decision making of investors since prospecting risk reduction will be inherent and lead to more exploration activities. Experiences in other countries indicate a multiplier factor plus a direct and positive correlation between the public investment in quality precompetitive geoscientific data and the targeting and eventual discovery of greenfield mineral deposits adding value through investment and jobs generation supported by that data. 22. This project also addresses the governance and institutional bottlenecks that slow the development of Peru’s world-class mining pipeline, particularly in copper. Despite a strong portfolio of projects, complex permitting procedures result in delays to mining production, postponing GDP growth, fiscal revenues, and job creation—costs that mount with every year of delay. In the last 15 years, the average delay for review and approval of the EIAs of the most important copper projects has increased from 6 months to up to 24 months. Accelerating timelines is therefore not only a matter of competitiveness but of delivering development benefits sooner. The digital transformation of the MINEM is essential to operationalize an effective Ventanilla Única Digital (VUD) that streamlines permitting, reduces uncertainty, and restores investor confidence. In parallel, the project will support a more formal, safe, and environmentally responsible artisanal and small-scale mining sector through targeted training services. 23. A comprehensive approach is embedded in the ToC, which positions de-risking as the foundational enabler for achieving the project’s medium-term outcomes—greater sector competitiveness and more sustainable mining practices, and for realizing long-term impacts such as Peru’s diversification into critical minerals, reduced uncertainty across the mining value chain, increased revenue, creation of more and better jobs and sustainable economic growth. This component will enhance Peru’s capacity to generate, manage, and disseminate high-quality geoscientific information as a public good, reinforcing INGEMMET’s role as the country’s national geological survey. Access to open, geoscientific data covering geology, geophysics, geochemistry, and remote sensing is critical for informed decision-making, sustainable resource management, and investment attraction. Subcomponent 1.1: National drill core library. 5 Peru currently lacks a centralized, modern facility built to international standards for the custody and management of drill core samples and geological specimens. Existing collections—comprising approximately 60,000 meters of drill cores and 77,000 geological samples are dispersed across various locations/ facilities and stored under inadequate conditions, limiting their preservation and hindering access to critical data for exploration and research, with drill cores forming the highest value part of an exploration survey. This situation limits the country’s ability to capture the economic value of existing and future geological data, drives up exploration costs through duplicated efforts, and slows both technical assessments and investment decisions. 31. The establishment of a national drill core library will allow for systematic preservation, cataloging, digitization and sensor-scanning of geological evidence accumulated over decades, including drill cores, samples, and associated technical documentation and scanned images (from visible, infrared and multispectral sensors). International experience demonstrates that public availability of reliable geoscientific information strengthens mineral prospectivity, drives applied research and improves a country’s competitiveness in attracting mining investment. Moreover, centralizing custody and access to this information will reduce duplicate drilling and, consequently, the associated environmental impacts, aligning geological data management with sustainable practices and the objectives of transitioning toward a more responsible and efficient mining sector. 32. Subcomponent 1.2: Generation of geoscientific information. 6 A significant portion of Peru’s high-Andean region, with high potential for strategic and critical minerals, lacks modern geophysical, geological, and geochemical information generated under international standards and key to prepare subsurface mapping analytical products. This data gap limits the country’s ability to conduct accurate mineral prospectivity assessments and to support evidence- based public policies. Current national geophysical coverage shows substantial gaps that hinder a comprehensive characterization and proper understanding of the subsurface features, while existing regional geochemistry does not have the density and coverage required to support regional geochemical and targeting assessments, territorial planning and environmental management. 33. This subcomponent will finance the generation of high-quality precompetitive geophysical and geochemical information to reduce geological uncertainty and strengthen the knowledge base for strategic decision-making. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 48. The project design draws on the World Bank’s long-standing engagement in Peru’s mining sector and lessons from global mining projects in Zambia, Malawi, Mongolia, Nigeria, and Guinea. Experience across these operations shows that strong government ownership, clear institutional mandates, and capable implementation units are indispensable for success. Reforms that lacked a committed government champion or were fragmented across multiple agencies underperformed, whereas projects anchored in empowered institutions achieved greater continuity, coordination, and effectiveness. 49. Another important lesson is that project design must remain realistic, focused, and closely aligned with sector priorities. Past technical assistance in Peru revealed that an excessive number of objectives and unenforceable covenants diluted outcomes and reduced sustainability. Likewise, global experience shows that reforms such as cadaster modernization or permitting simplification only delivered results when paired with practical tools, digital platforms, and sustained technical assistance. This underscores the need to define clear priorities, ensure institutional capacity, and provide hands-on support to make reforms operational in practice. 50. International experience also highlights the importance of addressing environmental and social risks early and systematically. Projects in Africa have shown that without timely attention to issues such as mine closure, site rehabilitation, water management, and ASM formalization, reforms risk generating long-term liabilities. In contrast, initiatives that embedded environmental planning and stakeholder engagement from the outset were better able to manage risks and create a foundation for sustainable sector development. 51. Finally, inclusive governance, local benefits, gender-responsiveness approaches, and robust monitoring frameworks have emerged as decisive factors for legitimacy and sustainability. Projects in Guinea, Zambia, and Mongolia demonstrate that reforms are more effective when they include structured consultation processes, opportunities for local suppliers and workers, and targeted support for women and vulnerable groups. At the same time, strong data systems, adaptive monitoring, and peer-learning platforms have proven essential to institutionalize reforms and enable replication across regions. These lessons reinforce that successful mining reforms must combine institutional capacity, practical tools, early risk management, and inclusive approaches to deliver lasting impact. Page 13 III. Technical and Economic Analysis Technical 59. The overall design of the project is anchored in Peru’s ongoing National Mining Policy process, whose justification was submitted to CEPLAN through the Pertinence Study 14 that confirms competitiveness and sustainability as the guiding priorities for reform. While the policy itself is still under preparation and expected to be approved in the coming two years, the Pertinence Study provides a clear framework for the modernization agenda. The technical design builds on Peru’s institutional strengths while addressing capacity gaps that have constrained investment attraction, particularly early exploration work. INGEMMET has a long tradition of generating geoscientific information, yet its facilities for drill core storage, geochronology, and modern survey techniques remain outdated, fragmented and require upgrades from older metallogenic belts to new and more performing mineral systems methodologies. The project will finance infrastructure, equipment, and training to bring these services to international standards, ensuring that the data produced is reliable, accessible, and directly relevant, in terms of geological subsurface details, to critical minerals investors and policymakers. On the regulatory side, MINEM has piloted the first phase of the VUD-MINEM platform as an informative digital tool, which has already demonstrated the potential to improve transparency. The project will build on this foundation by developing and operating a fully transactional VUD, aimed at streamlining procedures, reducing permitting times, and strengthening regulatory predictability and oversight. The project’s components target these bottlenecks. 60. Strengthening Peru’s geoscience infrastructure offers high economic returns by reducing exploration risk, accelerating discoveries, and lowering costs for both government and industry. A national drill-core library would retain and curate valuable geological samples that are currently lost, enabling cumulative knowledge to guide new exploration; globally, major discoveries typically emerge only after multiple companies revisit the same terrain, underscoring the high payoff from preserving core data. Taken together, these investments lower exploration costs and timelines, expand the pipeline of viable targets, and enhance the expected net benefits from private exploration, making the economic case for this component compelling. 61. Modernizing Peru’s mining administration and service delivery offers substantial economic benefits by reducing transaction costs, shortening project timelines, and improving environmental and social outcomes. Digital transformation at MINEM would consolidate fragmented, paper-based systems for permits, inspections, and environmental approvals, improving data interoperability and public access, enabling faster decisions, lowering compliance costs for firms, and strengthening oversight and accountability. These reforms are especially critical given Peru’s unusually long mine development timeline—about 15 years—driven by a permitting system perceived as unpredictable and duplicative; clearer mandates, digital interoperability across agencies, and streamlined, risk-based procedures would reduce delays and uncertainty, accelerating the transition of viable projects into construction and production, with significant returns through employment, fiscal revenues, and regional development. Complementary investments to support formal ASM miners through specialized technical training services would improve productivity, safety, and environmental performance, helping reduce operational risks and negative externalities. Together, these interventions improve regulatory efficiency, crowd-in private investment, and strengthen the economic performance and long-term sustainability of Peru’s mining sector. 62. The project is aligned with the Maximizing Finance for Development - Enabling (MFD-E) approach and contributes to MFD-E/Private Capital Enabled (PCE). Activities to enhance Peru’s geoscientific knowledge mapping and prospectivity analysis to enhance mineral exploration capabilities under the Component 1 and to improve regulatory efficiency, transparency, and social governance of Peru’s mining sector under the Component 2 are eliminating barriers to future private investment in exploration and mining and are considered MFD-enabling. The project also contributes to PCE, which is measured by the results indicator: private capital enabled in mining exploration which will lead to replenishing the depleted mineral reserves and generate new greenfield discoveries. It is expected MFD-E activities will enable US$800 million of mining exploration investment annually from 2029 and up to US$900 million per annum in 2034, which will represent a cumulative PCE of US$1,250 million from the project since Bank project effectiveness. This will increase at the same time the probability of discovering new mineral deposits of several critical minerals. Also, by reducing the permitting time, the project will reduce the current loss of GDP, tax revenues and jobs generation through bringing to development in a shorter period the mining projects currently in the pipeline. Building on its track record of de-risking mining investments and strengthening local development, IFC is well placed to complement the Project by mobilizing private capital and sharing lessons that enhance the sustainability and inclusiveness of sector reforms. 63. Gender. The project seeks to advance the status of women in ASM and is therefore aligned with Peru’s national ASM and gender quality policies and with the World Bank’s Gender Strategy 2024-2030. While mining in Peru remains a male-dominated sector due to cultural norms and stereotypes around gender roles (as is the case globally), women constitute a substantial share of the ASM workforce. Women make between 20–30 percent in ASM, and an estimated 8,000 to 14,000 women rely on pallaqueo 15 as their primary source of income 16. Yet, women disproportionately bear the adverse effects of ASM activities and remain under-protected. The informal, often precarious nature of their working conditions and lack of access to social safety nets expose them to severe health, social, and environmental risks. They face regulatory hurdles toward formalization, limited access to education and training, restricted knowledge and access to technologies and equipment, and lack of awareness of the health-related risks they are exposed to. In gold mining, for instance, women are often exposed to mercury, a potent neurotoxin used for the metal recovery. Economic Analysis 70. The economic benefits of the project are significant and multi-dimensional. 20 The geoscientific mapping activities under Component 1, which represent about 59 percent of total project costs, generate the largest share of measurable economic returns. Over 10 years, these activities are expected to deliver a 38 percent economic internal rate of return (EIRR) and a benefit–cost ratio of US$36 for every US$1 invested, highlighting how enhanced geoscientific data will boost private exploration. The net present value (NPV) of Component 1 alone is estimated at US$236 million. With all components included, the total NPV is US$797 million (6 percent discount rate), EIRR is 25 percent, and the benefit–cost ratio is 16.8. The results are highly positive, showing that while improved geoscientific data most directly enhances exploration favorability, investments in permitting efficiency also play key roles in long-term competitiveness and sustainability. 71. International benchmarks consistently demonstrate that every US$1 invested in geoscientific data mobilizes between US$6 and US$12 of private exploration spending. For instance, evaluations in Australia, Canada, and Ireland show benefit–cost ratios ranging from 1:5 to 1:9, with some cases exceeding 1:19 (Spain’s MAGNA program). Boulton (1999) estimated that each US$1 million invested in geoscience could generate discoveries valued at an average in situ value of US$125 million. Bernknopf et al. (2007) found multipliers of 1.2–8.2 in Baffin Island mapping programs.
