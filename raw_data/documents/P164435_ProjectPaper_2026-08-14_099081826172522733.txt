Umunnah Team Member No The World Bank Solar Energy in Local Communities (P164435) Page 1 Official Use Only I. BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING A. Introduction 1. This Project Paper seeks approval of the Executive Directors to (a) provide an additional financing (AF) of US$8.9 million—comprising a US$3.9 million Global Environment Facility (GEF) grant and a US$5 million IDA grant—to the Republic of Burundi for the Solar Energy in Local Communities Project (SOLEIL, P164435) and a Level 2 Restructuring to (i) revise the Project Development Objective (PDO) to align it with GEF requirements, (ii) revise the cost of the components, (iii) revise the disbursement estimates, (iv) amend the conditions of the project, and (v) revise the Results Framework (RF). The AF will scale up the project’s scope by increasing access to electricity and clean cooking solutions, while supporting private sector-led electric mobility (e-mobility) initiatives through an integrated, multisectoral approach at the energy-transport-environment nexus. The AF will also complement Government-led efforts to establish the enabling policy, regulatory, and institutional environment for e-mobility, thereby laying the foundation for future operations to further scale up, refine, and strengthen the development of the e-mobility sector. The project was approved in February 2020 and became effective in September 2020. 2. This restructuring constitutes the third restructuring of the project. The first restructuring, approved in June 2023, reduced the project cost from US$100 million to US$63 million by canceling the mini-grid component due to limited sector readiness for sustainable mini-grid business models in Burundi and reallocated funds across disbursement categories. The second restructuring and first AF, approved in February 2026, included additional financing of US$1.73 million, scaled up results-based connections, strengthened sustainability through improved connectivity, and extended the closing date to March 1, 2028. This AF is being processed separately because financing became available later and the eligibility requirements of GEF and Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (German Agency for International Cooperation, GIZ) financing prevented concurrent processing with the second restructuring and first AF. Implementation readiness is supported by the existing Fonds Energies Solaires et Cuisson (Solar Energy and Cooking Fund, FESEC) implementation arrangements, established grant implementation mechanisms, ongoing policy support for e- mobility, and technical assistance financed under this AF. 3. While the PDO remains highly relevant, GEF financing requires adding a reference to e-mobility. Accordingly, the original PDO, to expand access to energy services for households, enterprises, schools, and health centers in rural areas of Burundi, will be revised to expand access to energy and e-mobility services for households, enterprises, schools, and health centers in rural areas of Burundi. The RF will be updated to reflect a stronger focus on new connections, jobs, and private capital mobilization, in line with the Mission 300 (M300) initiative and the new corporate scorecard. M300 is a joint initiative of the World Bank Group (WBG) and African Development Bank initiative to provide electricity access to 300 million people in Sub-Saharan Africa by 2030. 4. The project is currently rated Moderately Satisfactory for both achievement of the PDO and Implementation Progress. While implementation was initially affected by delays related to the COVID-19 crisis and limited government capacity, the pace of implementation has since accelerated significantly. To date, approximately 85 percent of physical progress has been achieved (Seq No: 14), with cumulative disbursements of US$47 million, representing 76 percent of total financing. B. Country and Sector Context 5. Burundi, a landlocked country with a population of 12.9 million and an annual growth rate of 2.7 percent, faces significant challenges in delivering reliable basic services and generating sufficient jobs for its rapidly growing population. The country has one of the lowest electricity access rates globally, with only 25.9 percent of the population connected,1 compared to a Sub-Saharan Africa average of 52 percent. Access is highly uneven: While 72.5 percent of 1 https://www.esmap.org/Burundi_Beyond_Connections_Energy_Access_Diagnostic_Report_Based_on_Multi-Tier_Framework. The World Bank Solar Energy in Local Communities (P164435) Page 2 Official Use Only residents in Bujumbura and 42.6 percent in other urban centers have electricity, access in rural areas remains extremely limited at 1.8 percent, severely constraining economic activity, service delivery, and human capital development. 6. Climate change is an impactful factor in Burundi. The country is highly vulnerable to floods, wildfires, and landslides, which could have major impacts on the already vulnerable populations. Further, Think Hazard2 identifies earthquakes and water scarcity as medium risks, hence posing additional threats. Through the project, mitigation measures are proactively implemented to provide vulnerable populations with access to basic energy services, enabling them to prevent and mitigate the impact of these risks while reinforcing climate resilience. 7. In a context of structural vulnerabilities in fuel supply and a growing ambition for energy transition, the case for e-mobility is further reinforced by Burundi’s improving energy outlook. Investments in domestic renewable energy and expanding solar and off-grid electrification provide a growing electricity base that can support charging infrastructure development and is expected to contribute to improving public transportation services. This alignment between transport electrification and the broader energy transition creates a strong linkage between the two sectors and lays the foundation for a low-carbon mobility system, anchored in the country’s National Energy Compact under the M300 framework. The AF therefore adopts an integrated approach that combines energy access, transport electrification, and environmental sustainability through pilot investments and complementary policy and institutional support. 8. Beyond energy resilience, e-mobility value chains present meaningful opportunities for private sector engagement, job creation, and green skills development. However, adoption remains constrained by high up-front costs, limited access to finance, lack of charging infrastructure and viable business models, weak technical capacity, gaps in technical and regulatory standards, customs frameworks not yet adapted to electric vehicles (EVs), and inadequate battery end-of-life management. Establishing battery take-back arrangements will be important to manage environmental risks and reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. These market and institutional barriers cannot be addressed through private investment alone and require targeted concessional financing to demonstrate viable business models and establish the enabling environment for future market development. To address these constraints, the Government, led by the Ministry of Transport, is developing a national e-mobility strategy with support from the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) and the World Bank. 9. Burundi’s rising transport demand makes e-mobility a strategically attractive opportunity. The transport sector depends almost entirely on imported petroleum products and has been repeatedly disrupted by fuel shortages, resulting in price volatility, reduced mobility, and broader economic losses. Across the country, vehicle fleets including taxis and moto-taxis are dominated by high-emitting, aging vehicles that compound both the fiscal burden of fuel dependency and the public health costs of poor air quality. Against this backdrop, e-mobility using solar solutions offers a cost-effective and scalable pathway to reduce exposure to fuel supply shocks while significantly lowering operating costs for users over time. 10. GEF incrementality and rationale for support. The proposed GEF financing addresses these barriers by supporting private sector pilot investments through the FESEC platform, strengthening technical and institutional capacity, supporting the development of standards and implementation of initial elements of the national electrical and electronic waste (e- waste) management strategy, and demonstrating commercially viable solar-powered e-mobility business models, including solutions based on electric two- and three-wheelers and regional electric buses. Investment support will be provided through a dedicated e-mobility window under FESEC, complemented by technical assistance to strengthen business development, investment readiness, institutional coordination, and battery end-of-life management. By reducing first-mover risks, the GEF intervention is expected to catalyze private investment, enable future scale-up through IDA and private financing, and generate global environmental benefits through reduced GHG emissions, increased use of renewable energy in transport, and environmentally sound battery management. 2 Think Hazard, https://thinkhazard.org/en/report/43-burundi. To address these broader energy access and energy transition challenges, and in line with the M300 initiative, the Government of Burundi (GoB) is scaling up energy access through the National Energy Compact launched in September 2025, which targets 70 percent electricity access (approximately 9 million people) and 40 percent access to clean cooking solutions (5 million people) by 2030.3 Achieving these objectives will require an estimated US$3.49 billion, including 1.6 billion from private financing. The World Bank is supporting the GoB through policy dialogue, donor coordination, technical assistance, and financing to advance sector reforms, strengthen the enabling environment, and accelerate progress toward universal access by 2030. The proposed AF contributes to meeting these objectives by scaling up proven access interventions while introducing innovative private sector-led solutions to support Burundi’s longer-term energy transition. C. Parent Project Description and Status 12. Project description. The SOLEIL-Nyakiriza Project is designed to provide energy services to promote human capital development, enhance productivity of rural enterprises, and reach poor and vulnerable populations, including women. The project supports (a) electrifying up to 353 schools and 362 health centers with modular solar photovoltaic (PV) systems and implementing a multilayered maintenance approach; (b) providing 65,000 poor and vulnerable households with off- grid solar products; (c) distributing clean and efficient cookstoves (CECs) to 300,000 households; and (d) strengthening the enabling environment through improved policies, regulations, private sector participation, and capacity building for off- grid solar energy and clean cooking solutions. 13. The current PDO is to expand access to energy services for households, enterprises, schools, and health centers in rural areas of Burundi. The project is implemented through three components: (a) Component 1 - Energy Services for Schools and Health Service Centers (original cost US$27 million, revised cost US$28.61 million); (b) Component 2 - Energy Services for Households (original cost US$17 million, revised cost US$24.07 million); and (c) Component 3 - Technical Assistance, Capacity Building and Project Implementation Support (original cost US$19 million, revised cost US$20.98 million). The PDO remains highly relevant to the country and is in line with the World Bank’s mission, the M300 initiative, and Sustainable Development Goal 7 (SDG 7) to achieve universal access to electricity by 2030. 14. The project is implemented by a Project Implementation Unit (PIU) within the Ministère des Ressources Minières, Energétiques, de l’Industrie et du Commerce (Ministry of Mining Resources, Energy, Industry and Trade, MRMEICT4 ), which leads national energy policy design, oversees project implementation, and monitors results on behalf of the GoB. 15. Progress toward achievement of the PDO and overall implementation is rated ‘Moderately Satisfactory’. Following a slow implementation start driven by COVID-19 disruptions, limited implementation capacity, lengthy procurement processes, and the time required to establish new implementation mechanisms such as the private sector results-based financing (RBF) facility, project implementation regained momentum due to strategic actions, including the 2023 and 2026 restructurings, the appointment of a new project coordinator, and the signing of key contracts including the RBF. Progress toward achieving the PDO is advancing steadily, with approximately 390,000 people connected to electricity. Electrification of health facilities is now complete, with all 362 health centers electrified and fully operational, while school installations are actively under way (83), contributing to the end target of 353 schools equipped with standardized modular solar PV systems. Under the private sector component, as of May 2026, grants and RBF mechanisms for 54 companies are operational, with US$12.4 million committed (73 percent of the total allocation) and US$5.2 million disbursed, contributing to the targets of 402,000 people gaining access to electricity (390,000 reached so far) and 1.8 million people with access to clean cooking solutions (1.6 million reached) 3 https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/theworldbankgroupasset/document/2026/m300-energy-compacts/Burundi-National-Energy- Compact-Mission-300.pdf. 4 The Government’s ministries have been restructured, and MRMEICT is now the new ministry in charge of energy. The technical approach continues to emphasize innovative, modular, and scalable solar PV systems that can be expanded as demand grows. Under the school subcomponent, all kitchen shelters and 400 improved institutional cookstoves have been installed, with commissioning expected by the end of June 2026. A new, cleaner, and more efficient stove model has been tested and deployed in the 139 most recently equipped schools. The maintenance strategy is being implemented through complementary measures, including integrating maintenance payments into the health performance-based financing (PBF) (in preparation), rehabilitating and equipping district maintenance workshops (completed), providing a one-year training program for biomedical and solar PV technicians (ongoing), establishing a National Maintenance Center (in preparation), and developing a centralized remote monitoring platform while expanding digital connectivity across facilities (ongoing). This approach strengthens the energy-digital-health-education nexus by combining reliable energy access, digital connectivity, and remote monitoring with sustainable maintenance systems, local technical capacity, and improved delivery of health and education services. This AF will build on these achievements by supporting continued operation of the remote monitoring platform, maintenance of public facility solar systems, including out-of-warranty replacement of critical equipment, and provision of pans compatible with improved cookstoves built under the project in selected schools. 18. Under Component 2, the project established FESEC, managed by an independent fund manager Bamboo Capital Partners. The fund provides start-up, growth, and RBF grants to private companies to supply off-grid solar PV products and clean cookstoves while also ensuring product quality and after-sales monitoring. To promote sustainability and local ownership, targeted support has been provided to women entrepreneurs. As of May 2026, 73 percent of the total US$16.5 million allocation has been committed, of which US$5.2 million has been disbursed. To date, the fund has supported 25 solar companies with grants, reaching 65 000 households and facilitating the sale of 125,000 products. In the clean cooking sector, 30 contracts have been signed, with start-up and growth grants disbursed,resulting in the sale of 360,000 improved cookstoves. Overall, 12 women-led companies have been supported. In addition, the Private Sector Technical Assistance Facility was created to provide technical assistance to beneficiaries and other stakeholders such as microfinance institutions (MFIs), further strengthening the enabling environment. Recent learnings also highlight the critical role of consumer financing in overcoming affordability barriers for rural households, with FESEC beneficiaries leveraging village savings and loan associations (VSLAs), cooperatives and MFI being able to expand access to affordable energy solutions. These lessons have directly informed the AF, which expands technical assistance to FESEC beneficiaries and financial institutions while introducing a dedicated e-mobility window to support emerging private sector business models. 19. Component 3 has been reinforcing policy, institutional, and technical foundations for a sustainable and resilient energy sector in Burundi, with a focus on decentralized energy solutions. Under this component, the project strengthens technical assistance and capacity building in Burundi’s energy sector. It supports analytical work, advisory services, and institutional development to advance the World Bank’s engagement and help the GoB achieve its M300 targets through the National Energy Compact. Key activities include (a) least-cost power system planning and investment coordination through the Energy Sector Working Group (ESWG); (b) promotion of distributed renewable energy, electric mobility, and clean cooking solutions, including the development of off-grid and clean cooking quality standards, the national e-waste strategy, national strategy for clean cooking, and a revised energy policy; (c) sector reform and institutional strengthening, such as the revision of implementation decrees from the revised electricity law; and (d) human capital development through a new Renewable Energy Master’s Program launching in 2025–2026 at the University of Burundi (with nearly 30 percent of female students enrolled in the current program). The introduction of the e-mobility window is expected to generate broader development benefits beyond decarbonization. In very low-income countries, the rationale for e-mobility extends beyond reducing GHG emissions to include lower transport costs, improved public health, enhanced energy security, greater access, and social inclusion. EVs have lower lifetime operating costs than internal combustion vehicles due to reduced energy consumption and maintenance requirements, generating significant savings for commercial operators of electric two- and three-wheelers and regional electric buses. These benefits are particularly important in landlocked countries such as Burundi, where high fuel import costs make e-mobility attractive from both energy security and foreign exchange perspectives. Regional electric buses, together with electric two- and three-wheelers, can improve access to markets, health facilities, schools, and jobs in rural and peri-urban areas. In addition, e-mobility can increase electricity demand for rural electrification systems, particularly privately operated solar mini-grids, by encouraging daytime charging and improving their financial viability. 31. III. KEY RISKS 36. The overall risk of the project remains High. The overall implementation risk of the project remains High, reflecting Burundi’s fragile macroeconomic conditions, limited institutional capacity, and systemic governance constraints that affect implementation efficiency and sustainability. While the operating environment has improved since effectiveness, key risks persist related to long-term maintenance of public facility solar systems, coordination among implementing entities, and sustainability of private sector participation. This AF will scale up access to electricity and clean cooking solutions and support e-mobility activities while introducing additional technical, environmental, and coordination complexity that will need to be closely managed, particularly with respect to charging infrastructure, battery management, and private sector participation. These risks will be mitigated through dedicated technical assistance, strengthened implementation support, and enhanced coordination across implementing entities. 37. Political and Governance risk remains High. The political situation has stabilized since the 2020 elections. However, the Government is encouraged to improve its institutional capacity to mitigate the risks of political interference, coordination challenges across sectors, and potential policy or leadership changes. To mitigate these risks, the project relies on the interministerial Steering Committee, regular policy dialogue with the Government and development partners, and strengthened implementation support to maintain coordination and policy continuity. 38. Macroeconomic conditions remain fragile, with persistent inflation, limited fiscal space, fuel scarcity, and constraints in access to foreign exchange that could affect project implementation, procurement, and payment flows. The project mitigates these risks through phased implementation, regular procurement planning, close financial monitoring, use of local currency RBF disbursements, and coordination with the Recipient within the broader macro-fiscal dialogue supported by the World Bank and partners. Sustained reforms in exchange rate management and fiscal discipline remain critical to maintaining stability. The World Bank Solar Energy in Local Communities (P164435) Page 9 Official Use Only 39. Economic and financial summary: The original economic analysis used a cost-benefit framework to determine the development impact of the project, the rationale for public sector involvement, and the value added of the World Bank’s support. The economic returns of the project were derived from the cost savings generated by replacing traditional lighting and cooking solutions with solar home systems, community energy centers, and improved cookstoves for households, businesses, and public institutions, including health and education facilities. The estimated economic returns of the project did not account for other likely benefits such as increased level of service and reduced local environmental and health costs associated with deforestation and dry cell disposal. The same methodology has been used in the revised analysis of the project for the proposed AF. 48. The economic analysis undertaken for the first AF confirmed the project’s viability and remains valid for this AF. The updated economic analysis resulted in an economic internal rate of return (EIRR) of 40 percent and a net present value (NPV) of US$33.3 million at a 6 percent discount rate. The lower EIRR compared to the original appraisal (44 percent) reflects the removal of the mini-grid component early in implementation. The revised analysis also estimated that the project would avoid 57,790 tons of CO2 emissions through the deployment of solar products and CECs and confirmed that the results remained robust under sensitivity analysis. The findings of the revised economic analysis are confirmed to remain valid, and the modest scale-up of existing RBF activities under the AF does not warrant a new economic assessment. While the benefits of the new e-mobility pilot cannot yet be reliably quantified, the pilot is expected to generate valuable demonstration effects and implementation lessons to inform future scale-up. GEF Incremental Cost Analysis GEF Incrementality 1. The proposed GEF financing complements the IDA-financed SOLEIL by addressing the systemic market, institutional, technical, and environmental barriers that currently prevent the emergence of a sustainable e-mobility market in Burundi. While IDA financing primarily supports expanded access to electricity, clean cooking, and strengthening of energy institutions, GEF resources finance incremental activities that generate global environmental benefits by accelerating the transition toward low-carbon transport. 2. The principal barriers and the corresponding GEF-supported interventions are as follows: • High up-front investment costs and limited access to finance. The GEF will establish a dedicated e-mobility financing window under FESEC to provide catalytic grant financing for private companies, reducing first-mover investment risks and demonstrating commercially viable business models. • Limited charging infrastructure and uncertain technical and commercial conditions for deployment. The GEF will support pilot deployment of solar-powered EV charging infrastructure together with standardized connection protocols, safety standards, and utility integration arrangements, demonstrating scalable solutions suitable for Burundi’s context. • Limited technical capacity and private sector experience. The GEF will finance technical assistance, business advisory services, investment readiness support, and capacity building for e-mobility companies, financial institutions, and relevant public institutions. • Weak policy, regulatory, and technical framework. The GEF will support implementation of the national e-mobility strategy, development of technical standards, and implementation of initial elements of the national e-waste strategy. • Lack of environmentally sound battery end-of-life management. The GEF will support battery collection systems, temporary storage arrangements, and environmentally sound end-of-life management consistent with extended producer responsibility principles. Incremental Reasoning 3. Without GEF financing, SOLEIL would continue to expand access to electricity and clean cooking through proven delivery mechanisms. However, the e-mobility activities would not proceed at scale because the commercial, institutional, and technical risks remain too high for either government or private investors to assume. 4. The GEF grant finances the incremental costs required to overcome these first-mover barriers by investing in public-good functions that private investors cannot finance on their own, including charging infrastructure readiness, technical standards, battery stewardship, and institutional capacity. These activities reduce investment risks, demonstrate viable business models, strengthen the enabling environment, and are expected to catalyze future investment from the private sector and development partners, enabling the long-term scale-up of e-mobility in Burundi. The proposed GEF intervention is expected to generate the following global environmental benefits: • Reduced GHG emissions through the replacement of fossil-fuel-based transport with electric mobility solutions powered by renewable energy. • Increased use of renewable electricity in the transport sector through solar-powered charging infrastructure. • Improved environmentally sound management of traction batteries through strengthened battery collection, temporary storage, recycling, and end-of-life management systems. • Demonstration of commercially viable e-mobility business models that catalyze future replication and scale-up, resulting in additional long-term GHG emission reductions.
