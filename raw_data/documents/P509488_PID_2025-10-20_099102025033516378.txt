Introduction and Context Regional and Country Context 1. Central Asia is experiencing rapid population and economic growth and is poised to become a significant market as countries aim for higher income levels. With over 70 million people, Central Asia’s regional gross domestic product (GDP) was US$410 billion in 2023. Despite this, all five countries remain in the ‘middle-income’ bracket, with Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyz Republic in the lower middle-income group. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, the region’s economy grew an average of 5.5 percent annually, driven largely by fossil fuels exports (Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan). Growth in Central Asia is projected to reach 5 percent in 2025 and 4.4 in 20261. Rapid population growth and the pursuit of higher income levels demand new economic drivers. Several countries are now accelerating economic and social reforms, aiming for diversified and sustainable growth in the coming years. 1 Belacin, Matias, Leonardo Iacovone, lvailo lzvorski, and Sergiy Kasyanenko. 2025. As highlighted in recent studies from the region, sustaining Central Asia’s growth will depend on the region’s ability to foster competitive sectors and strengthen key enablers such as energy. Countries will need to scale up renewable energy to promote a cleaner and more diversified energy mix, while boosting productivity and energy security. Achieving this will require reforms that reinforce market principles, promote regional integration, and support economic diversification. Regional integration, in particular, can yield significant economic benefits—ensuring a reliable energy supply that supports economic stability and improves living standards through affordable and dependable power. 2 2. Expanding regional electricity trade is critical for Central Asia’s energy security and affordability. By tapping into the region’s diverse and complementary resources—hydropower surpluses from the Kyrgyz Republic and Tajikistan, thermal power from Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan, and the rapidly growing solar and wind capacity, especially in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan—regional integration can balance seasonal and variable supply, reduce outages, and lower system costs. The result is a more reliable and affordable power supply for businesses and households, coupled with better resilience against fuel shocks, extreme weather events, droughts, and other disruptions. Unlocking these benefits will require both physical and institutional infrastructure for electricity trade. 3. An integrated power system can serve as a powerful driver of job creation and economic growth across Central Asia. Regional electricity trade and integration will accelerate the rollout of large-scale hydropower, solar, and wind generation, as well as essential grid upgrades. These investments will spur private sector growth, creating thousands of _For Official Use Only_ local jobs by (a) building, digitalizing and upgrading cross-border transmission lines, substations, and regional interconnectors, thereby creating direct and indirect employment in engineering, construction, and logistics; (b) operating reliable networks that rely on skilled jobs in grid management, system operations, and maintenance; (c) supporting a more flexible and interconnected grid to absorb more variable renewable energy (like wind and solar), which generates jobs in manufacturing, installation, and system integration. Additionally, the increased reliability of integrated regional grids will help address a key bottleneck to doing business and therefore support industries and small and medium enterprises. An integrated power system will strengthen regional cooperation in Central Asia by creating mutual benefits that no country can achieve alone, while scaling up private capital mobilization. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Over the past three decades, the energy sector has been a key driver of economic growth in Central Asia and will remain crucial as the region transitions to cleaner, more inclusive, and diversified growth. Drawing on its rich hydrocarbon resources, the energy sector in Central Asia has been highly dependent on fossil fuels, although the region is well endowed in natural resources needed to shift towards clean energy. In 2022, 73 percent of electricity demand in the region was met by fossil fuels. Although energy endowments differ—some countries export while others import—this reliance on fossil fuels exposes the region to shared vulnerabilities, including volatile fuel prices, infrastructure constraints, and growing difficulty in providing reliable energy at home. 5. With energy demand set to increase by about 30 percent by 2030 and potentially doubling by 2050, there is a pressing need for a substantial expansion of clean generation capacity in the region to support economic growth and ensure energy security. A World Bank regional study3 has evaluated energy demands and identified least-cost generation and transmission options to meet this demand up to 2050, under various electricity trade scenarios. Deeper regional connectivity and trade could generate economic benefits of around US$15 billion between 2025 and 2050, under a business-as-usual scenario. Enhanced regional integration would increase the share of electricity trade up to 12 percent of total regional demand under the reference scenario (16 percent when considering a low-carbon development scenario) while ensuring energy security – compared to today’s share of around 3 percent. Such benefits would materialize primarily through avoided capital investments in generation and savings in fuel costs. The economic benefits of integration are therefore significant. 7. Despite renewed interest, the full potential of regional interconnectivity and trade outlined above remains largely underexploited. While there are issues associated with arrears of electricity payments for exports among countries, the current bilateral trade mechanism is not sufficient to sustain growth and enable the significant renewable energy scale- up required in the region due to its lack of transparency and flexibility. D. MPA Description 12. The MPA is built around three pillars and three overlapping phases. The pillars represent the crucial components for developing an integrated regional system: establishing a competitive market (Pillar 1), reinforcing, modernizing, and digitalizing interconnections (Pillar 2), and strengthening the enabling environment and institutional capacity (Pillar 3). Phase 1 (the Pilot Phase) aims to establish and operationalize a pilot day-ahead electricity market, finance critical Page 4 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade (P509488) transmission investments to strengthen the regional grid, and enhance the regional institutional framework. Phases 2 (the Consolidation Phase) and 3 (the Mature Phase) will further support the gradual evolution of the pilot market into a modern market structure with a wide range of market products. Concurrently, this process will inform the establishment and operationalization of regional institutions as well as the full integration of energy systems in the region. 13. The proposed project (MPA Phase 1) is aligned to the broader MPA framework and designed around the following three components: (i) Component 1: Pilot regional electricity market; (ii) Component 2: Reinforcing interconnections and system resilience; and (iii) Component 3: Strengthening the enabling environment and institutional capacity. E. Project Description 14. Component 1: Pilot regional electricity market. This component will finance the development, establishment, and operationalization of a pilot regional market in Central Asia. The pilot market will complement existing long-term bilateral power purchase agreements. The primary objective of the pilot market will be to establish a transparent mechanism that fosters trust in short-term trade. The pilot market can be established within a short timeframe (6–12 months) and has an initial operational period of 1–2 years. All five Central Asian countries could participate in the pilot for Phase 1, with Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyz Republic, and Tajikistan having confirmed their participation (as market participants). Discussions are ongoing with Turkmenistan. The pilot adopts a flexible and gradual approach to trade. A Regional Market Operator will be established to oversee market operations and act as the Central Counterparty for trades _For Official Use Only_ within the pilot market. A commercially available Market Operation Service Provider under a serviced market framework will be procured and financed by the project to act as Regional Market Operator for the pilot. This Regional Market Operator will be hosted by CDC Energia. KOREM (the market operator in Kazakhstan) is expected to play an important role in the pilot implementation, providing knowledge exchanges to CDC Energia and other agencies in the region, given its experience on market implementation and management in Kazakhstan. 15. Component 2: Reinforcement and Digitalization of Regional Interconnections. This component will finance key transmission investments in Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and Kyrgyz Republic. The proposed investments under Pillar 2 of the MPA were identified through a comprehensive least-cost grid development study and agreed with Central Asian countries.4 Together, these phased investments lay the foundation for a modern, reliable, and integrated Central Asian power network—enhancing resilience, unlocking renewable potential, and enabling a regional electricity market consistent with broader development goals. Specifically, Phase 1 will finance in Tajikistan the construction of a new 500 kV transmission line between Dushanbe and Regar, which involves upgrading and expanding substations. In Uzbekistan, Phase 1 will finance (i) the construction of a second transmission line of 220 kV connecting Uzbekistan-Paulgan-Fergana- Sokin, and (ii) reconstruction of the Uzbekistan section of the cross-border 220 kV Kristall (KR) - Yulduz (UZ) line. These lines will be equipped with fiber optic cables that will facilitate the implementation of a modern Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition/Energy Management System and establishment of an optical communication network. In the Kyrgyz Republic, Phase 1 will also finance two prioritized projects aiming to enhance transfer capacity and reliability between Kyrgyz Republic and Uzbekistan. This phase will involve (i) reconstruction of the Kyrgyz section of the cross-border 220 kV Kristall (KR) - Yulduz (UZ) line, and (ii) modernization and rehabilitation of the 220 kV Kristall and Torobaeva substations. The proposed investments under Phase 1 will enable Uzbekistan, the Kyrgyz Republic, and Tajikistan to integrate 900 MW of renewable energy and enhance grid stability. 16.
