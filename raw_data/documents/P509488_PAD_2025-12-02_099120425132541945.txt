Official Use Only Page 8 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT 1. The proposed Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade (REMIT) Program aims to increase regional electricity trade, strengthen transmission capacity, and enable the scale-up of renewable energy in Central Asia. The region is highly dependent on fossil fuels, although it is well endowed with the natural resources needed to shift towards clean energy. With energy demand set to increase by about 30 percent by 2030, and potentially quadruple by 2050, there is a pressing need for a substantial expansion of clean generation capacity to support economic growth, ensure energy security and enable the Region to meet its carbon neutrality targets. However, renewable energy potential is underexploited, and electricity trade remains limited. 2. Expanding the regional electricity trade is critical for Central Asia’s energy security and affordability. By tapping into the region’s diverse and complementary resources—hydropower surpluses from the Kyrgyz Republic and Tajikistan, thermal power from Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, and Uzbekistan, and the rapidly growing solar and wind capacity especially in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan—regional integration along with flexible hydropower can balance seasonal and variable supply, reduce outages, and lower system costs. This creates a more reliable and affordable power supply for businesses and households, while promoting resilience against fuel shocks, climate change and other disruptions. 3. An integrated power system can serve as a powerful driver of job creation and economic growth across Central Asia. Regional electricity trade and integration will accelerate the rollout of hydropower, solar, and wind generation, as well as essential grid upgrades. These investments will spur private sector growth, creating thousands of local jobs by (1) building, digitalizing and upgrading cross-border transmission lines, substations, and regional interconnectors thereby creating direct and indirect employment in engineering, construction, and logistics; (2) operating reliable networks that require skilled jobs in grid management, system operations, and maintenance; (3) supporting a more flexible and interconnected grid to absorb more variable renewable energy (like wind and solar), which generate green jobs in manufacturing, installation, and system integration. Additionally, the increased reliability of integrated regional grids will also help address a key bottleneck to doing business for small and medium industries. It will also strengthen regional economic cooperation by creating mutual benefits that no country can achieve alone, while scaling up private capital mobilization. A well-connected regional power system and integrated market will help strengthen collective energy security, affordability and resilience across Central Asia. Additionally, it will attract larger-scale financing and accelerate private investment, including by enabling the integration of incremental renewable energy sources beyond those which can be integrated at national level. Phase 1 of the Program is expected to enable approximately US$700 million in private capital investments through private sector-driven renewable energy projects. 4. The proposed Program leverages the World Bank Group’s engagement in Central Asia’s energy sector, which encompasses comprehensive support to critical energy sector reforms, strategic investments, private sector mobilization, and regional cooperation under a One World Bank Group (WBG) approach. Through parallel initiatives and instruments, the WBG helps countries across Central Asia to strengthen governance, enhance financial sustainability, and establish transparent, market-oriented frameworks that attract private investment. By combining policy support, risk mitigation mechanisms, technical assistance, and financing from the WBG, the Program advances the region’s energy transition, prioritizes public resources for strategic investments, mobilizes private capital for infrastructure, and reinforces long-term energy security and affordability. A. Regional Context 5. Central Asia is experiencing rapid population and economic growth and is poised to become a significantly larger market as countries aim for higher income levels. With over 70 million people, Central Asia’s regional gross domestic product (GDP) was US$410 billion in 2023, similar to Denmark and Viet Nam. All five countries remain in the ‘middle- income’ bracket, with Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyz Republic in the lower middle-income group. Before the COVID- 19 pandemic, the region’s economy grew an average of 5.5 percent annually, driven largely by fossil fuels exports Official Use Only Page 9 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) (Kazakhstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan). Growth in Central Asia is projected to reach 5.9 percent in 2025 and 5.0 in 2026.1 Several countries are now accelerating economic and social reforms, aiming for diversified and sustainable growth in the coming years. 6. As noted in recent Country Climate and Development Reports (CCDRs) of Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, and Kyrgyz Republic, Central Asian countries need to reduce their reliance on fossil fuels and accelerate energy reforms to foster growth in competitive sectors, enable a sustainable energy transition, and have better prospects for productivity and energy security. There is a common understanding among Central Asian countries of the need for such transition and reforms to put the sectors on a sustainable path and open the door to significant investments in the region. Regional integration can provide economic benefits, contributing to a reliable energy supply that supports economic stability and improves living standards by ensuring households and businesses have access to reliable and affordable power.2 7. Energy security is central to Central Asia economies. Globally, the energy sector is a key sector for job creation, generating more than 67 million jobs in 2023 (IEA, 2024), with the largest increase observed in the clean energy sector. The integration of regional power systems will reduce energy costs while increasing the reliability of services. This in turn will attract private investment, catalyzing greater economic activity and job creation. 8. After decades of fragmentation, Central Asian governments are forging stronger regional cooperation. Following a historic Central Asian heads of States summit in 2018, the countries’ leaders collectively submitted a resolution to the United Nations (UN) General Assembly, calling on the international community to support greater cooperation in the region. Concerted efforts in economic modernization and regional integration are now central in accelerating sustainable growth in Central Asia. Governments specifically committed to accelerating regional cooperation in the energy and water sectors, as exemplified by regional hydropower initiatives such as the Rogun and Kambarata-1 projects. This renewed cooperation is creating the basis for the proposed electricity market program. 9. Central Asian countries are facing mounting climate challenges. These include increases in temperature, extreme weather events, and glacial melt. The likely impacts include the expansion of deserts and arid areas. These will add pressure to natural resources such as pasture, forests, and wildlife, and could contribute to the spread of transboundary pests. The degradation of biodiversity, natural habitats, and ecosystems due to climate stressors may increase the vulnerability of impoverished and rural areas, which often lack the capacity to address these challenges. Key industries such as agriculture and other water-dependent activities will be impacted. Increased temperatures, more intense or frequent extreme weather events, and increased droughts are likely to influence energy demand, hydropower reliability and productivity, and damage energy infrastructure. Collaborative efforts on adaptation and mitigation in Central Asia can facilitate the development and adoption of technologies to address these challenges. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 10. The energy sector is central to the region’s transition to cleaner, more inclusive, and diversified growth. Drawing on its rich hydrocarbon resources, the energy sector in the region has been highly dependent on fossil fuels, although the region is well endowed with natural resources needed to shift towards renewable energy, including hydropower. In 2022, 73 percent of electricity demand in the region was met by fossil fuels. In particular, power systems in Kazakhstan (about 90 percent of generation mix), Uzbekistan (about 90 percent), and Turkmenistan (100 percent) are highly reliant on coal, gas, and oil. 11. Despite providing important benefits in terms of energy security, electricity trade remains limited and renewable energy is underutilized. Increasing energy connectivity and trade could facilitate the scaling up of clean energy deployment and attract international investment. A World Bank regional study (2024) identified least-cost generation and transmission options to meet this demand up to 2050, under various electricity trade scenarios (see Annex 3 for details) and considering decarbonization objectives. 13. Deeper regional connectivity and trade could generate economic benefits of around US$15 billion between 2025 and 2050, under a business-as-usual scenario. Enhanced regional integration would increase the share of electricity trade up to 12 percent of total regional demand under the reference scenario (16 percent when considering a low-carbon development scenario) while ensuring energy security – compared to today’s share of around 3 percent. Such benefits would materialize primarily through avoided capital investments in generation and savings in fuel costs. If countries continue their economic development along a low-carbon pathway, benefits could rise to US$26 billion, driven by enhanced trade and optimization of regional investments. The economic benefits of integration are therefore significant, even without considering decarbonization constraints on the system. 14. Central Asian countries can benefit greatly from developing hydropower and renewable energy, while in parallel expanding electricity trade. Modeling conducted for the CCDRs shows significant benefits in terms of energy security and economic growth when promoting scale-up of electricity trade and renewable energy in the region. Stronger regional integration will also enable greater penetration of renewable energy sources—such as solar and wind—raising their share in the electricity mix to 53 percent by 2050 compared to 39 percent without integration (from 3 percent in 2024). This translates into about 20 gigawatts (GW) of additional renewable energy capacity (a 14 percentage points increase). To support the clean energy transition, ensure affordable energy supply, and enable greater scale-up of renewables, hydropower capacity will need to double by 2050 in Central Asia. The development of flagship regional projects, such as the Kambarata-1 and Rogun hydropower projects, along with large solar and wind developments in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, will require scaling up electricity exchanges and significantly strengthening regional interconnections and planning coordination (see Annex 3). 15. Despite renewed interest, the full potential of regional interconnectivity and trade outlined above remains largely underexploited. Current bilateral trade mechanisms are not sufficient to sustain growth and enable the significant renewable energy scale-up needed, due to their lack of transparency and flexibility. Regional energy trade is hindered by a combination of challenges: (a) Challenge #1. The absence of an effective regional trading platform is a major obstacle, preventing efficient cross-border energy transactions. Regional trade has therefore been limited to marginal volumes (about 1-2 percent of electricity demand) and only through mid- to long-term bilateral contracts, often influenced by political considerations. International experience shows that a well-functioning and transparent energy market is pivotal for reaping the benefits of regional trade. There is a need to capitalize on existing electricity exchanges and use the current framework to pilot effective market mechanisms, setting the basis for the longer-term development of market-based trade. (b) Challenge #2. Aging infrastructure is another significant obstacle to the regional energy agenda. Chronic underinvestment and uncoordinated planning have affected the reliability and performance of the regional grid. The share of facilities aged 30 years or more ranges from 44 percent to 75 percent, and data systems need modernization. During peak operational times in winter, power flow over some lines exceeds their rate capacity, Official Use Only Page 11 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) resulting in more frequent and longer faults, higher total electricity losses at about 15–20 percent, and more numerous and frequent outages at both national and regional levels. A regional blackout in 2022 highlighted these issues when faults in one country caused outages across Central Asia. Lack of coordination across countries exacerbates the grid’s vulnerability. Investments in modernizing and digitalizing the grid are essential to improve stability. Cybersecurity risks – which increase the risk of outages – are an additional concern, given the prevalence of outdated communication networks and limited use of digital technologies. (c) Challenge #3. Institutional fragmentation also poses a significant challenge to the regional cooperation agenda. There is no regional energy institution that comprehensively coordinates energy systems in the region, resulting in a lack of cohesive regulatory frameworks, coordinated investment planning, and harmonized policies across countries. The roles of the Coordinating Energy Council as well as Coordinating Dispatch Center (CDC) Energia are limited to operational and technical matters related to transmission and dispatch businesses in Uzbekistan, Kyrgyz Republic, Tajikistan and Kazakhstan. Kazakhstan and the Kyrgyz Republic are pursuing a common electricity market under the Eurasian Economic Union treaty (which does not include Uzbekistan and Tajikistan), which will need to be taken into account when considering the long-term development of an electricity market in Central Asia. Engagements between the countries so far have been mainly at the technical level. Therefore, a solid regional institutional framework and cooperation among governments is essential to realize the benefits of regional trade. (d) Challenge #4: Continuous reform momentum. Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan rank among the top 25 countries with the largest fossil fuel subsidies, representing 2.7 percent, 6.6 percent, and 3.2 percent of GDP (in 2023) respectively. This led to a vicious cycle of shrinking fiscal space, narrowing resources for investment, and a large portion of sector assets becoming obsolete and needing modernization. It also disincentivized investments in renewable energy, further increasing reliance on fossil fuels to meet growing energy demand. Recent tariff reforms aim to enhance private sector participation, improve competition, and achieve full-cost recovery by 2030 across the region. These efforts include adjusting electricity tariffs, implementing new regulations, and transitioning towards competitive markets to ensure better governance and financial viability of energy sectors, with improved targeting of subsidies toward vulnerable population. Accelerating such reforms is a key ingredient to build trust in trade and reap the benefits of closer regional cooperation. 16. The proposed REMIT Program will support countries in addressing the challenges above through the development and implementation of a fully-fledged regional electricity market in Central Asia, while enabling the scale- up of renewable energy resources. Regional markets vary in scope and often include various offerings (such as day-ahead market, intra-day market, balancing market). Such offerings optimize price transparency and incentivize trade, allowing for the maximization of regional trade benefits. In parallel, regional and market institutions will need to be created, and the regional grid strengthened. Developing a fully-fledged regional electricity market for Central Asia will require concerted effort over several years. By enabling trade, countries can effectively share hydropower, solar, and wind resources, ensuring a reliable and sustainable energy supply. This approach will accelerate the clean energy transition and support energy security. 17. The first phase of this Program aims to (i) establish, operationalize and pilot a regional electricity market in Central Asia; (ii) finance key ready-to-implement “no regret” interconnectivity projects; and (iii) enhance the regional and national institutional frameworks and build their capacity. The key guiding principles for the Central Asia Pilot Market emphasize flexibility, rapid implementation, and compatibility with existing national and regional practices and entities. The Program will finance investments in critical infrastructure and digitalization needed to improve the technical and operational performance of the regional grid, as well as its capacity to better integrate variable renewable energy. The Project (Phase 1) will help establish and operationalize regional institutions as well as enhance the capacity of existing regional and national agencies to develop the proposed pilot regional market. The Program is consistent with the WBG Country Partnership Frameworks (CPFs) for the Kyrgyz Republic, the Republic of Uzbekistan, the Republic of Tajikistan and the Republic of Kazakhstan, and central to the engagement of the WBG in Central Asia, which prioritizes regional connectivity and integration.3 The Program is aligned with the CPF for the Kyrgyz Republic for FY2024–FY2028: Objective 2.2 “Enhance sustainability and increase renewables capacity in the energy sector” and Objective 2.3 “Strengthen infrastructure resilience to climate and disaster risks”; CPF for the Republic of Uzbekistan for FY2022-FY2026: Objective 1.4 “Improve the infrastructure for competitiveness and connectivity”, and Objective 3.1 “Decarbonization and the greener development of industry and the economy”; CPF for the Republic of Tajikistan for FY2019-2024 (as revised through Performance and Learning Review): Objective 8 “Strengthening Regional Connectivity and Integration”; and CPF for the Republic of Kazakhstan for FY2020-FY2025: Objective 3 “Strengthen Connectivity Infrastructure and Regional Services Delivery for Better Local and Regional Integration”. 19. The Program is aligned with and complements other initiatives of development partners in the region. The proposed MPA builds on the technical assistance provided by the former United States Agency for International Development (USAID) through its CAREM program in the past, which aimed to pilot different implementation options for a regional power market. The Project is aligned with the Asian Development Bank (ADB)’s Central Asia Regional Economic Cooperation (CAREC) initiative that aim at enhancing regional energy institutional frameworks and scaling up investments. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO), the Swiss State Secretariat for Economic Affairs (SECO) and European Union (EU) counterparts are also active in supporting the development of a regional power market in Central Asia, including through the Central Asia Water and Energy Program (CAWEP). Discussions are in progress with the European Bank of Reconstruction and Development (EBRD) and ADB about potential financing of complementary investments in future phases of the MPA. 20. The Project is compliant with the Maximizing Finance for Development approach and contributes towards Private Capital Enabled (PCE) corporate scorecard indicator. The most urgent investments under Component 2 of Phase 1 to modernize and digitalize transmission network as well as to strengthen the enabling environment in Tajikistan, the Kyrgyz Republic, and Uzbekistan will strengthen the system stability and support renewable energy integration, including renewable energy capacity developed by the private sector. PCE will be captured by the non-monetary results indicator: “Renewable Energy Capacity Enabled (Megawatt or MW)” and envisages 900 MW in renewable energy capacity enabled with the private sector participation by 2032. Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA) (i) Rationale for Using an MPA 22. An MPA is best suited for the proposed Program considering its scale, complexity, and duration. The use of the MPA is relevant as transitioning from current limited trade, based on bilateral Power Purchase Agreements, to a fully- fledged modern energy market requires focus and investments over a 10-year timeframe to achieve the desired development outcomes. Creating an electricity market is a complex endeavor and requires a phased approach allowing to embed learning from initial stages to capture best practices that can be used to design the next phases of engagement. An MPA is ideal for creating synergies between phases. The MPA approach allows appropriate overlapping between phases to ensure the continuity required in developing new markets and to avoid delays in project implementation. Finally, the MPA provides the flexibility needed to align national and regional investments with the scale and duration of support required. 23. The MPA is structured with an inherent learning agenda. Phase 1 is a deliberate learning pilot that generates the technical, operational, and behavioral evidence to refine market design in Phases 2 and 3. By standing up a simplified regional platform and core “soft” rules (governance, scheduling, settlement) alongside “hard” enablers (automated metering/MDMS, grid digitalization, fiber communications), Phase 1 produces real transaction, performance, and user‑experience data to calibrate product scope, algorithms, and standards. These lessons are codified into Phase 2 entry conditions and milestones, guiding the advanced day‑ahead/intraday market design and, subsequently, the fuller market (including regional balancing) in Phase 3—ensuring solutions are Central Asia‑fit before scaling. 24. The MPA also sequences institutional learning by testing roles and processes in Phase 1 and using structured stakeholder engagement to surface bottlenecks and political economy constraints early . Performance against clear milestones and political agreements then informs Phase 2 reforms, including adjustments to mandates, collaboration frameworks, and third‑party access, with relevant triggers embedded to move the needle on reform in lockstep with market upgrades. Across all phases, through its Regional Components, the MPA functions as a standing learning platform—combining monitoring and evaluation, midterm reviews, targeted technical assistance, preparatory studies, and capacity building—to translate pilot results into bankable next‑phase operations. Regular peer exchange and knowledge sharing, benchmarked to international experience, bring countries together to compare diagnostics, align on standards, and co‑solve implementation challenges, maintaining continuous feedback loops so each phase benefits from lessons learned and accelerates progress toward an integrated, efficient regional market. Under the MPA approach, broader lessons learned, and analytical works will flow seamlessly between participating countries. High-level policy engagement is built into the MPA design to ensure that regional integration is prioritized by participating countries. Strong coordination and information flows among stakeholders, including national governments, regional institutions, and development partners are essential to maintain alignment and momentum, which will be facilitated by the MPA long- term and multi-country approach. 25. An MPA signals to key stakeholders that the World Bank is supporting this agenda for the long term, which is critical to securing financing from other partners and the private sector. This is strategically relevant for soliciting longer- term financing commitments not only from other international financial institutions (IFIs) and development partners, but also from the private sector and commercial lenders in the later phases of the Program. It is also worth noting that the duration of the MPA aligns with the expected commissioning dates of the Rogun and Kambarata-1 HPPs, creating strong synergy between these projects. An established regional electricity market and expanded interconnection capacity will enable power to be effectively evacuated from Rogun and Kambarata-1. Phase 2 and Phase 3 of the MPA focus on strengthening the grid to ensure adequate redundancies in the evacuation of power from both plants. 26. Component 1: Pilot regional electricity market (US$2.2 million – Central Asia Water and Energy Program Grant). This component will finance the development, establishment, and operationalization of a pilot regional market in Central Asia. The MPA aims to support the development and evolution of a comprehensive regional electricity market in Central Asia. The Bank team has collaborated with countries to develop a detailed roadmap for the transition to a fully-fledged regional electricity market. The Roadmap, summarized in Annex 1, outlines a systematic approach to developing a regional electricity market, addressing political, technical, and institutional requirements in each phase. The ultimate objective is to transition from a pilot market in MPA Phase 1 to a fully integrated regional market in Phase 2 and Phase 3, thereby enhancing cooperation and efficiency within the electricity sector. 37. The proposed pilot market design is the result of close collaboration between the World Bank Group and Central Asian stakeholders, supported by a consortium of consultancy firms led by Nord Pool (one of Europe’s leading market operators). The pilot market will not negatively affect any existing trade arrangements, allowing all legacy contracts to co-exist with the introduction of the pilot. Similarly, all new bilateral contracts under discussion or negotiation will not be affected by the pilot. This initial phase is not focused on achieving large trade volumes. Instead, the primary objective is to establish a transparent mechanism that fosters trust in short-term trade. By doing so, the groundwork is laid for a more robust trading environment. Government Working Groups from all five Central Asian countries were established as part of the preparation process and members of these groups actively participated in developing the design of the pilot market.4 The proposed pilot aims to have strong demonstrational effect. The pilot market can be established within a short timeframe (6–12 months) and has an initial operational period of 1–2 years. 38. All five Central Asian countries could participate in the pilot for Phase 1, with Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Kyrgyz Republic, and Tajikistan having confirmed their participation (as market participants). Discussions are ongoing with Turkmenistan. The pilot adopts a flexible and gradual approach to trade. Component 3: Strengthening the institutional framework and enabling environment (US$1 million - Central Asia Water and Energy Program grant). Pillar 3 will support the creation of a regional institutional framework to promote collaboration on energy and build capacity in country and regional institutions. Given the current institutional fragmentation in the region, the Bank team in consultation with countries has carried out an assessment of potential institutional options to underpin regional cooperation in energy. The assessment evaluates the long-term institutional arrangements for cooperation in energy in the region. 50. A phased institutional transition is emerging as the most effective path forward. In Phase 1 (short term), the MPA will be integrated within the current regional institutional framework for energy cooperation, formalizing existing cooperation mechanisms among countries through the creation of a Central Asia Energy Council. This approach leverages recent agreements among countries to establish an energy body responsible for coordinating energy initiatives and projects throughout the region, though implementation will require time. Phase 1 will facilitate the formation of a ministerial-level Electricity Coordination Council, consisting of the Energy Ministers of Central Asia. This Council will serve as the governing entity for regional energy system planning, enhancing regional connectivity, promoting trade, optimizing the use of complementary resources, and furthering the development goals of both individual countries and the region collectively. The Energy Council will determine the institutional structure for both a regional planning coordination body and a regional regulatory authority. During this phase, the CDC Energia will serve as the regional system and market operator for the pilot market. Given the existing practice of Energy ministers convening on an ad-hoc basis, this strategy offers a practical starting point and establishes a strong foundation for the evolution of a comprehensive regional institutional framework over the long term. 51. In Phase 2 (medium term), the MPA will help consolidate and formalize regional governance by backing the Energy Council to oversee market expansion and sector reforms and establish other regional bodies. Those will include (i) a regional planning coordination body; (ii) a regional regulatory body; and (iii) a regional system operator body (which could be an expansion of the CDC Energia). The Energy Council could serve as a standalone regional organization overseeing regional cooperation and market development in energy or merge with other regional institutions under an expanded mandate. These potential and practical institutional options in Central Asia’s context will be discussed and developed in consultation with the countries in the region. The REMIT MPA will finance capacity building for existing entities and the establishment and operationalization of new regional planning and regulatory bodies, including support to define mandates, decision rights, and coordination protocols. The MPA also facilitates the decision on the long-term market operator (CDC Energia or an alternative agreed option) by pairing institutional diagnostics with hands-on implementation support, regular peer exchanges, and feedback loops from market operations—ensuring institutional reforms advance in lockstep with market integration. 52. Finally, by Phase 3 (long term), the MPA’s sequenced support culminates in fully established and functional regional institutions underpinning a sustainable regional electricity market . Harmonized policies and regulations, clear regional roles, and stable operational arrangements enable deeper system integration and a mature suite of market products. The MPA’s role here is to lock in institutional resilience—codifying standards, strengthening compliance and oversight functions, and sustaining cross-country collaboration mechanisms—so the regional framework endures and delivers shared benefits across Central Asia. 53. E. Lessons Learned 59. The design of the REMIT MPA was informed by the experiences and lessons drawn from several similar regional initiatives. Notable among these are the CASA-1000 Project (P145054), the Southern Africa Power Pool–Africa Renewable Energy Program (SAPP-AREP, P126661), and the Regional Energy Transmission, Trade, and Decarbonization Project in the Southern Africa Region (RETRADE, P175190). These projects underscored the importance of adopting a holistic, region- wide perspective while simultaneously identifying and supporting national-level initiatives that yield broader regional benefits. 60. This approach helped shape REMIT’s dual focus as both a horizontal and vertical MPA, supporting phased development within individual countries and at regional level . The MPA framework will strengthen learning among different phases, ensuring a coordinated approach among countries on regional transmission investments and electricity market development. Additionally, the Program design drew valuable insights from country-level initiatives in Central Asia and from ongoing programmatic technical assistance efforts, especially the “Scaling-up Regional Energy Interconnectivity in Central Asia” (P178748). These sources provided practical guidance on aligning national energy strategies with regional interconnection goals. Furthermore, key stakeholders in Central Asia already visited Europe to benefit from a capacity- building workshop to learn more about technical challenges in operating a large market exchange. Other capacity-building events are planned to take place in Asia and other regions where countries have valuable experience in overcoming challenges related to market design, infrastructure development, regulatory frameworks, and stakeholder engagement. 61. Successful regional electricity markets require strong cooperation and coordination among participating countries. Geopolitical factors such as access to energy resources, planning and development of infrastructure required for regional electricity markets, and political stability of countries can significantly impact the energy security of a region. To facilitate such development, there is a need for political commitment in Central Asia toward regional energy cooperation, including through piloting and ultimately fully-fledged integration of market principles. The fully-fledged electricity market includes harmonizing regulations, standards, and market rules to ensure smooth operation and efficient trading. Therefore, high-level discussions between Central Asian governments are important prerequisites to scale up regional power trade in the region. 62. Proposed market arrangements need to ensure flexibility and simplicity in design, taking into account current electricity trade arrangements and other complementary activities. While developing a fully-fledged regional electricity market for Central Asia in phases may take several years, international experience shows there is a strong rationale to start with a simplified market trading platform to capture short-term benefits, learn important and practical lessons on Official Use Only Page 24 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade (P509488) what would best fit the context of electricity trading, and provide a proof-of-concept on which additional products could be developed over time based on the evolving context. The pilot supported by the MPA is expected to be a ‘proof-of- concept’ to conceptualize and test preliminary elements of a regional electricity market in Central Asia with minimum to no changes in the current setup of the countries’ power systems. Other relevant initiatives evolving in Central Asia have been taken into consideration, including the Common Electricity Market of the Eurasian Economic Union, the proposed Tripartite Gas Union (as this affects the power sectors as well), Turkmenistan-Uzbekistan-Tajikistan-Afghanistan-Pakistan and CASA-1000 (Central Asia South Asia Electricity Transmission and Trade) projects. Given the long tenure of the MPA phases, the Program needs to be flexible to adapt design to changing circumstances. 63. The Program has already built upon the key findings of the former USAID and ADB initiatives and plans to extend the outreach to other IFIs (including the EBRD and the European Investment Bank) to explore joint financing of priority investments for Phase 2 and 3 as well as support for trade-oriented reforms. Trust and cooperation will support the development of a fully-fledged regional electricity market, facilitating enhanced connectivity and trade, and ultimately ensuring the long-term sustainability of Central Asia’s energy market. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis 72. Phase 1 of the MPA is expected to help strengthen infrastructure connectivity in the region and address challenges the power sector faces, including energy security, unreliable supply, operational inefficiencies, and vulnerability to climate change. Phase 1 consists of relative standards investments, such as construction or rehabilitation of 500 kV and 220 kV lines, and extension and modernization of existing substations and control and communication systems. These are all well- proven technologies that do not present technical risks or issues. While Component 1 is innovative for Central Asia, the associated technologies and investments have been well proven in other regions where power pool and market exchanges are common. 73. The economic feasibility of the operations is assessed using standard cost-benefit analyses, which are evaluated in terms of the net present value (NPV) and Economic Internal Rate of Return (EIRR). The analyses use a social discount rate of 6 percent to present all costs and benefits at a common point in time, with economic costs and benefits expressed in constant USD 2023. In line with World Bank guidelines, results are reported with and without the impact of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by applying shadow prices of carbon (SPCs) from the low and high price trajectories defined in the World Bank’s updated guidance note on “The Shadow Price of Carbon in Economic Analysis.” 74. A standard value for incremental Operations and Maintenance was assumed to be 2 percent of the total capital costs. Inflation was not included in the analysis. The economic lifetime of the investments was assumed to be 30 years after construction completion (through 2058). 75. The main economic benefits of the project are the following: The Program’s quantified benefits are driven by: the value of additional power flows enabled by new and upgraded transmission assets (scaled dynamically with demand and priced per MWh), reductions in overall power production costs through cheaper generation and import substitution enabled by a more flexible grid, lower technical losses, and avoided unserved energy as outages decrease; together these also reduce GHG emissions from loss reductions, with potential further gains from higher renewable integration. Beyond these, the program is expected to yield significant but hard-to-quantify economic benefits from enhanced regional integration, power trade, and institutional strengthening. 76. The results show that the proposed projects are economically viable. The Project generates an NPV of US$720.3 million and an EIRR of 30.7 percent, without considering GHG emission savings. After accounting for GHG emission savings, the NPV increases to US$778.3 million and the EIRR to 31.9 percent at the low trajectory of shadow prices of carbon, and US$836.5 and 33.1 percent respectively at the high trajectory of shadow prices of carbon. Gender. Women in Central Asia are making progress in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) fields through innovations in areas like programming, building satellites, and engineering software. However, women still face unique challenges in STEM fields throughout their childhoods, education, and careers. For example, in Kazakhstan, only 25 percent of energy sector jobs are held by women, and women still encounter cultural and social barriers when entering technical and management fields. Women remain significantly underrepresented in technical and managerial positions in the energy sector across Central Asia. In Tajikistan’s national utility, Barqi Tojik, women comprise only around 11 percent of managerial staff and 12 percent of engineers, while overall female employment in the energy sector stands at just 11 percent. In Uzbekistan, women account for approximately 15 percent of the energy workforce, with an estimated 10 percent in leadership roles. In the Kyrgyz Republic, women represent about 18 percent of the energy workforce, though evidence suggests their presence is concentrated in non-technical and administrative roles. These figures highlight persistent gender gaps in both technical and managerial tracks, stemming not only from cultural and social barriers but also from a lack of practical hands-on experience, mentorship, and career support opportunities for women. 96. To help address these gaps, the Project will integrate, where feasible, gender activities aligned with the World Bank’s gender priorities. A tailored internship program, targeted at female students, will be offered by NEGK, SIB, NEGU under the Component 2. The program will be open to both students and early-career professionals. The internships will provide practical technical training, mentorship, and exposure to managerial pathways in the energy sectors. This will also include collaboration with educational institutions to identify and encourage female graduates to participate in the training program. Through these measures, the project aims to increase women’s employment opportunities in technical and managerial positions in the energy sector. Given that this is a new initiative for the implementing agencies, the specific design features will be developed during implementation, tested, tailored to each country’s context and documented in the Project Operations Manual as the program evolves. Progress will be measured through the indicator: “Number of newly employed women in technical and managerial roles within relevant electricity companies (NEGK, SIB, NEGU)”. 97. Citizen engagement. 42 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) Detailed Description of MPA Program Pillar 1: Building a competitive market in Central Asia 1. This MPA Pillar aims to support the development and evolution of a comprehensive regional electricity market in Central Asia over the duration of the MPA. The Bank team has collaborated with countries to develop a detailed roadmap for market transition. The Roadmap outlines a systematic approach to developing a regional electricity market in Central Asia, addressing political, technical, and institutional requirements in each phase. The ultimate objective is to transition from a pilot market to a fully integrated regional market, thereby enhancing cooperation and efficiency within the electricity sector. Phase 1 and the tentative scope for Phase 2 and Phase 3 is outlined below. 2. Phase 1 will focus on the implementation of a pilot market. The pilot market is structured to ensure optimal short- term resource allocation, enhance the integration of renewable generation, and reduce overall generation costs while increasing the security of supply. The market is designed as a Net Pool market for national deficit or surplus trade, primarily focusing on a day-ahead market. This segment allows for efficient cross-border trading of national surplus or deficit by each country’s discretion. 3. The pilot market will use an existing regional institution as the market operator for the simplest and fastest implementation. The CDC Energia is identified as the most potential fit for this role. 4. The national transmission system operators are responsible for the scheduling, commitment, and dispatch of generation to meet their country's internal demand, including long-term bilateral agreements for imports and exports. The pilot market design allows for all types of internal markets in each country, from centralized to decentralized dispatch. The market will consist of one or more bidding zones for each country. The initial configuration follows the borders of the participating countries, but countries like Kazakhstan can opt for more bidding zones if preferred. The bidding zone configuration may be reviewed and analyzed at each stage, ensuring flexibility to adapt to any changes. 5. The pilot market introduces a day-ahead market as an additional short-term market for electricity. This market segment allows electricity to be traded one day before delivery, with a separate market clearing price for each of the 24 hours. Existing bilateral contracts will remain in place, and new bilateral contracts are allowed. 6. The Central Asia Regional Market Operator will act as the central counterparty for the organized market segment, ensuring a transparent, accessible, and reliable unified system of financial settlement. Settlement is to be done daily, with the market settlement system including the calculation of traded amounts, trading fees, and any other applicable fees. Sufficient reporting in the market includes trade and settlement results for participants, scheduling reports, available capacity data, and market-monitoring reports. Minimum data to be published should include the price for each market time unit, total trade volume, and scheduled cross-border flows. 7. The primary purpose and criteria for the design of the Central Asia regional power market are optimal short-term resource allocation, increased efficiency, enhanced integration of renewable generation, and reduced overall generation costs, including increased security of supply. The concept is based on creating economic merit orders while taking transmission constraints and existing contracts into account. Key principles of the proposed pilot are outlined below. Page 43 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) Figure 1.1: Key Principles of the Proposed Pilot Market 8. Market participant categories will be established and agreed with counterparts at the beginning of the implementation phase to allow eligible participants to join when permitted by both national regulations and regional rules. These categories will accommodate any eligible participants, provided they comply with their national legislation on cross- border trading. Potential participants include national utilities, single buyers, and independent power producers. The primary participants will include system operators responsible for transmission and managing transmission capacities. Parties will need to meet the agreed minimum eligibility criteria, such as having a minimum MW capacity for either generation or consumption. This setup will ensure a structured and inclusive approach to market participation. Further technical details about the pilot design are available upon request. 9. Phase 2 will entail the transition to an Advanced Market (a day-ahead market and intraday market). This will require a gradual shift from bilateral trade to an organized regional market, updating intergovernmental agreements, and ensuring the financial viability of utilities. Technically, it involves investment in hardware and software, enhancing data sharing, and updating methodologies. Institutionally, it demands increasing institutional readiness, advancing market regulation and oversight, and establishing cooperative mechanisms. Key activities include analyzing pilot market experience, designing and implementing advanced market structures, launching the intraday market, and harmonizing national grid codes. 10. Finally, Phase 3 will involve the transition to a fully-fledged market. This will necessitate finalizing unbundling of power utilities across Central Asia and opening national markets, ensuring third-party access, and improving intergovernmental agreements. Technical requirements include developing a regional cross-border electricity grid master plan, ensuring open access to data, and harmonizing grid codes. Institutionally, it will involve increasing institutional readiness, ensuring the financial viability of utilities, and establishing regional cooperation of regulators. Key activities include launching regional balancing markets, improving existing market segments, implementing technical requirements, and integrating national markets with the regional market. Developing a regional fiber optic network for energy market services in Central Asia offers significant benefits by improving the monitoring and control of energy infrastructure, enhancing reliability and efficiency. The new 500 kV and 220 kV transmissions line to be financed will incorporate optical ground wire and optical fibers will be installed on vital 220-500 kV regional interconnection. This optical fiber network will facilitate real-time grid management, enable better integration of renewable energy sources and supporting energy trading across borders. The fiber optic network also promotes energy efficiency through smart grid technologies and data-driven insights. It enables remote operations and maintenance, particularly in remote or hazardous areas, reducing costs and improving safety. Furthermore, the network supports the deployment of advanced energy management systems, empowering consumers to optimize their energy consumption. It will foster long-term competition, price transparency, and market liquidity in the regional energy market. The optical network also facilitates the integration of diverse energy resources, reducing reliance on fossil fuels. It enables the collection and analysis of large volumes of energy data, informing decision-making processes, and supporting predictive maintenance. Overall, the development of a regional fiber optic network for energy market services in Central Asia will contribute to a more resilient, efficient, and sustainable operational network. 14. Implementing a regional power system control, monitoring, and automation infrastructure yields numerous advantages. It enhances grid stability by facilitating real-time monitoring and proactive measures to prevent outages. It boosts reliability by minimizing human errors and enabling a rapid response to disturbances. Automation ensures efficient load balancing, matching supply with demand to prevent overloads, thus improving energy efficiency by optimizing generation, transmission, and distribution processes. This automation will support the integration of renewable energy sources by enabling dynamic control and demand-response programs, adjusting consumption in real time to fluctuations. Furthermore, such systems enhance grid resilience by enabling quick isolation and restoration during emergencies. Remote monitoring and management capabilities reduce the need for on-site inspections, enhancing operational efficiency. Automated systems reduce operational costs by streamlining processes and optimizing resource utilization. They (i) facilitate fault detection and diagnosis, reducing downtime; (ii) support smart grid initiatives, enabling advanced functionalities like two-way communication and grid optimization; (iii) provide data analytics from automation that can predict failures and optimize maintenance, improving asset management; and (iv) improve cybersecurity measures to ensure grid integrity. 15. Introducing new technology for the digitalization of regional energy networks offers substantial benefits for enhancing regional cooperation. Firstly, it enables real-time monitoring and control, enhancing grid stability and reliability that supports the integration of renewable energy sources, optimizing resource utilization across borders. Digital platforms facilitate (i) efficient cross-border energy trading, improving market transparency and cooperation; and (ii) demand response for enabling better management of peak loads and grid stability. Digitalization strengthens grid resilience and security against physical and cyber threats, fostering trust among participating countries. Furthermore, it promotes energy efficiency through real-time monitoring and optimization of energy consumption by supporting cross- border interconnection projects. It facilitates alignment of interests and investments, optimizing the planning and implementation of energy infrastructure projects by promoting efficiency and cooperation. 16. Reinforcing domestic transmission networks and regional interconnectors is crucial for enhancing regional energy security. Financing cross-border interconnections enables increased energy exchange between neighboring countries, promoting regional cooperation and diversification of energy sources. Such diversification reduces dependence on a single supplier, enhancing resilience to supply disruptions. Reinforcement of national and regional networks (i) optimizes national grid operation by reducing congestion and expanding transit capacity from regional interconnectors; (ii) enhances overall system reliability and stability; and (iii) facilitates integration of energy markets, promoting Page 46 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) competition, efficiency, and better resource allocation. These grid reinforcements are essential to improve the reliability and capacity of electricity flows from key generation centers (including Rogun HPP) and reduce transmission bottlenecks. They also provide much-needed redundancy, ensuring system security and operational flexibility under both normal and contingency conditions. 27. In parallel, the program supports the integration of Central Asian power systems by modernizing key substations (e.g., Kristall and Torobaeva in the Kyrgyz Republic), enabling stable and secure cross-border power exchanges, and reinforcing the energy supply to densely populated and industrially significant regions such as Uzbekistan’s Fergana Valley. By improving injection capacity and enabling higher utilization of generation resources, these investments are aligned with the broader regional cooperation agenda and initiatives such as CASA-1000. 28. Equally important is the digitalization component, which aims to establish the foundational infrastructure required for a transparent, market-oriented, and flexible electricity system. This includes the deployment of advanced metering infrastructure and an automated metering data management system, facilitating accurate monitoring, billing, and data-driven decision-making—critical enablers of regional electricity trading. Further investments in automated load- frequency control systems and contingency management tools, along with flexible hydropower, will improve real-time grid balancing, reduce reliance on spinning reserves, and support the integration of variable renewable energy sources such as wind and solar. Together, these measures enhance overall grid efficiency, economic dispatch, and system resilience, supporting the region’s transition to a modernized and sustainable power sector. Given the current institutional fragmentation in the region, the Bank team has carried out an assessment of potential institutional options to underpin regional cooperation in energy. 45. Currently, the key regional organizations on energy issues are the following: (a) Coordination Electrical Power Council of Central Asia (CPC) . A consultative body for coordinating the parallel operation of power systems in Central Asia. It provides a platform for mutually agreed decisions and rules to ensure economic, mutually beneficial, and reliable parallel operation of power systems are adopted by the participants and are binding. It supports the coordination of operational activities of power systems and energy facilities in Central Asia. The CPC is the governing body of the Coordination Dispatch Center ‘Energia’ (see below), founded by the transmission companies of participating countries. Its main mandate includes adoption and amendment of the CDC statute, or approval of a new version of the statute, making decisions on the reorganization of the CDC, determination of the main activities of the CDC, and resolving other issues related to its mandate. (b) Coordination Dispatch Center “Energia” (CDC Energia). An independent, non-commercial, international organization, founded by state electricity companies of the Republic of Kazakhstan, Kyrgyz Republic, Republic of Tajikistan, and Republic of Uzbekistan, headquartered in Tashkent (Uzbekistan). 46. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union, the function of overseeing energy flows between the electricity grids of Central Asia was assigned to CDC “Energia” in 1993. Governance was assigned to the CPC, comprising the heads of the national power systems and responsible for the administration and coordination of parallel operations of the Central Asian Power System. The CDC is responsible for coordinating power exchanges between the countries in the Central Asian Power System. CDC is a non-governmental, non-profit organization. Current CDC participants are: Kazakhstan Electric Network Management Company “KEGOC”, National Electric Grid of Uzbekistan, National Electric Grid of the Kyrgyz Republic, and Barki Tojik (Tajikistan). 47. A phased institutional transition is emerging as the most effective path forward. In Phase 1 (short term), the MPA will be integrated within the current regional institutional framework for energy cooperation, formalizing existing cooperation mechanisms among countries through the creation of a Central Asia Energy Council. This approach leverages recent agreements among countries to establish an energy body responsible for coordinating energy initiatives and projects throughout the region, though implementation will require time. Phase 1 will facilitate the formation of a ministerial-level Electricity Coordination Council, consisting of the Energy Ministers of Central Asia. This Council will serve as the governing entity for regional energy system planning, enhancing regional connectivity, promoting trade, optimizing the use of complementary resources, and furthering the development goals of both individual countries and the region collectively. Building on the activities of Component 3 of the project, the Energy Council will determine the institutional structure for both a regional planning coordination body and a regional regulatory authority. During this phase, CDC will serve as the system and market operator for the pilot market. Given the existing practice of energy ministers convening on an ad hoc basis, this strategy offers a practical starting point and establishes a strong foundation for the evolution of a comprehensive regional institutional framework over the long term. 48. In Phase 2 (medium term), the MPA will help consolidate and formalize regional governance by backing the Energy Council to oversee market expansion and sector reforms and establish other regional bodies. Those will include (i) a regional planning coordination body; (ii) a regional regulatory body; and (iii) a regional system operator body (which could Page 55 The World Bank Regional Electricity Market Interconnectivity and Trade Project (P509488) be an expansion of the CDC). The Energy Council and other regional bodies could be integrated over time under the Council of the Heads of State of Central Asia for Energy & Water Issues. In alternative, the Energy Council could serve as standalone regional organization overseeing regional cooperation and market development in energy. The REMIT MPA will finance capacity building for existing entities and the establishment and operationalization of new regional planning and regulatory bodies, including support to define mandates, decision rights, and coordination protocols. The MPA also facilitates the decision on the long term market operator (CDC or an alternative agreed option) by pairing institutional diagnostics with hands-on implementation support, regular peer exchanges, and feedback loops from market operations—ensuring institutional reforms advance in lockstep with market integration. 49. Finally, by Phase 3 (long term), the MPA’s sequenced support culminates in fully established and functional regional institutions underpinning a sustainable regional electricity market. Harmonized policies and regulations, clear regional roles, and stable operational arrangements enable deeper system integration and a mature suite of market products. The MPA’s role here is to lock in institutional resilience—codifying standards, strengthening compliance and oversight functions, and sustaining cross-country collaboration mechanisms—so the regional framework endures and delivers shared benefits across Central Asia. The World Bank conducted a study titled “Decarbonization through Enhanced Regional Electricity Trade in Central Asia”, which explores power sector decarbonization pathways in the context of regional integration. The study aims to support policy makers and the World Bank by leveraging key findings and quantitative data on the potential benefits of regional integration in Central Asia. The study explores the impact of two relevant “policy” dimensions that can shape the future evolution of Central Asia’s power systems: the level of regional integration (three incremental degrees of integration are investigated) and the decarbonization ambitions of the country power sectors (no decarbonization targets against power sector carbon neutrality by 2050). The combination of the above dimensions led to the exploration of six main scenarios, which were analyzed and presented in a detailed manner. Methodology 2. This model-based exploration is interpreted and presented in the context of supporting the coordination of actions and decision-making of the Central Asian countries. It is not meant to provide “predictions”, but to show the type and magnitude of the “response” of the Central Asia (and country-specific) power system, subject to different planning pathways (in particular, the role of regional integration to ease the decarbonization of the systems). The model-based exploration returns the “optimal” generation technology mix and trades (directions and quantities), as well as the associated economic indicators (costs), that meet the region demands under the above described six different scenarios. The electricity demands, over the analyzed time periods, are exogenously determined (assumption) for each country system based on econometric projection. 4. Three alternative (incremental) degrees of integration are analyzed, which “mimic” three different market integrations (for the evaluation of decision-makers and stakeholders): (a) National optimization (-N): Trading limited to current practices (2020–2022 levels). System expansion based on national master plans until 2030 and optimized thereafter. This is in essence the counterfactual business-as-usual scenario to be compared with higher integration alternatives. (b) Trade optimization (-T): Optimal trading, considering existing and planned interconnections. Only committed investments are considered as planned. Optimal system expansion does not include additional interconnectors, beyond those already planned. (c) Regional optimization (-R): Optimal system expansion from a regional perspective, including interconnection capacity between Central Asian countries. Only committed investments are considered as planned. Meanwhile, an upper bound of 30 percent of net import dependency is used for all countries from the energy security perspectives, based on discussion with countries. Key Findings 5. By 2050, electricity demand in Central Asia is projected to double under the Reference Scenario [R] (without decarbonization targets), reaching around 590 terawatt-hours (TWh), and to almost quadruple under the decarbonization scenario (with a power sector net-zero target by 2050), reaching about 1,100 TWh – up from 270 TWh in 2024, due to the electrification of systems. 6. Better regional integration would increase the share of electricity trade in total regional demand to up to 12 percent under the reference scenario (16 percent under the decarbonization scenario), while also ensuring energy security, compared to a much lower trade under current practices of around 3 percent. 7. Stronger regional connectivity and trade could generate economic benefits of around US$15 billion between 2025 and 2050 under the reference growth scenario, primarily through avoided capital investments in fossil fuel generation and fuel cost savings. Under the decarbonization scenario, these benefits rise to US$26 billion, driven by increased gains from enhanced trade and significant advantages from regional investment optimization. It is therefore worth highlighting that the economic benefits of integration are significant, even without transferring a decarbonization signal to the system. This higher level of regional integration would reduce the cost of energy supply by around 10 percent, enhancing energy affordability for both people and businesses. 8. Under the “Reference” (R) scenario, overall, the total installed capacity (including battery) to meet the electricity demand is projected to be around 150 GW in 2040 and 230 GW in 2050 (up from around 60 GW in 2022), across all the variants (level of integration), with a gradual uptake of variable renewable energy sources over the period, and still a strong reliance on gas-fired plants. However, a higher level of integration would facilitate the deployment of an additional 19 GW of variable renewable energy sources capacity by 2050. More interesting “policy” insights can be extracted by the comparative assessment of the responses of (and against) the Net-Zero (NZ) cases. Overall, the total installed capacity (including battery), to meet the electricity demand (assumption) under the NZ trajectory in the region is projected to be around 220–230 GW in 2040 and 490–520 GW in 2050 (twofold the value of the Reference case), with large uptake of renewable energy sources covering around 65 percent to 70 percent of the total installed capacity in 2050 and of the generation stock. Higher levels of integration allow for better optimization of investment and use of renewable energy sources across the region. As a result, the requirement for thermal capacity in the system decreases by 20 percent when the level of integration increases. 10. Stronger regional integration also enables greater penetration of renewable energy sources – such as solar and wind – raising their share in the electricity mix to 53 percent by 2050 compared to 39 percent without integration (from 3 percent in 2024). This translates into 14 percentage points higher or around 20 GW of additional renewable energy capacity. To support the clean energy transition, ensure affordable energy supply, and enable greater scale-up of renewables, hydropower capacity will need to double by 2050 in Central Asia. The development of flagship regional projects, such as the Kambarata-1 and Rogun hydro projects, along with large solar and wind parks in Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, will require scaling up electricity exchanges and significantly strengthening regional interconnections and planning coordination within Central Asia. Regional Transmission Master Plan 11. The least-cost analysis for the period 2030 to 2040 has explored various scenarios to optimize and expand regional trade through existing transmission infrastructure and new interconnections. The Regional Transmission Master Plan has evaluated the necessary investments for expansion and reinforcement at both national and regional levels under each scenario. The study estimates total investment requirements of about US$9 billion to modernize and expand the region’s energy networks and operationalize regional market by 2040, of which around US$2 billion are assessed as priority investments. Of this, the proposed REMIT program, focuses on US$1,018 million “no regret” projects, that are essential for strengthening and modernizing regional power systems and laying foundations for gradual market development in Central Asia. These investments constitute a phased strategy: each project delivers immediate national benefits while positioning Central Asia to fully realize the advantages of a regional connectivity and electricity market when it matures. This underscores the critical need for continuous investment to ensure energy security and reliability as well as to significantly enhance cross-border trade volumes. A forward-looking approach, supported by strategic policies and consistent funding, will be essential to meeting the evolving demands of regional trade. Prioritizing these investments will enable deeper economic integration, fostering broader economic benefits across the region. 12. Simulations have identified opportunities to boost regional power trade by establishing new interconnections between the region’s countries to strengthen and expand existing transit capacities. These new connections are aimed at optimizing the flow of electricity across borders, thereby improving the efficiency of regional power markets. By integrating these new interconnections, the region will achieve a more interconnected and resilient grid and thus benefit from enhanced stability, greater reliability, and increased economic opportunities through more robust and flexible power trading. Additionally, this expansion will enhance energy resource management and support the integration of renewable energy sources, fostering a more sustainable and robust power system. 13. With the planned investments, the region’s transmission capacity could triple by 2040, significantly reinforcing the regional interconnection network. This expansion will enhance cross-border electricity trade, optimizing the use of these connections by up to 76 percent. The increased interconnections will enable more balanced energy exchanges, strengthening overall grid stability. In the event of network failures or disruptions, the well-distributed and resilient infrastructure will help maintain reliability, securing the regional energy market and ensuring more efficient and dependable power exchanges.
