Introduction and Country context 1. Mali is a landlocked and fragile country with a rapidly growing population of about 22.6 million and a per capita gross domestic product (GDP) of US$907.6 in 2022. Mali is ranked 186 out of 191 countries in the United Nation’s Human Development Index in 2022. The country is experiencing one of the highest population growths in the world averaging 2.9 percent a year, and its population is projected to double by 2045, increasing the demand for social and infrastructure services, including energy services. Meanwhile, 40 percent of the population continues to live under the national poverty line and 90 percent reside in southern rural areas. There is a large wealth disparity between rural and urban areas with significant associated gender gaps. With a median age of around 16 years, youth unemployment represents a major challenge, as the number of young job seekers is estimated at 300,000 per year, while the formal sector can only provide around 50,000 jobs per year. These issues have been exacerbated recently by security and political crises, with additional socioeconomic vulnerabilities particularly in rural areas of the country.1 2. Mali’s economy experienced limited inclusive growth over the last decade but remained highly vulnerable to external, climatic, and political shocks. Annual GDP growth was relatively limited prior to the 2020 pandemic, averaging 4.5 percent over 2014-2019 (1.6 percent per capita) driven by private consumption and public investments in roads and social infrastructures. The economy experienced little structural change over the last decade and remains dominated by agriculture and low-productivity services, while manufacturing is small and concentrated in agro-industries and cotton ginning. This combined with the high concentration of exports in gold and cotton, exposes the country to commodity prices and climatic shocks. The low GDP per capita growth also limited progress in poverty reduction while human development indicators show mixed results. These challenges have been further exacerbated since 2020 with the COVID-19 pandemic, external shocks, growing insecurity, and political crises. Annual GDP growth averaged 1.2 percent over 2020-2022 (-1.7 percent per capita) because of the aforementioned shocks. These crises have also significantly affected the most vulnerable households. The pandemic for instance is estimated2 to have increased the poverty rate by 4.8 percentage points, pushing 900,000 more people below the national poverty line. 3. Mali has been experiencing instability and conflict since the 2012 security crisis, exacerbated by the recent military coups of August 2020 and May 2021. Mali has become increasingly fragile over the past 10 years. Since the 2012 rebellion, violent extremist upsurge, and coup d’etat, the country has been experiencing recurrent instability, conflict, and violence despite the 2015 Algiers Peace Accord (Accord pour la Paix et la Reconciliation au Mali). Since 2017, the situation has deteriorated in Mopti and Segou regions in the center, and recently conflict and violence are also gradually moving to the Koulikoro, Kayes, and Sikasso regions in the south of Mali. In many regions, the Government of Mali (GoM) is limited in its ability to provide basic services to the population. Political tensions and insecurity have also led to a surge of internally displaced people, generating new demand for services that the country is not able to meet. The lack of progress in education and health performance indicators can be attributed to the deterioration of security conditions, particularly in the north and center regions. This situation has been exacerbated by the recent military coups of August 2020 and May 2021, increasing the country’s fragility. 4. The COVID-19 pandemic has affected the country’s socioeconomic progress and highlighted the need for accelerating electricity access as a means of boosting resilience to new variants and future crises and building back the economy. With the COVID-19 pandemic, households are affected through an income drop due to direct health impacts, confinement measures, and a decline in remittances, aggravating poverty and gender inequality. Measures to prevent the spread of COVID-19 reduced incomes (informal traders, transportation operators, and so on) and increased layoffs (household service providers and so on). The drop in international remittances and domestic transfers due to COVID-19 resulted in a total estimated increase in the poverty rate of 0.6 percentage points (corresponding to 120,000 additional poor people). The impact on labor income is an increase in poverty by 0.06 percentage point. The impact of the pandemic will be more severe for households headed by a member aged 65 or more (those who tend to be more vulnerable to the disease). Increasing access to electricity in health facilities and households will help boost Mali’s resilience to the medium/long term impacts of COVID-19 and future crises. 5. Electricity access and quality of electricity services are among the top priorities for Mali in its efforts to support economic and social recovery following COVID-19 and political and security instability. To recover from the multiple crises it faces, Mali has elaborated its Strategic Framework for Economic Recovery and Sustainable Development (Cadre Stratégique pour la Relance Economique et le Développement Durable) (2019–20233), and the transitional government has demonstrated commitment to implement that strategy. Increased access to reliable and affordable electricity services is a top priority for the GoM to power socioeconomic development activities4. The GoM sees expanded access to reliable energy as an important input to creating jobs; diversifying the economy; improving human capital outcomes; reducing fragility; and improving the delivery of basic services, such as health and education, in rural areas as well as agricultural production through enabling irrigation and refrigeration. The GoM’s objective, as outlined in the new electrification strategy5 (Programme National d’Amélioration de l’Accès à l’Electricité au Mali– PNAAEM)6 that has been recently updated with IDA’s support, is to expand sustainable electricity access for rural transformation, prioritizing potential economic nodes with agro-processing and mining activities. 6. Energy poverty has been identified as one of the key drivers of fragility in Mali, as in the other G5 Sahel countries.7 The provision of energy services is a key part of Mali’s strategy to improve living conditions of the population and of the strategy for mitigation of the risk of conflict and violence (2020–2023) that the GoM recently adopted. Increasing access to electricity in conflict areas, in the northern and central regions, will increase resilience and serve as a prevention measure. In accordance with this strategy, the electrification of high-risk areas can contribute to addressing the spread of conflict by preventing conflict spillover effects and increasing trust between the state and citizens, which is a dynamic prevalent across the Sahel. Addressing energy poverty will also benefit women and girls as they often have to spend more time fulfilling household chores manually, including, for example, fetching firewood and water, and as a result, have less time for income-generating and educational activities. 7. Mali has low carbon emissions, but it is highly vulnerable to climate change, which will affect its agricultural sector and pose a direct threat to food security and household income, particularly for rural households. The country’s undiversified economy and high dependance on rain-fed agriculture, in a region where droughts, floods, and locust infestations occur regularly, makes household incomes and overall GDP growth prone to shocks. Mali is already experiencing high natural risks, including flooding (river and urban), water scarcity, extreme heat, and wildfires,8 which are expected to increase further due to climate change. Increased pressure on water and food resources will exacerbate poverty and conflicts between communities. B. Sectoral and institutional context Mali Power System Status 8. Mali's current power system is fragmented and faces three interlinked challenges: high costs of generation, mainly due to heavy reliance on expensive thermal generation, low levels of access to electricity services and a sector paved by a lack of efficiency and equity in energy services provision. The power system is organized into an interconnected national grid serving 30 urban centers including the capital Bamako, 35 isolated distribution networks in rural areas, and two localities connected to the Côte d’Ivoire distribution network, all operated by the National Electricity Utility (Electricité du Mali, EDM). In 2021, the total installed capacity in Mali comprised 780.74 MW and 53.45 MWp, of which 663.22 MW and 50 MWp for the interconnected grid; and 117.52 MW and 3.45 MWp for the isolated centers. For the interconnected grid, the capacity mix consists of hydropower/OMVS - 183.70 MW, solar PV – 50 MWp thermal - 379.52 MW, and imports from Compagnie Ivoirienne d’Electricité (CIE) – 100 MW, while the isolated grids in regional centers had a total installed (mostly thermal-based) capacity of 118,17 MW. Outside the perimeter of EDM, the Malian Rural Electrification and Domestic Energy Agency (Agence Malienne pour le Développement de l'Energie Domestique et l'Electrification Rurale, AMADER) also manages about 250 privately operated mini-grids in remote areas, most of which are hybridized with solar PV to improve reliability and affordability of electricity services in rural areas. Mali is endowed with excellent solar potential, with an average solar radiation estimated at 5–7 kWh per m2 per day and well distributed, and a daily sunlight duration of 7 to 10 hours. The continuous decline in investment costs of solar electricity generation20 and battery energy storage systems represents a unique opportunity for the country. Yet, today, solar energy remains unexploited and much-needed transmission and distribution network reinforcements limit the integration of new generation projects. Developing least-cost solar power projects on a competitive basis could contribute to increase domestic generation capacity, diversify sources of electricity supply, accelerate the shift away from expensive thermal production, and significantly lower the cost of electricity generation in Mali. The development of least-cost solar PV has then been identified as a top priority of the 2023–2040 LCDP and the 2022–2026 Sector Investment Plan, to complement hydropower. According to the LCDP, the share of variable renewable energy (VRE) will increase from its current level of less than 5 percent to more than 50 percent by 2035. This means that over 80 percent of the energy mix will be from renewable energy sources by 2035, mainly hydropower and solar PV, with significant climate co-benefits. 13. The GoM has decided to fast-track the implementation of solar parks as part of its medium and long-term solar deployment strategy. Over the last 10 years, the GoM received many unsolicited proposals from independent power producers (IPPs) to develop solar projects and only one of 50 MWp (AKUO solar) has been operational since March 2020. Several privately owned solar projects are currently at different stages of preparation/development, and the negotiated prices (above US$0.10 per kWh, bur below average generation cost). Given Mali’s limited fiscal space, leveraging private sector financing is critical to deploy grid-connected and off-grid solar generation projects. However, unplanned, and often noncompetitive processes, have impeded the timely delivery of cost-effective electricity supply by IPPs. With a US$21 million technical assistance—to the West African Power Pool (WAPP) under the Solar Development in Sub-Saharan Africa - Phase 1 (Sahel) (P162580), which was approved in mid-2018—the World Bank is supporting several countries, including Mali, to develop regional solar projects at scale, which are included in the WAPP master plan to share energy resources at the regional level. Under the BEST project, there will also be BESS deployment on the interconnected grid to help integrate the solar plants and improve the network's stability. 14. Contrary to the current market practice with spontaneous offers, the solar park approach aims to support Mali in piloting an innovative large-scale solar tender with battery storage, following a fully structured approach to bring down costs, reduce delays to reach financial close, and increase penetration of least-cost domestic generation. Mali is leveraging the work done under the Sustainable Renewables Risk Mitigation Initiative (SRMI)21 on the best practice for competitive bidding, as well as the World Bank experience, most recently in the regional solar park IPP with storage, supported by the Burkina Faso Solar Energy and Access Project (P166785). As part of SRMI, the GoM has access to significant resources from the Green Climate Fund (GCF) SRMI Facility and could also benefit from resources from the Climate Investment Fund (CIF) in the next years. The GoM would provide prearranged comprehensive de-risking measures, such as financing for the provision of land, permits, and power evacuation, as well as off-take risk mitigation instruments and financing packages available to bidders. However, the successful implementation of this approach will depend on the renewed commitment of the GoM to adhere to the competitive selection of IPPs according to the provision of the Public-Private Partnership (PPP) Law adopted on December 30, 2016, and the electricity law promulgated in 2000. Though Mali’s electricity sector was dominated by public sector institutions, it was liberalized by the Presidential Decree No. 00-019/P-RM of March 15, 2000. The Ministry of Mines, Energy, and Water (Ministère des Mines, de l’Energie et de l’Eau, MMEE) is responsible for the formulation, promotion, coordination, and monitoring and evaluation (M&E) of the national energy policy. The GoM has a concession agreement with the vertically integrated state-owned utility, EDM, which is responsible for electricity service delivery in Mali. EDM owns the interconnected grid and manages the isolated small grids in regional centers. Within the perimeter of its concession, EDM has monopoly over power transmission and distribution, while generation is open to the private sector,28 but EDM is the single buyer. EDM is also responsible for cross-border electricity trade (export and import). EDM supplies energy from its owned and rented thermal generation plants, electricity imports from Côte d’Ivoire, and the regional OMVS hydropower facilities (Manantali, Felou and Gouina). The Electricity and Water Regulation Commission (Commission de Régulation de l'Electricité et de l'Eau, CREE), established in 2000, oversees the regulation of the water and electricity sectors and reports to the Prime Minister’s Office. CREE’s mandate, which is limited to EDM’s concession perimeter, is to protect customers, promote competition, when possible, arbitrate disputes between the GoM and operators, and approve adjustments to ensure cost reflective tariffs. The capability of the regulator to effectively regulate the sector remains, however, limited today, especially regarding tariff setting. IDA is providing technical assistance to CREE to put in place a transparent system for tariff adjustment. Outside the EDM concession area, AMADER was created in 2003, with the mandate to provide electricity services to the population in remote areas. It supplies electricity through a PPP approach and acts as de facto regulator for rural electrification. Discussions are ongoing to find a sustainable model for harmonized tariffs across the rural electrification space29. Furthermore, on the institutional setting, an ongoing study is also assessing an extended scope for the CREE to regulate AMADER and rural electrification tariffs. The current discussions are only about harmonization in the area of rural electrification and not about equalization that would include grid electricity rates. Finally, to encourage the development of renewable energy, the Renewable Energy Agency (l’Agence des Energies Renouvelables du Mali, AER) was created in 2014 (replacing the National Center for Solar Energy). It has the mandate to expand and accelerate the use of renewable energies in Mali. The national energy sector policy and renewable energy strategy are being updated with the support of the Bank-financed MESIP to reflect recent developments in the sector and further promote private sector participation in renewable energy development. 25. Mali’s electricity sector also benefits from important developments in regional integration. Mali is a member of OMVS, which also includes Guinea, Mauritania, and Senegal. OMVS developed Manantali (200MW, 104MW for Mali), Félou (60 MW, 27 MW for Mali) and Gouina (140 MW total, with 47.6 MW for Mali) hydropower plants and is developing further projects in the Senegal river basin. EDM is also a member of the WAPP, an institution established in 1999 as part of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) to harmonize national electricity systems within the region and deliver power in a stable, reliable, and cost-competitive manner. Through the Solar Development in Sub-Saharan Africa - Phase 1 (Sahel) Project (P162580), the World Bank has been supporting the WAPP to strengthen the regional technical capacity for preparation of large-scale solar parks and integration of solar electricity into the grids. Component 4: Contingent Emergency Response Component. A Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC) with zero allocation may be used to contribute to an emergency response through the timely implementation of activities in response to an eligible national emergency. In the energy sector in Mali, the emergency conditions may arise after a terrorist attack or any major civil conflict or unrest that may damage power infrastructure. The CERC mechanism will be further defined in a CERC Operational Manual which will be part of the Project Implementation Manual (PIM), which will include triggers and conditions for the use of funds. This manual will clearly outline the triggers, eligible expenditures, and procedures for tapping into the CERC.
