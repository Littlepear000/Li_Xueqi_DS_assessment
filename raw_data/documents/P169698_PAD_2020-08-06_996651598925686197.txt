Page 5 of 58 The World Bank Log‐In Georgia (P169698) . I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Georgia’s economy has grown significantly over the past decade, culminating in its recent classification as an upper‐middle income country. A small and open economy, Georgia has a population of 3.7 million and a land area of 69,700 km2 and ranks seventh in the global Ease of Doing Business rankings.1 Georgia’s economic growth has been strong over the past decade, with far‐reaching reforms improving governance and the business environment. Between 2010 and 2018, the Georgian economy grew at an average annual rate of 4.8 percent, fueled by those reforms, and by boosting exports and integrating with the regional and global economy. Poverty has declined, even though inequality remains a concern. Georgia is also vulnerable to external shocks and regional disturbances and faces a population decline due to emigration and low fertility.2 2. Georgia will need to overcome income inequality and a lack of economic opportunities, especially in rural areas, to address economic dualism. Poverty and inequality remain high in rural areas with a fifth of people still considered poor. As of 2019, rural households earned 11 percent less than urban households; the per capita gap is wider, at 16 percent.3 In rural areas, households headed by women are particularly vulnerable to poverty.4 The Systematic Country Diagnostic identified the need for addressing economic dualism as a high‐priority, noting the importance of equalizing access to opportunities and investing in people in order to ensure that rural residents can participate in the modern economy, whether in rural activities or through internal mobility.5 3. The global outbreak of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19) threatens to reverse past gains in economic development and poverty reduction. The Government’s reaction to the pandemic was swift and comprehensive; precautionary measures were undertaken even before Georgia recorded its first COVID‐19 case and a nationwide State of Emergency, imposing sweeping mobility restrictions and closing non‐essential shops in March 2020. As the number of active COVID cases declined, the Government ended the national state of emergency on May 22, 2020 and accelerated the pace of opening the economy. While the response was effective in flattening the infection curve, it also took a toll on economic activity. The country is now expected to slip into a deep recession in 2020, and experience severe job and income losses and business closures. Around 500,000 Georgians are at risk of downward mobility and poverty is expected to increase by 2.8 percentage points. 4. Georgia’s economy is expected to contract by 4.8 percent in 2020 with risks tilted to the downside. The containment measures’ peak effect is likely to have occurred in April (‐16.6 percent year‐on‐year) and May (‐13.3 percent year‐on‐year), bringing cumulative GDP growth to ‐5.4 percent year‐on‐year in the year‐to‐May. Preliminary data suggest a tentative recovery in domestic consumption starting in June, while non‐resident spending remained essentially zero as borders reopened in July for a limited set of countries. The shock to demand has been broad‐based. As the economy recovers, the deficit is expected to gradually decline to levels prescribed by the fiscal rule.6 On June 24, 2020, the Parliament approved an amendment to the budget with additional fiscal spending of 4.0 percent of GDP, including 2.2 percent of GDP in social transfers, 1.0 percent of GDP in support to businesses, and 0.7 percent of GDP in additional health spending. The immediate shock might deepen economic dualism and the urban‐rural divide, posing challenges for the country. Georgia’s urban areas have benefited from economic growth and greater access to economic opportunities, but the rural hinterland has stagnated; for instance, the capital Tbilisi received 78 percent of total Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) between 2015 and 2019.7 Now, the COVID‐19 pandemic represents a severe risk of losing important gains in the fight against poverty, with the greater risks for those who cannot stay‐at‐home to work or learn, or whose incomes have been affected by reduced economic activity. The slowdown may further constrain economic opportunities for people in rural areas. 8. Women may also face growing barriers in the wake of the pandemic. Georgia is highly vulnerable to climate change and natural disasters. Risks include the negative effects of a rise in temperatures on agriculture, and of the increasing frequency and severity of extreme temperature events, droughts, frosts, and floods that can compound avalanche, mudslide and rock‐fall risk (for infrastructure) in mountainous areas. These effects could be more severe in rural and remote mountainous regions, where agriculture is still the major economic activity. Investments in climate‐resilient infrastructure, and which promote climate‐smart agriculture and enable economic diversification will be part of the response.12 10. International experiences suggest improved access to and use of the Internet will help address some of these development challenges. The Government seeks to reduce the gaps between urban and rural areas by improving how people, enterprises, and institutions across rural Georgia can access high‐quality, affordable broadband internet, and thence, use a wider range of digital services to access information, markets, and services. This expectation is in line with a range of national, regional, and global experience showing how increased access to and adoption of broadband is associated with improved economic growth13 and localized positive impacts on employment rates and protecting rural areas from depopulation.14 These investments will also support Georgia’s recovery from and resilience to natural disasters or crises such as the COVID‐19 pandemic, and enable the delivery of remote education, health, public services, and other essential services. For example, improved connectivity can boost remote learning and support livelihoods hampered by social distancing. Investments that help people and enterprises stay connected and working during natural disasters or pandemics will also boost economic resilience. The proposed Project also reflects the World Bank Group’s (WBG) crisis response approach to the pandemic by investing in Georgia’s recovery through support to develop key elements of the digital ecosystem. Rural broadband connectivity and use is thus underdeveloped, with potential to grow. Demand for Internet service has been strong; as noted before, 70 percent of rural households subscribe to available, basic services.24 A large group of small and medium‐sized service providers (SPs) compete with larger national SPs to serve these markets. However, offered connections are typically low speed with high unit prices. Rural connectivity remains constrained because of limited investment creating low‐capacity 'middle‐mile’25 and ‘last‐ mile’ networks in many rural settlements. Investments are low due to a mix of factors. First, costs of building high‐ quality broadband infrastructure to many rural settlements are significant for private investors, and there is a perception of limited potential returns on investment. Second, upstream markets remain concentrated with larger SPs, limiting the ability of competitive and smaller SPs to connect and offer a wider, more affordable range of services. Only three of 29 major middle‐mile network routes in the country have more than two competing service providers. Such concentration of the market led the electronic communications industry regulator, the Communications Commission (ComCom), to start regulating wholesale broadband prices in 2019. The GoG has also introduced regulations to help reduce the costs of broadband network deployment and service provision.26 17. Limited competition, high deployment costs, and underinvestment lead to a market failure that limits the expansion of delivering high‐speed broadband in many rural settlements. Crucially, investments in upgrading and expanding middle‐mile networks have remained limited. The result is that Internet services available in rural areas (and some smaller towns) are either of limited quality or that upstream networks are not able to deliver the capacity needed for high‐speed connectivity to downstream users. Even where Internet services are available, consumers in rural areas complain of limited choice of plans and lower‐than‐advertised speeds, implying that they are in effect paying more per unit than consumers in larger cities.27 This has created a counterproductive cycle of low supply in response to perceived low demand, keeping access to digital opportunities unequal between cities and the rest of the country. 18. More affordable and higher‐quality broadband internet access in Georgia thus hinges on fixing the gaps in the middle‐mile infrastructure. Analysis by ComCom in early 2020 has found that there are more than a thousand settlements (out of about 3,400 in all) across Georgia that do not currently have access to high‐capacity middle‐mile infrastructure, and where private SPs do not plan to invest in such connectivity in the next three years (to 2023). Hence, these settlements could lack coverage of high‐quality broadband retail services at affordable prices in the foreseeable future. In 2015, the Government set up the Open Net Program (ON Program), formally the National Program on Broadband Infrastructure Development, to extend high‐quality and affordable broadband services to those areas where the private sector would not be investing in any reasonable timeframe. 19. To complement improved coverage in rural areas, increasing the use of broadband internet will require addressing gaps in adoption of existing use‐cases. Internet use has been increasing nationally, but people in rural Georgia lag behind urban Georgia as well as even in rural European Union (EU) settlements in activities such as Internet banking, seeking health information online, or searching for jobs online. This is in spite of the availability of many of these services in the country, and via global platforms. Limited access to affordable high‐quality connectivity and resultantly reliable access to internet‐enabled digital services can constrain adoption of available digital services in rural areas. Component 2 includes activities to boost the inclusion of persons who might be at risk of being left behind in or left out of the digital economy. IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis 62. Project activities are expected to have positive economic impact. The analysis focused on Subcomponent 1.1. While they do not lend themselves to traditional economic analysis, Subcomponent 1.2 and Component 2 will also support activities that promote private investment, competition, and the expansion of Georgia’s digital economy, all expected to support innovation and inclusive growth. 63. The public financing of broadband connectivity is justified given: (1) the existing and sustained market failures that limit private investment; and (2) expected mobilization of finance for development. As noted previously, the ON Program aims to connect those settlements where private investment and competition are limited. It is expected to catalyze an estimated US$20 million or more in private investment by SPs as they connect and serve downstream markets. Notably this will be in parts of the country that are typically not the targets of such investment and will include small and medium‐sized SPs who might otherwise not be able to extend their reach into these settlements due to the high cost of upstream connectivity. 64. International experience also suggests economic benefits from improving access to opportunity in connected settlements. The Project is expected to have a positive economic impact, accelerating GDP growth and increasing access for rural inhabitants and businesses to economic opportunities. This is apart from the immediate impact of increasing connectivity to schools and government offices, and of households to internet applications including online learning, media, e‐commerce, and financial services. Citizen engagement. The success of the Project is predicated on the input of the residents of the selected settlements, and it includes activities and processes that strengthen their voice and agency. Citizens will be engaged at all stages of the Project cycle. At the preparation stage, the Project has drawn on consultations with citizens to inform Project design and confirm the validity of Project activities. This included focus group discussions and interviews within remote rural areas and workshops with rural residents as well stakeholders in Tbilisi (such as government agencies and private service providers). These engagements provided insight into the design of Project activities, specifically to inform the design of Subcomponents 1.1, 2.2, and 2.3, by identifying the possible barriers to digital inclusion and the mechanisms to overcome them. They have also provided insight into the design of citizen engagement activities throughout the life of the Project as summarized in the Project Stakeholder Engagement Plan. Key risks to the Project implementation and achievement of its PDO are (a) institutional capacity for implementation and sustainability risks, (b) fiduciary risks, and (c) stakeholder risks. Political risks are considered moderate even in the context of the planned elections in late 2020, given the broad support for the major activities under the Project. Macroeconomic risks to the Project— Page 28 of 58 The World Bank Log‐In Georgia (P169698) including to counterpart funding—are considered moderate, even in the wake of the COVID‐19 pandemic and its possible economic impacts given the strong recognition across Government of the importance of improving access to broadband internet to support social distancing while maintaining business continuity of the Government, including public service delivery. 81. Weak institutional capacity for implementing and sustaining the operation poses a Substantial risk and may adversely impact achievement of the PDO. Sustaining the outcomes of the Project holds some uncertainty due to Open Net NNLE’s limited track record. Risk mitigation measures include technical assistance for network construction—especially in the initial phases—and operations, and implementation capacity building provided under Subcomponents 1.1 and 3, as needed. The Project will also consider the possibility of an alternative arrangement where Open Net NNLE will outsource network management and operation in case of sustained issues in capacity development and mobilization, or limited progress towards meeting the PDO. Furthermore, a risk of unforeseen shifts in demand, causing SPs to stop or reduce their connectivity purchases and undermine the financial sustainability of the network, subsists. Component 2, which seeks to build sustained demand for broadband services, combined with extensive and regular stakeholder engagement with the SPs through mechanisms identified in the POM, are intended to mitigate some part of the risk of demand shifts. 82. The three implementing agencies will also need to closely coordinate to implement Project activities. This includes the preparation of terms of reference, technical specifications, procurement evaluations, and deliverables monitoring and acceptance for activities jointly implemented by multiple agencies. The agencies have limited experience in implementing World Bank‐financed projects. Mitigation measures include the creation of the PCG to improve coordination across implementing agencies, and definition and capacity building of a single PIE to support implementation of each activity. Moreover, with the national regulatory agency, ComCom, being organizationally independent, it is critical that collaboration with Open Net NNLE, a regulated entity, is undertaken while respecting the institutional and organizational autonomy of the regulator and without any influence on the operation of the PIE. For this, the roles and responsibilities of these two organizations in the cases of joint implementation will be clearly defined in the POM to minimize chances of confusion or conflict. 83. Fiduciary risks are also considered to be Substantial. While Georgia’s overall fiduciary environment is reasonable, the limited experience and capacity of implementing agencies to handle WB fiduciary systems suggests elevated risks to Project implementation. The proposed mitigation measure is to support capacity enhancement of the PIE to undertake the Project implementation functions, reducing the residual fiduciary risks. Component 3 includes possible capacity building activities for all the implementing agencies, as needed, to support Project fiduciary functions. 84. Stakeholders’ opposition or weak engagement could have a negative impact on the achievement of the PDO; this risk is therefore deemed Substantial. The GoG originally launched the ON Program in 2015 but has faced resistance from some of the larger SPs given their concerns about the new network disrupting the industry status quo and due to gaps in understanding of the ON Program’s objectives and design. These SPs still retain significant political‐economic influence; it is possible that they might resist sharing existing infrastructure needed for the ON network to operate. SPs might also engage weakly in the validation of settlements’ readiness for investments and demand for broadband internet subscription. This would lead to possible misallocation of public investments. To mitigate these risks, the GoG has increased communication with the SPs and clarified that the ON Program’s design addresses the concerns expressed in the past, including reassurance that the program will not serve end‐users, but only offer wholesale services. The Project’s design reflects these characteristics and supports Page 29 of 58 The World Bank Log‐In Georgia (P169698) enhanced communication to build support among all stakeholders. The Project design also includes significant citizen and stakeholder engagement processes to improve the quality of information available at the design stage and minimize the risk of limited interest among primary or secondary beneficiaries. 85. Other risks, associated with the effects of COVID‐19 pandemic, are considered Substantial. The pandemic might impact global supply chains for the goods to be procured, macroeconomic and fiscal conditions could influence the ability of SPs to participate and affect counterpart financing, and household and enterprise incomes limiting willingness to pay for improved broadband. Constraints to the movement of people and goods could delay implementation of key activities. Project staff or consultants may not be able to travel, delaying stakeholder and citizen engagement activities or technical consultancies. Mitigation measures cannot be defined at this time, due to the evolving nature of the situation. They will be devised in consultation with the implementing agencies. The potentially significant impact of the pandemic on Project implementation progress in the medium term, and limited clarity on mitigation measures lead this risk to be Substantial currently. Subcomponent 1.1 supports the creation of middle‐mile networks as part of the Open Net Program of the Government. In 2015, the Government set up a National Program on Broadband Infrastructure Development (henceforth, the ‘Open Net Program’) to improve middle‐mile connectivity in rural areas. It created an organization, Open Net NNLE, for its implementation. Open Net NNLE is a non‐entrepreneurial, non‐commercial legal entity, chartered by GITA, which is in turn a Legal Entity of Public Law under the MOESD. The Program is to be implemented as defined in the Government Resolution 375, with the policy oversight of the MOESD, and with the involvement of the regulator ComCom. The Government has defined this program as seeking to replicate, as closely as feasible, the RAIN Program of Lithuania, which has received significant funding from the European Union. The Government has sought inputs and feedback from the staff at the agency overseeing the RAIN Program to assist in the design of the ON Program and has also factored in the technical inputs of the Bank in the design of the program. Page 49 of 58 The World Bank Log‐In Georgia (P169698) 3. Although the Program was defined by Government Resolution No. 375 first in 2015, it continues to have relevance. It will build connectivity infrastructure to connect those settlements in Georgia that (1) have more than 200 inhabitants,91 (2) do not have high‐speed broadband connectivity (defined subsequently), and (3) will be not be served such connectivity by private service providers in the next three years. The ComCom has verified the list of eligible settlements in 2019, during the Project preparation period. As of January 2020, there are 1,064 such eligible settlements across Georgia that do not have access to affordable and high‐speed broadband connectivity. Moreover, the Bank has engaged with various stakeholders, including civil society, service providers, citizens, and public agencies in 2018 and 2019, in the context of its technical assistance to the Government on development of the NBDS, and found significant interest and support for the ON Program. 4. The Project will finance the construction of middle‐mile networks to connect selected settlements and to establish open access points of presence (PoPs) at those settlements. The Project will finance the design, building, installation, and activation of backhaul infrastructure for up to about 1,000 settlements identified by the ComCom as eligible (of a total of 3,388 settlements across Georgia); most of the rest of the settlements will be covered by a pilot program financed by the GoG using its own resources, and some settlements might not be eligible in the future. The network will include sections that are newly constructed and, where suitable existing infrastructure exists, the program will use that infrastructure either through leasing of capacity or the purchase of indefeasible rights of use (IRUs). 5. The intervention will address a sustained and growing market failure to create the middle‐mile infrastructure that can deliver global standard internet services in rural Georgia. Even with nine operators providing domestic middle‐mile (wholesale) connectivity services in Georgia, there is market dominance in this segment of the broadband value chain. This has led to the growing gap in rural areas vis‐à‐vis larger cities in terms of good quality internet connectivity. Notably, when the GoG first undertook its identification of eligible settlements in 2015, the number was 500, it grew to 700 in 2017, and is now at over a thousand. This implies a falling behind of these settlements as demand for Internet has grown and new digital services and tools require better‐quality connectivity. 6. It is important to note that the infrastructure to supply basic Internet service exists, often relying on lower‐capacity wireless technology. But this is not sufficient to supply high‐speed connectivity to households or users such as schools or enterprises. Absent access to such infrastructure and high‐speed Internet service, the gaps between rural and urban areas will continue to grow, broadband enabled use‐cases will be unequally available, and digital economy development in the country could stall and foster spatial inequality. The pace and steadiness of private investment to fill middle‐mile infrastructure gaps is unclear, especially in the post‐COVID‐19 environment. Moreover, these infrastructure improvements will need to be complemented by efforts to further enable the development of Georgia’s digital economy and to promote such use‐cases that attract more people and enterprises to adopt broadband and use the digital services that it enables. 7. The Program is designed to align with international best practices—notably EU guidelines—to avoid distorting the market or displacing equivalent private investment. The Program is designed to address the specific market failure of development of the middle‐mile infrastructure. Its network will only connect settlements that the private SPs will not cover in a reasonable timeframe to provide the quality of services at reasonable prices per the NBDS. Moreover, the Program is expected to mobilize finance for development. Open Net will connect settlements to a high‐capacity middle‐mile infrastructure, but the service provision to end‐users will be undertaken by private SPs on commercial terms. In this manner, the ON Program both bridges the gap that holds back provision of high‐quality broadband services to these settlements and creates an opportunity to mobilize private investment by SPs who will need to develop the last‐mile infrastructure to connect to their customers. The ON Program will extend access to broadband internet to settlements (villages and towns) across Georgia at similar quality and effective prices per unit as available in the capital. 9. The Government has considered various possible models to mobilize finance for development. The GoG has taken many steps in line with international good practice to improve the enabling environment for digital infrastructure, seeking to boost access to affordable broadband. However, investment and coverage of high‐ quality and affordable broadband remained low in rural areas. Noting the increasing gap, since 2015 the GoG attempted—through discussions with SPs—to identify an appropriate model to address the digital divide. While the RAIN model of a public design, build, operate model was considered in the beginning, the Government also attempted to attract private SPs into some form of cost sharing to build out the network. However, these attempts did not yield results. Moreover, the continued and increasing gap in infrastructure development in the selected settlements since 2015 also underscores the presence of a market failure. The Government also considered the option of only constructing the passive elements of the network and leaving the active elements for development by SPs per their requirements. However, that choice was found to limit the possibility for smaller‐sized SPs to effectively take part due to the higher investment requirements, and hence, could have limited competition in the market. The Government has thus decided to go ahead with its original choice of implementation model, to construct and activate the network. The Government remains committed to this Program, noting that the selected settlements, which have already been identified by the regulator ComCom, will not have access to high‐speed broadband internet services or will have higher effective prices due to the lower quality of connectivity. 10. The network design will ensure technological neutrality; SPs will be able to use the network to offer their own services to end‐users. And end‐users will be able to choose among all retail SPs in their settlement, and thence, the service and technology (e.g., 4G, fiber internet) based on their preferences. The availability of improved connectivity has the potential to trigger innovation in service provision by the SPs themselves (e.g., new service offerings in rural areas), and by other firms delivering digital services (e.g., e‐commerce, online learning) once these new markets open. 11. The primary technology for the network, given the capacity requirements to deliver the targets of the NBDS, will be OFC infrastructure. In the coming years, newer technologies (especially 5G wireless networks) might emerge as options, but currently they do not offer the same balance of quality and reliability as OFC networks.94 Where the costs of underground OFC deployment might prove to be prohibitive (e.g., in high‐mountainous regions), or where cost savings are possible by using existing utility or public infrastructure, the network can be deployed using poles (e.g., for street lighting or electricity) and aerial cable.95 The costs of these deployments are lower, but they are more susceptible to some natural disasters such as landslides that are more likely in some regions. On the other hand, underground fiber networks are susceptible to flooding. Hence, in both cases, the design of the network shall seek to enhance climate resilience given the specific location. The precise mix of underground and aerial infrastructure for the network will be determined during the final technical design and reflected in the specifications of the bidding documents; at this time, the Project assumes about a third of the network will be aerial. 13.
