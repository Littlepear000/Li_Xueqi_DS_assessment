STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Tunisia’s economy in 2024 experienced a cautious recovery in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic, albeit with challenges and opportunities. The country has one of the most diversified economies in Africa, with a historically large services sector that has grown over the past decade.1 In 2023, significant export gains came from tourism recovery2 and export-oriented industry.3 However, severe drought and tighter financing conditions impacted growth in 2023, leaving the economy below its pre-COVID-19 pandemic level state. Real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth in 2024 was 1.4 percent, compared to 2.7 percent in 2022.4 This translated into an unemployment rate of 16 percent in quarter 3 of 2024 and a labor force participation rate of 46.2 percent, 1.5 percent below pre-COVID 19 pandemic levels.5 Further Tunisia continues to face challenges in covering its financing needs despite an improving external balance. Pressure on public finance remains significant. While the fiscal deficit was reduced in 2024, it continues to be higher than pre-COVID period (6.3 percent of GDP in 2024 versus 2.9 percent in 2019). 2. Economic challenges and vulnerability to climate change are affecting Tunisians unequally . Inequality, as measured by the Gini Index, increased from 32.8 to 33.7 between 2015 and 2021, mainly driven by disparities within urban and rural areas and between regions. Disparities persist between the interior regions, where official poverty ranges between 22.5 percent (North-West region) and 37 percent (Center-West region) and coastal regions of Grand Tunis (4.7 percent), North-East (15.2 percent) and Center-East (13.2 percent).6 Vulnerable and poor households in poverty-stricken regions are particularly exposed to natural hazards and climate change due to limited access to resources and public services, and limited capacity to weather shocks. 3. To respond to these challenges, the 2024 Government program focusses on promoting sustainable growth and social justice. The Government has selected priorities for the next five years. On the social side, there has been a focus on increasing support to vulnerable groups, promoting economic inclusion and jobs. Enhancing human capital remains critical particularly for health, education and employability. Expanding digitalization and simplifying administration processes is another priority target. The Government plans to improve the business environment and private sector development to promote growth and jobs. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Tunisia’s health system continues to face the impact of an ongoing demographic and epidemiological shift. The share of the population above 65 is high in Tunisia (9 percent), almost twice the average for Middle East and North Africa (MENA) countries (5 percent), and well above peers such as Algeria, Egypt, and Morocco. Non-communicable diseases (NCD) continue to be the main source of the disease burden, causing 86 percent deaths, with particularly high and increasing rates of hypertension and diabetes. In 2016, population-level prevalence of hypertension was at 29 percent, and of diabetes 16 percent, placing Tunisia amongst the countries with the highest rates in the MENA region. Climate change is anticipated to increase health risks and emergencies faced by Tunisia's most vulnerable communities, exacerbating environmental health concerns. Tunisia’s Mediterranean location makes it highly vulnerable to climate change, with extreme weather events such as heat waves, heavy rainfall, and drought becoming more frequent. Rising sea levels could impact nearly 78,700 people by 2100.9 Four years of drought, have significantly reduced agricultural production, increasing food insecurity and malnutrition.10 Climate-related hazards pose high risks to 26 percent of the population (18.9 percent exposed to drought, 7.3 percent to heatwaves, and 1.3 percent to floods). 11 Under a high emission scenario, the mean annual temperature is projected to rise by 5.3°C in 2100, causing as many as 56 deaths per 100,000 in the elderly (65+ years) by 2080 compared to the estimated baseline of 4 deaths per 100,000 annually between 1961 and 1990.12 Other negative health impacts associated with climate change in Tunisia include the recurrence and emergence of vector-borne diseases (malaria, leishmaniasis, dengue and West Nile fever), an increase in water-borne and food-borne diseases, and the aggravation of diseases related to air pollution.13 Rural poor populations, as well as outside workers, women, children, the elderly are disproportionately affected.14 Environmental factors such as poor air quality, water contamination, and exposure to hazardous chemicals further exacerbate the burden of NCDs. 6. Tunisia faces pandemic preparedness challenges, with priority needs to strengthen disease surveillance, laboratory systems, and public health workforce. Although Tunisia has made progress in response capacity under International Health Regulations (IHR), the COVID-19 pandemic exposed gaps in emergency preparedness and response, resulting in one of the highest mortality rates in the region. Early warning and disease surveillance systems lack an integrated epidemiological intelligence platform to connect human, animal, and environmental health data, with inadequate analytical capacity at local levels and poor cross-sectoral communication channels. Laboratory infrastructure shows gaps, with none of the 321 public human health laboratories having ISO certification (compared to only 3 of 8 veterinary laboratories), insufficient specimen referral systems, and limited capacity for advanced diagnostics like Matrix- Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry. This is particularly concerning given Tunisia's 38 percent surge in antibiotic consumption without corresponding antimicrobial resistance surveillance.15 The public health workforce exhibits deficiencies, including insufficient multidisciplinary response capacity at subnational levels, the absence of an integrated Human Resources Management Information System across health and agriculture ministries, and inadequate training in veterinary health and field epidemiology. The One Health concept remains poorly integrated into educational curricula, while emergency response teams lack capacity for effective simulation exercises. These challenges collectively undermine Tunisia's ability to implement the comprehensive 7-1-7 bottleneck analysis approach for rapid detection and response to potential outbreaks. Institutional arrangements for emergency and pandemic preparedness and response are coordinated by the Ministry of Health. The country has mechanisms in place to detect, prevent, and respond to health crises such as pandemics (e.g., COVID-19), though challenges remain in coordination, resources, and implementation. The Strategic Health Operations Center (SHOCROOM – Centre Stratégique d'Opérations Sanitaires) was established in 2010 with support from the World Health Organization (WHO) to service as a 24/7 public health emergency coordination hub, operating within the Ministry of Health and directly linked to the Minister’s cabinet. Initially designed to manage the H1N1 pandemic, it has since played a pivotal role in responding to major national and international crises, including: (i)the 2011 humanitarian crisis at the Tunisian Libyan border; (ii) response efforts to terrorist attacks and mass casualty incidents; and (ii) the management of the COVID-19 pandemic. The SHOCROOM will be established by the Government as Tunisia’s National Public Health Emergency Operations Center (PHEOC). 8. Tunisians encounter multiple barriers to accessing quality healthcare services due to organizational constraints. Health services are organized in a traditional pyramid structure, with the majority of health system capacity managed publicly by the Ministry of Health (MOH). To date, the number of health facilities include 110 hospitals of circonscription, 31 regional hospitals, 38 university hospitals, and over 2,100 public primary health centers (Basic Health Centers, centre de santé de base – CSB). A shortage of healthcare professionals and physicians further exacerbates the challenge, making quality medical services difficult to access. Data from the MOH health map (2019-2021) shows the relatively low levels of health workers per 10,000 which has not increased between 2019-2021, with both generalist and specialist doctors at 5/10,000; health technicians at 12/10,000; and nurses 20/10,000. These figures are below global averages, in particular for nurses.16 Beyond constraints of human and physical resources, the health system’s centralized organizational structure hinders implementation of quality improvement initiatives, with limited use of data and evidence-based planning at the health district (circonscription) level. Even as various central entities, such as the National Authority for Assessment and Accreditation in Healthcare (Instance nationale de l’évaluation et de l’accréditation en santé-INEAS) have been established and are implementing interventions such as clinical guideline development and accreditation of facilities, the uptake of these interventions at the regional and service delivery levels is limited. For example, clinical practice quality and supportive supervision are lacking, and are amongst the reasons behind low rates of control for hypertension and diabetes.17 Private sector plays a key role in service delivery, employing over 50 percent of all doctors, owning a majority of advanced medical technologies such as 75 percent of Computed Tomography (CT) scanners and 81 percent of Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scanners, and therefore accounting for about half of current health expenditures.18 9. The Primary Health Care (PHC) system also faces other constraints, including: (1) limited access to coordinated and effective and curative services throughout the care pathway, (2) a fragmented health information system, and (3) inadequate medical equipment, particularly in hard-to-reach areas. Of the 2,100 CSB, only 364 provide daily medical consultations, while 1,094 operate only once a week, resulting in low rates of use. The care pathway is often fragmented, as many CSBs do not have complementary examinations such as radiology and biology. This forces patients to multiply visits for a simple biological check-up, due to the lack of online appointment systems and the non-accessibility of results by treating physicians via an integrated information system. Similarly, general practitioners in primary care settings refer patients to higher-level facilities without the necessary information, as the health information system is still largely paper- based. Although an electronic register for vaccination (EVAX) is operational with 8 million accounts and more than 900 thousand registered acts, the deployment of the Electronic Medical Record, set up with the financial support of the European Union (EU), remains limited in a pilot area of seven CSBs. It lacks essential features such as structured data entry for laboratory checkups, health status monitoring, clinical decision support, and screening test tracking. In addition, although the CSBs are equipped with certain equipment, major preventive and curative maintenance problems lead to frequent breakdowns. Geographic disparities and transportation challenges pose obstacles to equitable access to emergency care, particularly for vulnerable populations living in hard-to-reach areas. These disparities raise major health equity issues, as delayed access to emergency care increases the risks for these populations. A current World Bank analysis of travel times to emergency centers reveals that more than 4.7 million people in peri-urban and rural areas are beyond the 30- minute threshold set by the government, unlike urban populations with faster access to care. For the population covered by an emergency medical department (SAMU) in zone 1 which includes Tunis, 67 percent of the population is within a 30- minute ambulance ride from emergency services. Women face numerous constraints in accessing emergency care. Lack of transportation for women and girls that live in hard-to-reach areas affect their ability to reach emergency services. This is particularly critical for women in labor or have complications during childbirth. In addition, climate-related hazards will exacerbate access to emergency services, with extreme weather events such as flooding disrupting roads and further lengthening travel times, disproportionately affecting rural and peri-urban communities. 11. Despite Tunisia's efforts to expand emergency services following the COVID-19 pandemic, the country continues to face challenges with service quality in emergency care. Currently, emergency services are provided across various levels of healthcare facilities, including university and regional hospitals, which operate 24/7, district hospitals open until 6 PM, and CSBs operating until 2 PM. A total of 192 emergency units are distributed across 25 university hospitals, 31 regional hospitals, 105 district hospitals, and 31 CSBs. The significant demand for emergency care is reflected in the 7,112,406 consultations recorded in 2024. University hospitals handled 1,721,403 consultations (24.21 percent), regional hospitals recorded 1,883,006 consultations (26.47 percent), district hospitals managed 3,150,613 consultations (44.29 percent), and CSBs provided 357,384 consultations (5 percent). Despite the extensive reach of emergency services, challenges persist due to outdated equipment and information system bottlenecks, ultimately affecting the quality of emergency care. 12. Pre-hospital emergency services face challenges due to the lack of standardized prioritization systems, inadequate information management, and limited adoption of Dossier médical informatisé (DMI) across healthcare facilities. The pre-hospital emergency medical services operate with six SAMU and 37 mobile medical units for emergency care and resuscitation (SMUR). In 2024, these pre-hospital emergency teams conducted 11,526 primary interventions, urgently responding to victims and critically ill patients at the scene. Additionally, 16,908 secondary interventions were carried out to transfer critical patients between healthcare facilities, ensuring continuity of care and access to specialized medical services. However, the medical regulation centers that manage dispatch operations currently lack a standardized prioritization system, and the information management remains inadequate. SAMU continues to rely on paper reports, lacks centralized data collection, and does not integrate with hospital and DMI records. The implementation and utilization of DMI vary significantly across different levels of emergency services. In university hospitals, DMI systems have been widely implemented, with 50 percent fully installed and utilized, while the remaining 50 percent are installed but only partially used, indicating challenges in adoption or system optimization. In regional hospitals, the situation is more complex. While 32 percent of hospitals have fully installed and operational DMI systems, a significant 52 percent have them installed but only partially utilized, reflecting similar adoption challenges. Furthermore, 16 percent of regional hospitals have not installed DMI systems at all, highlighting a gap in digital transformation. The situation is more concerning in district hospitals and CSBs, where DMI systems are neither installed nor used. The absence of digital health records at this level suggests a critical barrier to efficient patient data management, coordination of care, and overall healthcare service improvement. 13. Hospital emergency departments are generally well-equipped but face challenges in coordination and optimizing patient pathways, particularly for critical cases requiring intensive care. For critical patients, the major issue is the shortage of intensive care unit beds. Tunisia currently has approximately 150 functional intensive care unit (ICU) Page 4 beds, some of which are only for post-surgery care. This results in Tunisia having about 1.25 critical care beds per 100,000 people, compared to 9.6 in South Africa and 46.5 in Türkiye. For stabilized patients the issue is the lack of a system to locate available beds in hospital departments, leading to extensive but often futile follow-ups by Emergency Department (ED) staff. Consequently, some patients spend their entire hospital stay in the ED, with estimates indicating that up to 60 percent of those needing admission remain there. 14. In response to lagging health outcomes and fragmented governance, the Government has been implementing reforms outlined in the National Health Policy (PNS). Developed through a broad stakeholders’ dialogue, the PNS focuses on three core principles to reach universal health coverage: (i) strengthening governance, (ii) improving transparency, and (iii) deepening citizen engagement. Strategic choices of the PNS include (1) placing the citizen at the center of health sector priorities, (2) improve equitable access to care, (3) prioritize preventive and protective health services, and (4) strengthen the stewardship of both public and private sector service delivery. These reforms aim to boost patient trust, health worker motivation, and system efficiency. By 2030, Tunisia aims to reduce maternal mortality from 45 to 19 per 100,000, obesity rates from 26 percent to 20 percent, out of pocket expenses from 38 percent to 25 percent, improve access to quality health services, provide at least 60 percent of the population with 30 minute access to an accredited primary health care facility, and expand basic health insurance coverage to 100 percent of the population and increase government health spending to 6 percent of GDP. The expected Project beneficiaries will include the whole population given the Project scope to improve the populations’ access to primary health care, emergency, and hospital services nationwide. The health workforce will be an important Project beneficiary as they will receive training and capacity building in disease surveillance, rapid response training, emergency response and field epidemiology training and skills in laboratory management, waste management practices and IT systems. D. Project Components 17. Through three interlinked components, this Project will seek to improve access to resilient, quality, and responsive health services. Activities within the Project form an interconnected strategy to strengthen Tunisia's health system in alignment with the goals set forth in the National Health Policy. Component 1 establishes the foundation by enhancing the country's pandemic preparedness and emergency response infrastructure through improved surveillance systems, laboratory capacity, and environmental health. Component 2 builds upon this resilience by reorganizing primary healthcare services to efficiently deliver preventive services, with a focus on governance and information systems improvements, while ensuring an emergency-ready primary health care system. Investments in emergency medical services through component 3 enable the creation of an end-to-end care continuum from pre-hospital emergency response through hospital-based emergency departments to intensive care units. This integrated approach ensures that patients receive appropriate care at each level of the system, with strengthened referral mechanisms and information systems facilitating smooth transitions between service levels. Page 6 18. The three components of the Project are fully aligned with Tunisia's PNS priorities. Component 1 focuses on strengthening health system resilience to protect citizens' health through the implementation of an integrated One Health surveillance system for both human and animal health. This digital approach enables better monitoring and response to health threats, directly supporting the PNS goal of citizen protection. Component 2 addresses the reorganization of prevention and primary healthcare services by establishing family medicine as the entry point to the health system. The Project draws upon extensive consultations with the Ministry of Health, insights from the Tunisia COVID- 19 Response project (P173945) and analytical products from the Health Financing and Service Delivery Strengthening for Universal Health Coverage Project (P178625). Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis 53. There is a strong economic case for investing in health emergency and pandemic preparedness and response. Global evidence suggests that comprehensive investments in PPR institutions, systems, and workforce are critical for countries to build emergency-ready health systems. WHO estimates that every US$ 1 invested in the health emergency cycle sees a return of over US$ 8, and a 5-year investment is projected to save 1.5 million human lives and produce US$ 240 billion of economic gain.28 The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in massive disruptions to Tunisia’s economy, with GDP contraction of 8.6 percent in 2020 due to lockdowns, healthcare costs, and lost productivity.29 Tunisia relies heavily on tourism and international trade, both of which are highly vulnerable to pandemics. 54. The Project aims to address current strains on Tunisia’s health system and strengthen resilience across primary, emergency, and hospital care. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed vulnerabilities, overwhelming hospitals and 28 https://www.who.int/europe/emergencies/our-work-in-emergencies/preparedness 29 World Bank. Macro Poverty Outlook for Tunisia : April 2024. https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents- reports/documentdetail/099957204052432037/idu153486ab6165bc14ebe1a1af18f685bb0fd62 Page 17 underscoring the urgent need for stronger primary healthcare, emergency stockpiles, and rapid response teams. Many hospitals face challenges such as aging infrastructure, overcrowding, and equipment shortages, which limit their ability to provide quality care, particularly during health crises. To address these issues, the Project will invest in primary, emergency, and hospital health systems to ensure equitable access to quality healthcare. Strengthening primary care will enhance preventive services, early disease detection, and community-based healthcare, reducing pressure on higher-level facilities. Investments in emergency care and hospitals will improve critical response capabilities, upgrade infrastructure, and expand access to advanced treatment options, ensuring comprehensive and efficient patient care. This approach aligns with Tunisia’s PNS, which prioritizes primary healthcare as the foundation of the health system while enhancing emergency response and hospital services. The Project will also tackle urban-rural disparities and improve both accessibility and quality of care: rural areas have fewer hospitals and specialists, forcing patients to travel long distances for medical care. Rural Tunisians face challenges in accessing a healthcare facility. Strengthening hospitals and primary health centers will bridge this gap, improving Tunisians’ access to timely and quality medical services. F. Economic analysis 55. The proposed investments in PHC, pandemic preparedness, and emergency services are expected to yield large health benefits compared to their costs, according to the task team’s economic analysis . Each of the three elements of the PDO – increased access to care, quality of care, and pandemic resilience – will translate directly into improved population health. The proposed health system investments yield substantial economic returns across three components. Component 1 strengthens pandemic prevention, preparedness, and response, with every dollar invested potentially generating benefits of US$34-US$1,703 for averting health losses and US$1,102 for preventing GDP contraction during pandemic events, while additionally safeguarding human capital development through maintenance of essential services. Components 2 and 3 collectively improve primary healthcare access and quality, generating a benefit-cost ratio of 8.9 with an internal rate of return of 72 percent, through averting an estimated 19,774 disability- adjusted life years annually through improved access and 24,937 through enhanced quality. These components also create significant efficiency gains that will sustain improvements beyond the project period, and as a result Components 2 and 3 have an estimated net present value of US$744.8 million. Key environmental risks include infrastructure-related impacts associated with establishing health data management systems (sub-component 1.1) and rehabilitating PHCs (sub-component 2.2). These activities will occur within existing government- owned facilities, avoiding land clearing or new development, and are unlikely to affect biodiversity or ecologically sensitive areas. However, rehabilitation and minor construction activities in health facilities could lead to localized, temporary environmental impacts including, air and noise pollution, wastewater management, solid waste generation, and Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) risks. Given the multiple project sites and varying local capacities, ensuring Page 21 compliance with environmental standards and contractor obligations may be challenging. Another significant environmental risk is healthcare waste management, including biomedical waste, outdated equipment, and E-waste. Waste management will require proper handling, segregation, and disposal to prevent contamination risks to healthcare workers, patients, and surrounding communities. Poorly managed healthcare waste, particularly hazardous and infectious materials, can contribute to environmental pollution and public health risks. Mitigation measures will be embedded within the Project design as subcomponent 1.4 which specifically targets hospital waste management improvements, reinforcing national capacity for medical waste segregation, collection, transportation, treatment, and disposal. The Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF) developed under the COVID 19 project (P173945) will be updated to reflect the requirements of the current Project. The revised ESMF will outline detailed screening procedures, compliance measures, and mitigation strategies for managing environmental and social risks in healthcare facilities. The ESMF will also include measures for the safe disposal of medical waste, decommissioning of outdated equipment, and E-waste, ensuring alignment with national regulations and international best practices. Furthermore, a healthcare waste management plan will be integrated into the Project Operations Manual, specifying clear protocols for handling, storage, and treatment of hazardous materials. Additionally, the Project will assess the feasibility of using incineration facilities for treating high-risk medical waste. This assessment will examine environmental and public health risks, regulatory compliance, and cost-effectiveness. The incineration option will only be pursued if it meets international environmental standards and includes robust emission control technologies to mitigate air pollution risks. Overall, while the project presents moderate environmental risks, these risks can be effectively managed through well-designed mitigation measures, strengthened institutional capacity, and close monitoring of environmental compliance. 71. The Project has been screened for short and long-term climate disasters and risks and has been found to be highly exposed, while the potential risk to Project activities is moderate. Tunisia’s mean annual temperature is 19. 4°C and the country has already experienced a mean increase of 1.4°C since 1901, with greater warming in the summer than in the winter.30 While a decrease in precipitation by about 3 percent was observed over the past 30 years, stronger precipitation events resulting in flash floods have been registered in recent years.31 2. Climate change is a major contributor of health emergencies in Tunisia, disproportionately affecting urban and rural low-income populations. Climate was a contributor to 94 percent of natural disasters in Tunisia between 1980 and 2020, with floods comprising 79 percent of those disasters.32 Tunisia ranks 69th out of 188 countries on the Notre Dame- Global Adaptation Initiative (ND-GAIN) index which captures vulnerability to climate change in combination with readiness to improve resilience.33 Among the vulnerability factors identified is the low availability of medical staff per capita - especially in rural areas - and projected changes in vector-borne diseases. Temperature increases and changes in rainfall patterns are likely to exacerbate respiratory diseases, water-borne diseases and vector borne diseases.34 The annual number of days with a heat index above 35°C is expected to increase from 57 to 84 by 2040-2059 under a middle of the road projection scenario.35 Increased temperatures were already found to be associated with increased mortality in Tunis between 2005 and 2007.36 3. In addition to increased health needs, climate change also contributes to putting a strain on Tunisia’s health systems by damaging healthcare facilities and life-sustaining infrastructure. As an example, in 2020, the orthopedic hospital of the Manouba governorate was flooded.37 Healthcare facilities in Tunisia often need major renovations and do not appear to have adaptation plans for climate change38, and Tunisian general university Hospitals are not equipped to cope with massive influx of patients.39 The following adaptation and mitigation measures will be incorporated into the operation.
