Page 6 of 58 The World Bank Lao PDR Strengthening the National Statistical System (P178002) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. The Lao People’s Democratic Republic is a lower-middle-income country of 7.3 million people1. Economic growth has been driven by capital accumulation in the natural resource sectors and other large infrastructure developments, such as mining, hydropower, and transport-related projects. Having been one of the fastest growing economies in East Asia and the Pacific before COVID-19, Laos is experiencing a growth slowdown. While the economy grew at an average of 7.0 percent per year between 2003 and 2019, signs of deceleration appeared in 2013. Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth steadily declined from 8.0 percent in 2013 to 5.5 in 2019 due to a contraction in the mining sector and the impact of natural disasters, before falling to 3 percent in 2021 owing to COVID-19. The recovery is expected to be gradual, with growth projected to rise to around 4-5 percent in the medium term, below pre-pandemic levels.2 2. Despite two decades of strong economic growth, poverty remains high, and inequality has risen. While the poverty rate (based on US$3.20 per day in 2011 PPP) fell from 46.6 percent in 2012 to 37.4 percent in 2018, it remains high compared to structural and regional peers (29.3 percent in Ghana, 20.0 percent in Indonesia, 5.0 percent in Mongolia, 6.6 percent in Vietnam and 5.4 percent in Paraguay). Inequality has risen. The Gini index increased from 36.0 to 38.8 between 2012 and 2018.3 The growth slowdown and COVID-19, coupled with uncertain global economic outlook, are expected to have a profound impact on poverty and inequality. 3. Laos faces deteriorating macroeconomic conditions, a legacy of its capital-intensive and resource-driven growth pattern. Public and publicly guaranteed debt was estimated at 89 percent of GDP in 2021, one of the highest in the region. High debt service obligations, coupled with the limited availability of foreign currency, have contributed to a rapid exchange rate depreciation, with the Lao kip depreciating by almost 60 percent against the US dollar over the year to July 2022 and a pervasive parallel market for foreign currency. This has compounded the external debt burden. The growth slowdown has put a strain on fiscal revenues. The ratio of domestic revenues to GDP fell steadily from 16.3 percent of GDP in 2015 to 11.8 per cent in 2020 and is estimated at around 13 percent in 2021. Weak revenue performance, rising public debt and debt service burdens, foreign exchange liquidity constraints and the depreciation of the kip have contributed to deteriorating macroeconomic conditions and threatened development prospects. 4. Fiscal stress has forced expenditure cuts in key development areas, while rising inflation has affected livelihoods and food security. Declining fiscal revenues and growing interest payments have squeezed the fiscal space available for key development areas, including human capital development. Public spending on health and education is estimated to have declined from an already low 4.4 to 3.1 percent of GDP between 2016 and 2021. Laos already suffers from low levels of human capital. The country’s score on the World Bank’s Human Capital Index suggests that a child born today will be 46 percent as productive when she grows up as she could be if she enjoyed a complete education and full health. The rapid exchange rate depreciation, coupled with rising global inflation, has driven up consumer prices. The inflation rate surged to 30.1 percent in August 2022, undermining living standards and aggravating food insecurity, with 34 percent of households responding to food inflation by 1 Lao Statistics Bureau (as of 2021). 2 World Bank. 2022. “Lao PDR Systematic Country Diagnostic: 2021 Update”. 3 World Bank. 2020 “Lao PDR Poverty Assessment 2020: Catching Up and Falling Behind”. The sustained growth of the capital-intensive resource sector did not create sufficient jobs. The emerging domestic private sector has been hampered by an unfavorable business environment, particularly through a lack of competition and transparency. With few jobs being created in other sectors, a large proportion of the workforce remained engaged in subsistence agriculture. The employment-to-population ratio fell from 81.9 percent in 2012 to 60.8 percent in 2018 and is expected to have declined further during COVID-19. Women were disproportionately affected by insufficient job creation. Female labor force participation dropped by 15.8 percentage points to 66 percent in 2018, compared to a 9-percentage point decline in male labor force participation to 78.4 percent. Low female labor force participation is attributed to gender norms that women are responsible for household chores and childcare. A gender earnings gap that was not present in 2012 emerged in 2018: in the private sector, women received on average 10 percent less pay than similarly qualified men employed in similar occupations and locations.4 6. The natural resource-based economy is increasingly vulnerable to climate change and natural hazards. Laos is also exposed to major natural hazards, with extremely high exposure to flooding. Annual expected losses from flood events range between 2.8 and 3.6 percent of GDP. Key economic activities, including mining, hydropower, and agriculture, have also resulted in high rates of deforestation, natural resource depletion, and environmental degradation. The annual cost of environmental degradation was estimated to be equivalent to 19.3 percent of GDP in 2017. Climate change will likely accelerate environmental degradation and intensify natural disasters. With more than half of households relying on agriculture and the Mekong River system, livelihoods in Laos are particularly vulnerable to climate-related and weather-related shocks as well as environmental degradation. 7. The regional context plays a key role in shaping economic opportunities, with the Laos-China railway representing an important investment in regional connectivity infrastructure. The largest export markets and sources of foreign direct investment (FDI) include China, Thailand, Vietnam, and Japan, with China accounting for a significant share of FDI. The Laos-China railway that started operating in 2021 will connect Laos to the entire network of the Belt Road Initiative (BRI), while the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), which became effective in January 2022, offers opportunities to deepen regional integration. Success in capitalizing on the country’s strategic geographic location for inclusive development will depend on the ability of the Government of Lao PDR (GoL) to make the right and timely policy decisions to expand its export markets and diversify the economy toward job-creating sectors, while ensuring that the benefits of growth are shared more broadly. 8. The Ninth National Socio-Economic Development Plan 2021 – 2025 (9th NSEDP 2021–2025) aims to translate the National Socioeconomic Development Strategy 2025 and the National Vision 2030 into actions. The plan aims to address economic and financial difficulties and lay a foundation for the country to graduate from Least Developed Country (LDC) status, become an upper-middle-income country, and realize the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030. For that purpose, the plan outlines six outcomes including economic growth, human capital, well-being, environment, integration and connectivity, and governance. 9. Against the backdrop of this shifting and increasingly complex and transforming economic landscape, evidence-informed decision making is more important than ever. As Laos strives to promote regional integration and position itself as a land-linked economy, key statistics in the realms of economic activities, national accounts, 4 World Bank. 2022. “Lao PDR Systematic Country Diagnostic: 2021 Update”. The challenging situation in the private sector and the labor market, coupled with the impact of COVID-19 and rising inflation, has emphasized the needs for household- and individual-level statistics to inform policies and programs aiming at improving livelihoods and protecting households from shocks. At the same time, high fiscal deficit and public debt levels have resulted in tight fiscal constraints. The evidence-based policy design approach, underpinned by monitoring and evaluation systems, is an important tool for policymakers to formulate and implement tax and expenditure policies that balance distributional and efficiency objectives and maximize the development impact of scarce resources. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 10. The Lao Statistics Bureau (LSB), a semi-autonomous agency-equivalent organization under the Ministry of Planning and Investment, is the main producer of official statistics and the national statistical system coordinator in Laos.5 The national statistical system, known as the Lao PDR Statistical System (LSS), consists of LSB, the statistical centers of line ministries and the Bank of Lao PDR (BoL), and the statistical centers at the provincial- and district- levels. The first statistics law of 2010 upgraded LSB from a department under the Ministry of Planning and Investment (MPI) to a ministry-equivalent organization. The law assigned LSB the responsibility of providing the overall direction of the national statistical system through its mandate to draft the LSS’s development strategy, and leading the production, management, coordination, and dissemination of official statistics. The number of LSB staff has increased significantly since then from around 60 to over 130 staff to carry out its new mandate and meet the increasing demand for statistics. Despite some progress, most of the new staff are inexperienced. The LSB’s inadequate human resource and institutional capacity and the lack of an overarching governance and coordination model for the LSS have constrained LSB from carrying out its new mandate to support the development of the LSS. 11. Laos has made progress in advancing its statistical capacity in recent years. There has been an increase in data production both by LSB and the statistical centers of line ministries to support the policymaking process. Statistical tools and survey designs have been improved. The Strengthening the National Statistical System Project 2013 – 2018 (P129825), referred to as LAOSTAT in this document, has improved the LSS’s capacity to produce and disseminate more reliable and timelier macroeconomic statistics including GDP, Consumer Price Index, and Balance of Payments statistics in accordance with international standards and in response to user needs. Strategic documents that will guide LSB business operations and improve institutional capacity have been developed, such as the ICT strategy, the human resource development strategy, the dissemination strategy, the institutional capacity building plan, and the Lao quality assurance framework. Other socio-economic indicators, especially those related to the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) and Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), have also been produced and made available in international databases. The SDGs open data portal was launched in June 2021. 12. There has been an increasing level of government engagement in the LSB’s work in recent years. Initiatives have included an amendment of the statistics law in 2017. Development partners have contributed significantly to the growth and maturity of statistical activities in Laos. Bilateral and multilateral partners have provided both funding and technical support to the development of the LSS. In the field of economic statistics such as national accounts and external statistics, the International Monetary Fund (IMF), the Asian Development Bank (ADB), and the World Bank are the key partners. For social statistics, the United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA) and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) have provided funding and technical assistance to improve availability of population, children, and gender statistics. Technical assistance has been provided by the International Labour Organization (ILO) to improve labor statistics, the International Organization for Migration (IOM) to improve migration statistics, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) to improve agriculture and food security statistics, ADB to improve gender statistics, and WB to improve poverty and inequality statistics. LSB has received technical support from the Government of Luxembourg for institutional and organizational strengthening. Technical assistance and financing for ICT investment to strengthen sub-national statistical centers and promote vertical data integration are under discussion with the Government of Hungary. 14. The demand for the LSS’s products is strong, both from the GoL to support monitoring and evaluation of the NSEDP, and from business and international organizations. Of all the five pillars of the Statistical Performance Indicators (SPI)6: data use, data services, data products, data sources and data infrastructure, Laos scores highest in data use, showing a strong demand for the statistical system’s products by international organizations.7 The NSEDP, a five-year strategic development plan to guide policymaking, is outcome-based. The 9th NSEDP 2021 – 2025, which focuses on quality, green, inclusive, and sustainable growth to support the country to graduate from Least Developed Country (LDC) status and achieve the SDGs by 2030, outlines 168 indicators to be tracked as part of the Monitoring and Evaluation Framework.8 To meet the demand for relevant and good quality official statistics to support the evidence-based monitoring and evaluation of the NSEDP, improvement in the production and dissemination of statistics is needed. 15. Despite achievements in the past few years, there are still important data gaps to be filled and data dissemination to be strengthened. Laos lags behind its regional peers in advancing the national statistical system’s performance. The country’s overall SPI score was estimated at 54.1 in 2019, lower than 56.2 for Cambodia, 66.0 for Vietnam, 72.2 for Indonesia, 75.7 for the Philippines and 76.1 for Thailand. The largest gap is in data services, in which the country ranks in the lowest quintile of the global ranking, suggesting an opportunity for improvement in areas of data dissemination, data releases, online access, and other data services. Data dissemination practices are not user centric. The LSB data portal is fragmented, comprising several platforms (LAOSIS, LAOINFO, SDG Open Data), as a result of ad-hoc support from multiple donors. It is not updated in a timely manner and has limited coverage and openness. Significant challenges remain in the compilation and dissemination of economic and national accounts statistics. National accounts statistics currently published are insufficient to assess the performance of the economy (Table 1). Only GDP by production is produced on an annual basis and is usually published with long lags. Page 11 of 58 The World Bank Lao PDR Strengthening the National Statistical System (P178002) GDP at sub-national levels is produced but not published and needs quality improvement to meet international standards. LSB has subscribed to the IMF’s Enhanced General Data Dissemination System (e-GDDS) and adopted the standards of the 2008 System of National Accounts (2008 SNA) and the 6th edition Balance of Payments and International Investment Position Manual (BPM6). Compilation of GDP by expenditure and quarterly GDP are among the recent initiatives, as the demand for these statistics has grown strongly in recent years. However, the lack of capacity and commitment to data sharing between statistical producers has been identified as a major barrier to the compilation and timely dissemination of national accounts statistics. 17. Data on government finance, international investment positions, international financial flows and the banking sector are not regularly updated in the public domain. Insufficient commitment to dissemination and implementation of the Statistics Law to ensure data sharing between core statistics producers (LSB, MoF, and BoL) has been viewed as a key weakness. The Ministry of Finance (MoF) published a public and publicly guaranteed (PPG) debt bulletin for the first time on the MoF’s website in May 2021 and for the second time in June 2022. This is a crucial step toward improving debt transparency and debt data dissemination. However, the coverage and scope of the bulletin should be expanded to include more debt details and fiscal risk management. As efforts to stabilize macroeconomic conditions continue, it is expected that fiscal management and public debt data transparency will continue to attract international attention. 18. Capacity in census and survey data collection has increased, although frequency and dissemination practices have to be improved. LSB has made a successful transition from Paper-and-Pencil interviewing (PAPI) to Computer-Assisted Personal Interviewing (CAPI) on the Lao Expenditure and Consumption Survey (LECS), Labour force survey (LFS), Lao Social Indicator Survey (LSIS), economic census and agricultural census. The transition helped reduce data processing and publication lag times for socioeconomic indicators. For example, as a result of this transition, the gap between completion of the LECS fieldwork and publication of the survey report was reduced by half. However, the frequency of data collection is not sufficient to meet the demand to monitor and inform the GoL’s development agenda (Table 2). The nationally representative household survey and the labor force survey are conducted every five years, preventing policymakers from closely monitoring and responding effectively to poverty and labor market issues. COVID-19 is a case in point. LSB has played a role of the LSS coordinator with some degree of success, but there is scope to further strengthen the LSB’s capacity to lead the system and produce the desired outcomes. According to the 2017 Statistics Law, LSB should have an influence on the development of statistics across ministries and subnational government bodies and the official statistics that the system generates. Operationally, LSB has the responsibility for providing guidelines and directions on the operations of the LSS and collecting, consolidating, and requesting statistics from line ministries and disseminating them at the national level. The project follows a model of theory of change with final intended outcomes being more and better statistics for evidence-based policy planning and M&E, and progress on the statistical system modernization agenda. It begins with the identification of key challenges (Figure 3). First, there is a lack of high-quality data for evidence-based policy planning and monitoring and evaluation. There are some data gaps remaining, especially in the area of national accounts. In areas where data are available, some statistics do not comply with international standards (e.g., the GDP and CPI base year was last updated in 2012), while some are produced infrequently and released with long lags. Secondly, the use of statistics has been limited by weak dissemination and inefficiencies in the statistical system. Data and statistics are therefore not used to their full potential for decision making. Users are not aware of or do not have user-friendly access to statistics, or by the time they have access, statistics have become less relevant or outdated. When statistics are used for policymaking, oftentimes they are used improperly and could be misleading. The lack of an integrated data system limits the use and value of administrative data, preventing the cross-validation of different data sources and undermining holistic decision making. Lastly, there is limited human resource and institutional capacity to support the production of high-quality statistics and modernization of the LSS. 65. Activities and outputs are organized in three main pillars to tackle the key challenges and create the desired impacts. Activities supported by this project, detailed under Component 1, aim to tackle the first challenge. They include improving the methodology used for compiling statistics and supporting affordable and sustainable data collection. These activities will generate key outcomes which include the 2008 SNA milestone 2 (e.g., GDP by industry, expenditure, and income, GNI and quarterly GDP, core price statistics), increased availability and quality of economic, social and gender statistics, adoption of cost effective and innovative data collection methodologies, and increased frequency of firm, poverty, and labor market data. Statistics produced under this component will fill the data gaps that are deemed necessary for policy planning under the country’s evolving and challenging context.23 For example, more frequent firm and labor market data are necessary for policy planning to develop the dynamic private sector and labor market, while GNI is a better measure of how economic activities translate into a country's living standards given the importance of FDI and remittances in Laos. More accurate price statistics and household welfare data are necessary for monitoring macroeconomic risks and how they affect living standards. Activities detailed under Component 2 aim to tackle the second challenge, support activities under Component 1, and lay a groundwork for modernization of the LSS. They include implementing a user-centric dissemination strategy, piloting an integrated data system, and investing in necessary ICT modernization. These activities will increase data exchange between the LSB and line ministries, improve timeliness of statistical releases to comply with international standards, improve accessibility of microdata, statistics, and publications, and enhance data literacy and satisfaction among users, which in turn, will enhance the overall use of statistics. Activities detailed under Component 3 will invest in improving capacity of the LSS and LSB to implement activities under the project and push forward the modernization agenda. Building on experience from the LAOSTAT project, a high-level coordination mechanism will be set up. A strong coordination mechanism is important for ensuring successful implementation of the project including adopting the 2008 SNA, piloting the integrated data platform, progressing toward statistical system modernization, and coordinating donor support to statistics. As the central agency for official statistics, LSB needs to coordinate statistical activities across the statistical centers or units at various ministries for sectoral statistics and statistical offices at provincial and district levels for subnational statistics. Bilateral and multilateral donors are also active in the area of statistics, primarily to support monitoring of development outcomes. Recently, the Ministry of Planning and Investment has created a coordination mechanism by establishing the statistical working group, chaired by LSB and co-chaired by the Bank and UNFPA. This project will leverage on the existing coordination mechanism to coordinate donor support on statistics and establish a new high-level coordination mechanism that comprises LSB and six key ministries that participate in the data integration pilot to facilitate and support the data integration agenda. 71. Twinning arrangements proved a viable approach to providing technical assistance and consultancy services for project activities, but require stronger procurement capacity. Under LAOSTAT, twinning arrangements were used to complement the weak capacity of the implementing agency and provide access to a wide range of expertise needed to support project activities on a timely manner. However, procurement of the twinning firm was complicated and time consuming, causing a lot of delay in the previous project. Moreover, capacity building and knowledge transfers were rather limited partly due to low absorptive capacity. Therefore, under this project, it is important to i) prepare procurement packages (especially requests for proposals and bidding documents) well in advance; ii) clearly state capacity building and knowledge transfer requirements in the ToR; iii) build adequate procurement support for LSB into project design; and iv) obtain an experienced procurement consultant from the beginning of the project to support the project implementation unit on procurement work. 72. IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis 79. Quantifying the rate of return on investment in data and statistics is not straightforward given their public good nature and externalities they create. Official statistics are a public good, while data have externalities as their economic value increases when they are integrated. The economic value of data and statistics is largely reaped when they are used to better inform the decision-making of individuals, businesses, and governments. Data and statistics can alleviate information asymmetry and reduce inefficiencies in the economy. Several works have attempted to quantify the economic value of data. In businesses, PwC found data-driven firms have higher stock-market valuations, and those with data analytics capabilities perform better than peers within the same industry.25 In New Zealand, a valuation of the census was performed in 2014, suggesting that for every NZ$1 invested into the census, there is a return of around NZ$5 to the New Zealand Government and economy. 26 Enhanced dissemination and data usage increase the economic value of data. Estimates for the value of open government data as a percentage of GDP have ranged from 0.08 percent to 7.19 percent while a recent Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) report cites a range of 1 percent to 2.5 percent of GDP.27,28 Kubota and Zeufack (2020) found that the returns on investing in data transparency are higher as a country’s integration to the global economy deepens. The project is therefore designed to close the gender data gap. As noted in the description of Subcomponent 1.2, the World Bank Strengthening Gender Statistics (SGS) team conducted a preliminary assessment, covering 24 indicators identified as priority indicators for gender statistics globally, in three economic domains: employment, asset ownership and entrepreneurship. For Laos, seven indicators are classified as “available’, seven as “partially available”, and 10 as “unavailable.” In collaboration with the World Bank SGS team, the project will support the strengthening of production and dissemination of gender statistics by implementing recommendations to incorporate a gender lens in the ALFS, LECS and enterprise survey as well as financing the LSB’s production and dissemination of gender statistics factbooks and gender analysis. Procurement 22. Procurement capacity and risk assessment. LSB, the main implementing agency, has reponsibility to carry out all project procurement activities. Based on LSB’s experience with the LAOSTAT during 2013-18 and findings from the World Bank’s procurement capacity assessment in May 2022, it is concluded that the LSB management has extensive experience with the Bank’s operations procedures and previous STAT-1 procurement knowledge. However, for preparation of the complex ICT system procurement related documents and the additional project’s procurement workload, LSB will require external support from a procurement consultant. The Bank has provided training on procurement regulations to LSB staff. Procurement risk is assessed as Moderate. A procurement capacity and risk assessment of the LSB conducted by the World Bank identified the following procurement risks: (a) lack of adequate LSB staff and staff who has experience with World Bank Procurement Regulations and ICT system procurement that may lead to delays and noncompliance, (b) lengthy internal procurement review and approval process; (c) weak coordination between the LSB and other key stakeholders and with other line ministries that could result in delays; and (d) governance risks associated with conflict of interest and transparency. To mitigate the risks, the following actions are recommended: a. A full-time national procurement consultant to be hired to provide procurement support and further build capacity within LSB. b. To ensure that capacity is built and sustained within LSB, they will assign at least one government staff to be assigned as the procurement officer to work alongside the consultant. c. The Bank will provide additional procurement training, including the use of STEP training to LSB staff. d. The POM from LAOSTAT will be updated to clearly define roles and responsibilities and strengthen the coordination arrangements between the LSB and other implementing units and line ministries to ensure well- coordinated implementation. e. All procurement related documents will be posted on the LSB website. 23. Procurement strategy and procurement approach. LSB has prepared a Project Procurement Strategy for Page 53 of 58 The World Bank Lao PDR Strengthening the National Statistical System (P178002) Development (PPSD) for the project with support from the World Bank team, and the document will be available in separate project files and uploaded in STEP. The PPSD presents how procurement activities will support PDOs of the project and deliver the best value for money under a risk-based approach. In addition, the PPSD includes the rationales for procurement decisions, including selection of the approach to market and procurement methods. The PPSD and the Procurement Plan of the project shall be regularly updated as appropriate during project implementation. 24. The PPSD has identified selection of a twinning consulting firm (with an estimated cost of US$1.0m) and other ICT system developments (centralized data portal and integrated data platform system and software design – US$1.1m) as the major procurement activities attractive to the international market. To avert the risk of late selection of the twinning firm as happened in the predecessor project, advance procurement is recommended (i.e., preparing the draft TOR, launching the request for expression of interest, and drafting of the RFP) before the project becomes effective. Other than the above, consulting services under the project are relatively small assignments, such as ICT assessment and strategy (US$50,000), Services for security enhancement (US$120,000), annual external financial audit (US$100,000), and individual consultants for components 1 and 2 and PIMT consultants, with cost estimates ranging from US$10,000 to US$300,000. The PPSD analysis recommends that most of small consulting services would be carried out by national consulting firms, and national approach is considered as appropriate using the Consultants’ Qualifications Selection (CQS) method. For the major procurement activities above (over US$300,000), Quality and Cost Based Selection (QCBS) or Quality Based Selection (QBS) will be used. For contracts with individuals for some positions that do not require international experience and for which there are individuals with the qualifications and experience in the national market, national approach would be used. The international market approach would be considered for contracts that need international experience, in which case hiring international consultants would be beneficial for project implementation. 25.
