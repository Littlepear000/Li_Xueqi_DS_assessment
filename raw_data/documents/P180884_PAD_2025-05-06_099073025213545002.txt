Project Strategic Context 1. Cameroon is endowed with abundant resources and economic potential, but its development is hampered by significant and multifaceted challenges. As a lower-middle-income country with a Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita of US$1,7361, Cameroon has experienced limited and volatile economic growth over the past four decades, underperforming its structural peers in the region and its aspirational peers globally2. By 2022, the population living in poverty had risen to over 10 million out of the total population of 29 million3. Nearly 87 percent of all jobs in Cameroon are informal4. Urban areas, where the majority of formal jobs and the greatest economic potential are located, face significant challenges. Fundamental challenges include: (i) a severe deficit in urban infrastructure, exacerbated by rapid population growth, unplanned urban expansion, and climate change; and (ii) inadequate land administration as bottlenecks for infrastructure and private sector development. These structural weaknesses impede livability, connectivity and productivity, limit economic opportunities, discourage private investment, and highlight the urgent need for increased infrastructure investment, and better policies and governance to support sustainable growth and job creation in cities. 2. Ongoing crises such as conflict and climate change have undermined livelihoods, escalated forced displacement, and worsened the country’s economic prospects and livability. The United Nations Office High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) estimated more than 2.1 million people were forcibly displaced in Cameroon as of February 2025, mainly due to the Boko Haram insurgency in the Far North region, the socio-political crisis in the South-West and North- West regions, and the influx of over 400,000 refugees from the conflicts in the Central African Republic (CAR) and Nigeria5. Cameroon has faced increasingly high climate and disaster risks from floods, droughts and landslides, leading to increased vulnerability of lower-income and at-risk households, dwindling resources, rampant food insecurity, and rising social tensions. The severe flooding in the Far North region from July to October 2024 as a recent climate-related disaster, led to 38 casualties, affected more than 448,000 people, destroyed over 56,000 homes, and damaged numerous critical infrastructures6. The combination of these security challenges and climate change impacts has highlighted the need for investment in public infrastructure and greater resilience. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Cameroon’s high and rapid rate of urbanization has not yet been fully leveraged for its socio-economic development potential. By 2023, 59 percent of Cameroon’s population lived in cities, with 51 percent of these urban dwellers concentrated in the two largest cities of Yaoundé and Douala7. With an annual urbanization growth rate of 3.6 percent, the urbanization rate is expected to reach 73 percent by 20507. However, rapid urbanization has not led to significant economic growth or job creation. Not only does Cameroon lag far behind successful Asian countries that have leveraged urbanization to drive economic transformation, but the country also lags behind regional peers such as Ghana, Nigeria and Côte d’Ivoire with comparable rates of urbanization8. While Cameroon's cities offer higher median salaries9 than rural areas and are home to most of the country’s businesses4, Douala and Yaoundé have the country’s highest unemployment rates (15.4 percent and 11.7 percent respectively, compared to the national average of 6.1 percent in 20214). The urban poverty rate, which had declined between 2001 and 2014, has almost doubled from 8.9 percent in 2014 to 16.5 percent in 202110. In parallel, as urbanization continues, Cameroon’s outdated land administration has not sufficiently enforced land-use plans or secured urban land tenure, leading to an increase in land disputes, significant bottlenecks in infrastructure projects, and a land market that is not functional. Leveraging urbanization for economic transformation in Africa requires systematic investment in urban infrastructure, land administration services, and institutions to enable job creation, private sector-led economic growth and poverty reduction11. 4. Yaoundé, the political and administrative capital of Cameroon, faces rapid, uncontrolled urban growth coupled with inadequate infrastructure and climate resilience. Yaoundé’s GDP is estimated at 14.4 percent of the national GDP, growing at an average rate of about 2.3 percent over the past decade12. The city accounts for about 26 percent of all formal private sector and state-owned enterprise employment in the country4. The central urban areas concentrate jobs and services. Access to these jobs and services, and mobility in general, are hampered by inadequate road networks and dilapidated road conditions, resulting in severe congestion and long commuting times13 which reduce productivity and livability. Although Yaoundé has benefitted from development partner investments over the past three decades, its drainage systems remain inadequate to meet the needs of its growing population and the challenges from climate change14. The 2023 landslide in Yaoundé killed more than 30 people and disproportionately affected informal and vulnerable neighborhoods15. Although the city’s sanitation master plan prioritizes drainage infrastructure, its implementation is hampered by insufficient funding and fragmented coordination. Compounding the challenges, Yaoundé’s rapid urban sprawl - from a footprint of 38 km² in 1980 to over 300 km² today16 – has increased demands on urban infrastructure and compromised climate resilience. Poorly regulated land markets and weak land administration exacerbate these issues, making it difficult to sustainably manage urban growth and infrastructure development. 5. Douala, Cameroon’s economic capital and main port city, faces significant urbanization challenges despite its essential role in the country’s economy. Douala contributes to over 50 percent of national formal jobs and 32 percent of national GDP, and is home to 65 percent of Cameroon’s large companies and 75 percent of the country’s industrial units4. Like Yaoundé, economic activities in Douala are concentrated in the city center, resulting in long commutes from the outskirts, and are characterized by heavy traffic congestion and inadequate road infrastructure. Between 1975 and 2014, Douala’s built-up areas grew at an alarming annual rate of 12.8 percent17. While Douala’s development strategy strives to improve livability and position the city as an economic leader, rapid and uncontrolled urban expansion is placing significant pressure on land, including human settlements on natural drainage and mangroves, a challenge exacerbated by inadequate land administration. The loss of green, natural areas exacerbates vulnerability to climate change, and inadequate stormwater management increases the risk of flooding. The 2015 City Master Plan aimed to address these issues through investment in major road networks, better enforcement of spatial planning, green spaces and public facilities for people, and more affordable housing. However, its implementation is not sufficient. The implementation of the Master Plan is partially supported by the World Bank-financed Inclusive and Resilient Cities Development Project (Projet de Développement des Villes Inclusives et Résilientes, PDVIR, P156210) and Douala Urban Mobility Project (Projet de Mobilité Urbaine de Douala, PMUD, P167795), which focus on priority Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) corridors, nodes, and infrastructure around vulnerable neighborhoods. Targeted interventions at the nexus of urbanization and climate are essential to reduce emissions and increase climate resilience through sustainable urban development. Globally, cities account for 70 percent greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions and almost all face significant risks. In Cameroon, urban vulnerability is mainly a result of increasing climate and disaster risks and a lack of risk-informed urban land-use planning, enforcement and infrastructure. The increasing frequency and intensity of disasters in cities, and the massive urban settlements in high-risk zones, have led to a severe loss of permeable and natural areas and have drawn increased attention to urban resilience. In this context, the Government of Cameroon (GoC) ratified the Paris Agreement in 2016 and submitted its updated Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) to the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) in 2021, aiming at a 35 percent reduction in GHG emissions by 2030, conditional on financing. The updated NDC outlines Cameroon’s adaptation vision, prioritizing urban development and public works. Aligned with these goals, the Cameroon Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) highlighted the urgency of actions to avoid high-carbon urbanization, and proposes five key actions: (i) sustainable, compact urban planning; (ii) strong policy enforcement; (iii) a green housing policy; (iv) energy- efficient building regulations; and (v) climate adaptation strategies. In line with the NDC, the National Adaptation Plan for Climate Change, and the CCDR, this project will support low-carbon urban development through investments that incorporate energy-efficient measures, green spaces, nature-based solutions (NBS), and compact urbanization, as well as enhance urban resilience and climate adaptation through improved drainage, stormwater and disaster risk management. 7. The main binding constraints limiting sustainable urban growth in Cameroon are: (i) significant urban infrastructure deficit; (ii) inefficient governance, policies, institutional capacity, and operational instruments for sustainable urbanization; and (iii) inadequate land administration services. These constraints are elaborated as follows. 8. Yaoundé and Douala face a severe infrastructure deficit, particularly in terms of urban roads, drainage, and services to vulnerable neighborhoods. Home to approximately 30 percent of the country’s population18, the two cities struggle with inadequate road networks, frequent flooding and other climate-related disasters, and ubiquitous slums. In 2019, urban roads in Yaoundé and Douala handled approximately 5 million trips per day, and congestion has worsened due to insufficient, under-designed, and poorly maintained roads19. A lack of asset management and maintenance, inadequate financing, and weak climate resilience have led to deteriorating infrastructure conditions, with widespread potholes exacerbating congestion, posing safety risks, and causing economic losses. These challenges underscore the urgent need for targeted investments in roads, drainage, and community-level infrastructure for inclusive and sustainable growth, in line with the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 11: Make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient, and sustainable. Given the scale and complexity of the infrastructure challenges in Yaoundé and Douala, addressing their deficits remain a national priority, as their economic and demographic weight significantly influences the country's urbanization trajectory20. Experiences from PDVIR and recent projects call for continued investments in the two cities through a climate-resilient, spatially coordinated way while incorporating the life cycle of infrastructure assets. 9. Policy and technical support and institutional capacity building are needed at the national and city levels for sustainable urbanization. The Ministry of Housing and Urban Development (Ministère de l’Habitat et du Développement Urbain, MINHDU) oversees urban development policies, operational instruments, and investments in urban infrastructure and upgrading. To implement the National Development Strategy 2030 (NDS-30), the GoC is in urgent need of developing two critical policy frameworks: the National Cities Modernization Plan (NCMP) and the operational strategy of the National Slum Upgrading Strategy. MINHDU has been tasked to lead the development of these key strategies with stakeholders, which are essential for the national framework and operationalization of the sustainable transformation of livable cities across the country, building on the lessons from the two leading cities. Land administration in Cameroon is characterized by limited institutional arrangements, outdated policy and legal frameworks, inadequate coordination, widespread informality of land tenure, and insufficient financial resources, hindering economic growth in cities such as Yaoundé and Douala. Cameroon’s land administration system is based on an outdated legal framework anchored in the 1974 Land Ordinances21. At the national level, the formal land administration system, managed by the Ministry of State Property, Surveys and Land Tenure (Ministère des Domaines, du Cadastre et des Affaires Foncières, MINDCAF), remains largely paper-based and is plagued by inefficiencies, lengthy and costly procedures, and growing tenure insecurity, with an increasing number of land titles being contested or cancelled. Land disputes dominate the administrative and civil courts, accounting for 85 percent and 65 percent of cases respectively22. This is exacerbated by the fragmented land tenure system, which includes customary systems managed by community leaders and a formal system inaccessible to most of the population. Only 18 percent of households hold formal land titles4, leaving the majority to rely on informal, often contested transactions. Limited land administration and the absence of a well- functioning land market have encouraged speculative land practices, driving up land prices and making formal housing unaffordable for the majority of urban dwellers. As a result, informal settlements expand rapidly, often in hazardous locations with limited access to infrastructure and services, exacerbating social inequalities and climate vulnerability. As noted in the Country Private Sector Diagnostic (CPSD), securing land for business in Yaoundé and Douala is difficult and expensive, and land acquisition and title security are the key concerns for residential and commercial developers. Despite legal protections, women face additional barriers to land ownership due to gender-biased customs. These challenges underscore the urgency to improve the land-related policy and legal frameworks, modernize land administration services, and introduce mechanisms to land tenure security. 11. Past efforts to reform land policy and modernize land administration in Cameroon have yielded only limited results. The 2005 reform, the most significant in the past two decades, aimed to decentralize the issuance of land titles by shifting responsibility from the central office to regional and divisional offices23. While this reform significantly reduced the processing time for land title applications, it increased land title insecurity due to growing irregularities in the issuance process. In 2010, at the behest of the International Finance Corporation (IFC) supported Cameroon Business Forum, the GoC introduced One-Stop Shops (OSSs) in Yaoundé and Douala to speed up land registration for businesses. However, the results have been mixed. Although the digitalization of land administration, particularly the cadaster, has been a long- term priority for the GoC, limited budget allocations to MINDCAF and other constraints have prevented substantial progress. The most notable initiative in this regard has been the African Development Bank-funded Land Modernization and Business Climate Support Project (Projet d’Appui à la Modernisation du Cadastre et au Climat des Affaires, PAMOCCA). The PAMOCCA contributed to modernizing Cameroon’s land administration by establishing a geodetic network, digitizing land registry archives in selected cities (excluding Yaoundé and Douala), introducing a new land administration diploma for staff training, and providing logistical support to MINDCAF. Nevertheless, building on the PAMOCCA, significant efforts are needed to continue the digitalization of the country’s land administration system. 12. C. Project Beneficiaries 16. In total, the project will benefit an estimated 2.1 million people in Cameroon. The main beneficiary groups are as follows. First, residents and businesses in and around urban neighborhoods that receive the physical investments in Yaoundé and Douala will benefit directly or indirectly from improved access to urban infrastructure. Second, people in Wouri and Mfoundi, where Douala and Yaoundé are located, will benefit from the issuance of land titles with improved tenure security, including an increased percentage of female beneficiaries. Third, people and institutions involved in urban development and land administration will benefit from increased capacity, efficiency, transparency and accountability. Fourth, additional benefits will come from more and better jobs, such as labor-intensive civil works and jobs related to sustainable urban and land administration services, as well as induced benefits to the private sector and citizens. In addition, an assessment of gender and vulnerability gaps and measures will maximize the benefits for women, girls, and the vulnerable population groups, including the poor and internally displaced people. D. Project Components 17. The project adopts a multi-sectoral approach to invest in urban development and land administration while adapting to the challenges of climate change. The integrated project invests mainly in climate-resilient infrastructure and land administration services. The project consists of four integrated components. The first component invests in critical urban infrastructure, accompanied by policy and technical support and capacity building focusing on priorities and operationalization. The second component complements the first to address the critical, chronic land administration bottlenecks that hinder sustainable urbanization and private sector growth. The third and fourth components invest in project management and emergency preparedness, respectively, to ensure effective project implementation and rapid response to emergencies. The cities of Yaoundé and Douala are leading economic agglomerations and are expected to drive growth and job creation in the country and provide demonstration effects for other cities in Cameroon. In addition, the project incorporates multi-stakeholder processes and actively involves citizens and beneficiaries. GBV remains a critical issue in Cameroon, including in urban centers such as Yaoundé and Douala. Women and girls face violence in both private and public spaces, with 44 percent of women in these cities having experienced physical violence since the age of 1543. Only 22 percent of survivors seek help due to stigma, fear, and inadequate support. Only 39 percent of women who report experiencing GBV seek help from available services, while others turn to informal networks that do not always provide effective interventions44. Social, medical and legal service workers lack training in trauma-informed care, resulting in inadequate psychosocial support for survivors. Poor coordination between health and social services further limits survivors' access to support. Community-based support exists, but lacks sufficient funding and legitimacy in unfamiliar areas. GBV information centers and emergency shelters remain scarce, leaving survivors with few options for and forcing many to remain in abusive situations. While the Ministry of Women’s Empowerment and the Family (Ministère de la Promotion de la Femme et de la Famille, MINPROFF) has established Women and Family Promotion Centers (CPFFs) in Douala and Yaoundé, they focus on education and training activities rather than GBV support. Coordination with other services remains weak. However, these existing centers, such as the one run by Elles Cameroun in Douala, can be considered unhygienic and lack beds, and the uncertainty of their funding does not guarantee their continued operation. 80. GBV Response and Support. The project will strengthen GBV response services by constructing or rehabilitating two pilot centers in Yaoundé and Douala, complementing and reinforcing the project’s GBV: Sexual Exploitation, Abuse, and Harassment (SEA/SH) Action Plan. These centers will provide safe shelter, psychosocial support, legal aid, and health care, with a focus on survivor-centered care. Center procedures will also outline the roles and responsibilities of the center staff, establish standards of confidentiality, and ensure monitoring and evaluation mechanisms to maintain the quality and effectiveness of the services provided. A referral system will map multi-sectoral services and involve community leaders to improve victim identification and support, and awareness and communication materials will inform survivors of available services, including hotlines and safe reporting channels. The project will work closely with the Ministry of Social Affairs (Ministère des Affaires Sociales, MINAS) and MINPROFF to integrate the centers into the broader social welfare system and contribute to policy alignment and capacity building. 81. Land Tenure Security for Women. Women in Cameroon face barriers to housing, land, and property rights, with only 24 percent of land titles in Wouri and Mfoundi registered to women45. Although Cameroonian law officially grants men and women equal rights to land ownership, deep-rooted customary practices, particularly around marriage and inheritance, often override these legal provisions, severely limiting women's ability to own or inherit property. Many women are unaware of their legal rights, or the procedures required to obtain land certificates, leaving them vulnerable to fraud and eviction, and excluded from land ownership and legal transactions. 82. The project will take actions to support women’s land rights. The project will provide legal assistance to simplify the complexities of land registration within the MINDCAF for women, including widows, single mothers, and female- headed households. The project will also support the existing "legal clinics” already established by the MINDCAF to support women. Awareness-raising campaigns will educate women and their husbands about their rights, land administration, and the necessary steps to obtain legal aid and land certificates. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a project supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms or the World Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly reviewed in order to address project-related concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the World Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of World Bank non-compliance with its policies and procedures, and the Dispute Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints through dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the attention of Bank Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond. Climate Vulnerability and Risk Analysis 8. Cameroon is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climate change and its climate readiness is limited. 64 The average annual temperature has increased and mean annual rainfall has decreased over the past decades. Average annual temperature has gone up by 0.86°C over 46 years, from 24.28°C in 1974 to 25.14°C in 2020. The warming has been more significant in the north of Cameroon and most rapid in December through February and September through November.65 Under the worst-case climate scenario, temperatures are projected to rise on average by 3.9°C over the next two decades with wide variations across regions. Rainfall is expected to increase from an average of 5.8 millimeters over the next 20 years to 10.4 millimeters by 210066, although precipitation levels will vary throughout the country.67 9. Both Douala and Yaoundé are at high risk of climate-related disasters. Douala and Yaoundé are among the most flood- prone cities in Cameroon, with frequent flood events that have caused significant damage to the economy, infrastructure, and livestock and led to loss of life.6869 The department of Wouri is exposed to medium risks of floods, extreme temperatures, and wildfires; its capital, Douala, is a coastal city facing increasing risks from floods, climate-induced erosion and landslides, and violent winds as a result of warming oceans and rising sea levels65. Yaoundé is also exposed to extreme weather events, including intense precipitations that, among other factors, have caused floods. In fact, in 2021, Yaoundé experienced severe flooding, with the central and southern regions of the city being the hardest hit. Streets and buildings were inundated, and vehicles were left stranded68. An increased frequency, duration or intensity of climate events could lead to significant losses and damage. 10.
