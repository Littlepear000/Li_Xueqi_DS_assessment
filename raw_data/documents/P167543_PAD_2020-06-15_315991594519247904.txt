Page 6 of 89 The World Bank Niger: Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion Project (P167543) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Niger is a large, landlocked and poor country in the arid Sahel region of West Africa. Most of the country's territory is sparsely populated or uninhabitable desert with a low population density (18 inhabitants per sq. km) and a low rate of urbanization (more than 81 percent of the population in Niger lives in rural areas1). Around 50 percent of Niger's estimated 20.7 million inhabitants are younger than 15 years old and 75 percent are under 25. The current population growth rate is over 4 percent with a particularly high rate of births to young mothers. This young population is predominantly under-employed with a literacy rate of only 29 percent (15 percent among young women). With a poverty rate of 44.1 percent and a per capita annual income of just US$420, Niger is one of the world’s poorest nations. 2. Low rural productivity, insufficient human capital, and weak governance hamper Niger’s development. According to the June 2017 Systematic Country Diagnostic (SCD), Niger’s three principal challenges that inhibit the country’s economic growth and constrain poverty reduction are: (i) a low rural productivity, caused in part by irrigation problems, limited adoptions of new technologies, and lack of access to finance; (ii) insufficient human capital due, in particular, to inadequate skills and training and poor provision of public services; and (iii) weak governance in key sectors, such as agriculture and telecoms, caused by insecurity and lack of public service delivery which in turn leads to limited economic opportunities. 3. Niger is a fragile environment, and the security situation has deteriorated in recent months. Niger has been integrated on the list of Fragile, Conflict-afflicted Situations (FCS) countries, as of 1 July 20202, and throughout the new IDA19 cycle, as a Medium-Intensity Conflict country. Niger has ranked near to last in the United Nations (UN) Human Development Index since 2010. Meanwhile, external pressures due to conflicts involving violent extremist groups in neighboring Mali, Burkina Faso and Nigeria, and the lack of a state presence in southern Libya, have dramatically increased internal risks to Niger, with the potential to fuel pre-existing inter-communal tensions. Responding to security and humanitarian needs is further stretching already limited public resources. At the same time, weak governance and dissatisfaction over the management of public resources (including mining revenues) and the delivery of services is reinforcing grievances and encouraging alternative – especially religious - forms of mobilization. 4. The recent outbreak of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) caused by the 2019 novel coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) has been spreading rapidly across the world and is likely to have severe economic and social consequences. There are 978 confirmed cases and 65 deaths as of June 12, 2020 in Niger. The global COVID-19 outbreak is expected to have a significant negative impact on Africa’s economy. The World Bank predicts that economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) will decline from 2.4 percent in 2019 to as low as -5.1 percent in 2020, bringing the first recession in 25 years.3 The effects of the pandemic-driven global economic downturn and its impact on Niger’s economy are difficult to predict at this stage, but it is likely that impact will be significant and negative. 5. Niger expects to take full advantage of the digital transformation, which has the potential to impact all sectors of socio-economic life. Niger's ambition is to use digital infrastructures and services to modernize its economy, strengthen its growth, reinforce security, improve its governance and support efforts in key sectors including: agriculture5, access to financial services, health, education and youth entrepreneurship. The “Niger 2.0” Strategy – implemented under the leadership of the National Information Society Agency (Agence Nationale pour la Société de l’Information, ANSI) – is designed to bring significant impact of digital transformation in just a few years, with a strong focus on rural areas. As part of the Niger 2.0 Strategy, the Smart Villages Program is aiming at leveraging digital technologies with the objective of reducing poverty and increasing incomes, improving citizens’ living conditions and resiliency to shocks. In addition, the program intends to enhance social inclusion, to develop e-Government services such as e-Agriculture, e-Health and e- Education, to reduce the gap in the access and use of digital services between men and women and finally to support financial inclusion through access to mobile money accounts and services. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context Digital infrastructure and telecoms / information and communication technologies (ICT) sectoral and institutional context 6. Niger has one of the least developed digital infrastructures in the world and ranks at the bottom of most international digital rankings. The development of digital infrastructure is lagging in Niger and indeed the country was rated in last position (176th out of 176 economies) in the 2017 ICT Development Index (IDI) of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU)6. The market structure in telecommunications is reasonably competitive, with four players competing for the market. These are Airtel Niger (90 per cent owned by Bharti Airtel of India), Orange Niger (previously owned by the French Orange Group and now owned by the Niger company Zamani Com), Moov Niger (owned by Maroc Telecoms, which is in turn 48 per cent owned by Etisalat, UAE) and Niger Telecoms (formerly Sonitel and SahelCom, which were merged in 2016; a 100 per cent state-owned enterprise that has been through a number of unsuccessful privatization attempts). As of late 2019, Airtel was the market leader with around 42 per cent of the market followed by Moov (26 per cent) and Orange (24 per cent), with Niger Telecom trailing with just 7 per cent. However, Niger Telecom benefits from an effective monopoly in backbone fiber as it has legal protection which prevents other operators from laying fiber on routes where it already has capacity available. Investor confidence in the market received a jolt with the decision of Orange Group to sell its entire share in Orange Niger to Zamani Com, which is owned by local business interests, effectively withdrawing from the Niger telecom market. The sale was completed in November 2019. 9. At least 10 percent of the population has no mobile telephony coverage, and around half of the population is not covered by mobile broadband. According to figures provided by the sector regulator (ARCEP), there are approximately 6,092 villages representing a total population of 1.93 million (around 320,000 households), that still have no cellular coverage, even for voice services (2G). In addition, there are still gaps in the extent and coverage of rural and urban municipalities as well as rural communes. For example, 12 percent of the population of rural communes is still not covered, and this figure rises to 46 percent in Agadez, 27 percent in Diffa and 20 percent in Zinder9. While many African countries are experiencing an explosive growth in internet penetration, driven primarily by 3G and 4G mobile services, the ownership of smartphones remains low in Niger, and mobile broadband (3G/4G) networks cover only half of the population compared to 70 percent for Africa according to the GSMA. 10. Several factors help explain the dismal performance of digital infrastructures: notably a high share of the population living in rural areas (84 percent of the total population compared to 60 percent for SSA – see Figure 1.3), limited access to electricity (11 percent of the rural population have access to electricity compared to 23 percent for SSA10), and high mobile prices (a basket of mobile services represents 33 percent of monthly Gross National Income (GNI) per capita compared to 14 percent for Africa). The lack of affordability can be partly attributed to a high level of sector taxation, while inadequate mobile applications and services (that are not always available in local languages) and low international connectivity11 contribute to a relatively low level of usage by regional standards. Digital financial services 11. Niger is one of the weakest performers in financial inclusion in Sub Saharan Africa and in the West Africa Economy Monetary Union (Union Économique et Monétaire Ouest Africaine, WAEMU) region. According to the 2017 World Bank Financial Inclusion index (Findex), 84 percent of the adult population in Niger is not part of the financial system compared to an average of 58 percent in SSA, 57 percent in Burkina Faso, and 65 percent in Mali. Only 2 percent of the adult population has access to credit, one of the lowest rates in the world. Limitations in financial access are particularly acute among women and people living in rural areas. COVID-19 is strongly affecting the delivery of access to financial services. The crisis thus requires an urgent need to shift to digital channels to deliver financial services. 12. While the constraints to financial inclusion resemble those of other countries in WAEMU, they are more accentuated in Niger. Constraints to financial inclusion include low proximity of financial services providers, restrictive approach of banks, underperformance of the microfinance sector, limited financial literacy, and weak financial infrastructure. Low physical access to financial institutions is one of the biggest issues in Niger given the very low population density with 18 people per sq. km which makes brick and mortars branches unprofitable. According to the International Monetary Fund (IMF) Financial Access Survey12, there are only 0.13 commercial banks per 1,000 km2 meaning that it takes several hours or even a whole day for a customer to visit a bank branch. 13. In addition to the low proximity issues, the microfinance sector is facing a crisis13 with more than 17 percent of non- performing loans and over 70 percent of the microfinance institutions in financial distress14. Another issue pertains to the high difficulties of populations to fulfill banks’ customer due diligence requirements (formal identification (ID), proof of address and income) which contribute to restrain access to banking services. Indeed, it is estimated that less than 13 percent of the population has a formal ID form. There are no formal physical addresses in rural areas. The lack of information on rural populations is also a big constraint to access to credit15. 14. While digital financial services are considered the most effective ways to improve financial inclusion, they remain underdeveloped in Niger. The ratio value of financial transactions through mobile to gross domestic product (GDP) is one of the lowest in the region, representing 3.7 percent compared to 33.8 percent in Mali and 58.2 percent in Burkina Faso 16. Access and usage of digital financial services in Niger remains among the lowest in SSA. In 2017, according to Findex, the percentage of adult population with a mobile money account doubled from 3 percent in 2014 to 9 percent, but was still low compared to the average of 37 percent in SSA. Access to first generation digital financial services (e.g. person to person -P2P transfers, bill payments through mobile) remains limited and second generation of digital financial services (e.g., savings and microcredit) is barely available. Total number of mobile money accounts is estimated at 2.8 million, representing twice the number of banks and Multilateral Financial Institution (MFI) accounts in Niger, but half the number of mobile money account in Mali, and one third of the number of Burkina Faso. Only 7 percent of the adult population in Niger had received or sent money through mobile phones compared to an average 21 percent in SSA. 3.1 percent of adult population has received payments for agriculture products. The project is aligned with the Niger Economic and Social Development Plan 2017-2021 (Plan de Développement Economique et Social, PDES) and with the national digital strategy for Niger. The GoN has included in its PDES the expansion of broadband coverage on the territory (sub-program 5.3) and the development of financial services (sub- program 7.3). It has also developed a “Niger 2.0” Strategy, which is implemented under the leadership of ANSI; the strategy has identified several priority programs, including the Smart Villages Program focused on connecting villages in rural areas and delivering digital applications and services to the population. It is important to differentiate between the Smart Villages Program which aims to provide a full range of digitally transformative services to rural areas, and this Smart Villages Project which covers selected foundational components of the Smart Villages Program, namely extending cellphone and broadband access and usage of mobile money in rural areas. 17. The project is aligned with the FY18-22 Country Partnership Framework (CPF17), and more specifically with “Focus area 3: better governance for jobs, service delivery, and growth / Objective 6: improved access to digital services.” Under this objective, the CPF aims to foster the expansion of digital networks in rural areas by the private sector and developing the use of key digital applications and services by the population. Rationale for World Bank Involvement and Role of Partners 44. By targeting unserved villages, the proposed project would bring valuable economic and social benefits to a portion of the population of Niger, which would otherwise remain excluded without public intervention. This population is defined by low capita incomes, low average revenue per user (ARPU) and limited private infrastructure and services offerings in low density and remote rural areas. Private operators, which are the main providers of digital services, have little incentive to extend services to rural areas and communities, where the prospects for financial return are limited or even negative. The Mobilizing Finance for Development (MFD) approach (see Annex 1) would be applied to both the universal communications and financial inclusion agendas. 45. The World Bank is well positioned to support the Government’s aspirations to bring universal coverage of digital services to its citizens and to modernize public service delivery and financial inclusion through the adoption of digital solutions. The World Bank has been supporting these agendas across the six global regions for several decades. World Bank support to ongoing projects has included universal access to communications and key elements of a Digital Government. It is envisioned that the Project would take both agendas to the next level, increasing significantly the impact footprint in terms of the number of beneficiaries and value-add of the interventions and foster the Digital Economy in Niger. The World Bank’s value-added arises first from its technical expertise and global knowledge in addressing the identified issues in the ICT sector of Niger, which are found in many countries around the world. The World Bank has been involved in the digital sector of Niger for the past two years, through technical assistances (on the operationalization of the universal access fund and the development of the digital ecosystem) and specific reforms within a Development Policy Operation (DPO). 46. To reach the objectives set during implementation, the involvement of the World Bank appears crucial, as the policy reforms considered in this project are ambitious, complex, and politically sensitive . Playing its role of “honest broker”, the World Bank is in a unique position to provide the needed expertise and the neutral advice, to remain involved in the long term, and to gain buy-in and support from government officials at the highest level. The World Bank will be able to appropriately collaborate and coordinate with other development partners to ensure the complementarity with other initiatives involved in the development of the ICT sector. For example, the African Development Bank (AfDB) is currently implementing a project in the sector of around US$36 million, involving the rolling-out of around 1,070km of fiber optic network (connecting Chad, Niger, Algeria, and Nigeria). This will certainly benefit implementation of the Smart Villages project, by reducing the costs of international bandwidth. 47. The Smart Villages project has a relatively narrow focus, with funds devoted mainly to extending connectivity in rural areas and enhancing the provision of digital financial services. But it is part of a wider Niger 2.0 vision which unites the actions of many other development partners, donors and private sector actors in the country. For instance, the African Development Bank is financing the construction a regional fiber cable; the ITU helped develop the vision document for smart villages, while implementing companies for the pilot smart villages include Intelsat, Avanti and Niger Telecom. The project will also ensure effective collaboration and synergies with other projects in the WBG portfolio in Niger, as illustrated in Annex Table 1.2. Digital connectivity and services have increasingly become a basic necessity for households and yet remain out of reach for many segments of the population for accessibility and affordability reasons. Reducing this ‘digital divide’ by improving access to those services (by reducing prices, increasing coverage and improving quality of services), especially for unserved or underserved villages and population segments, can significantly reduce the opportunities gap between the haves and have nots. Expanding connectivity to rural villages will increase the number of individuals and businesses that have access to broadband internet, telephony and digital financial services, which will benefit most, if not all, social and economic sectors in these areas. The Niger authorities will also benefit from increased connectivity for their own needs, i.e. to carry out its duties in a more efficient, effective and transparent manner. C. Sustainability 59. The MFD approach will ensure the financial and economic viability of the investments in white zones. The purpose of the MFD approach proposed in Component 2 is to optimize the use of public funding to make the investment commercially viable in the long term. By increasing demand, Component 3 will reinforce the commercial viability of the infrastructure. In addition, Component 2 will put the emphasis on local ownership through the active participation of the local populations in the works and the proposed maintenance schemes, which will contribute strongly to the durability of the infrastructure. 60. The development of digital services will be based on their long-term viability. Among all the criteria used to select the proposed services and platforms, sustainability will be one of the key aspects looked at by the ANSI. Over the past decade, many digital services for rural development pilots and initiatives have been launched with limited impact and very low viability. This is due to several factors: (a) there is often lack of ownership with off-the shelf solutions pushed to the users, or replicated from other countries or context; (b) the pilot provides the technical solutions for free or without any cost-recovery plan, and afterwards the users are not ready to pay after the pilot is over; and (c) the technology adoption and digital literacy have been an obstacle to scale-up, so the pilots rarely reached a critical mass of users ensuring the financial viability of the service. Component 3 will address the three issues in parallel by providing customized training to the users, build the viability of the service in the initial design, and ensuring ownership by involving the end user in the design of the service. Component 2 – The technical design of Component 2 builds on lessons learned and best practices of similar projects that have been implemented in other countries26, as well as the recent 2018 report on “Innovative Business Models for expanding Fiber-Optic Networks and Closing the Access Gaps" sponsored by The Digital Development Partnership of the Digital Development Global Practice. The infrastructure component is designed around four core principles (evidence of market failure, general interest as set out by the PDO, public capital funds limited to the minimum necessary, and open access PPPs). The bidding specifications will be technology neutral in order to allow as many operators as possible to participate in the bid and will not have a technology-based bias that could favor one operator over the others27. The bidding will request only minimum specifications, such as minimum internet speed, number of locations to be covered, size of population to be covered, and standard telecommunications services to be delivered such as voice and messaging services. The private operators will be able to select the design of their choice, which could include, among others: (i) passive telecom towers to facilitate the deployment of active mobile antennas by mobile network operators; (ii) a fully active Radio Access Network deployed by a mobile network operator, who provides a local roaming access to other mobile network operators; (iii) low-cost ‘microsite’ alternative deployed by third-parties for rural areas targeted by the project who then sell their services to mobile network operators; and (iv) other types of wireless access such as satellite access. The technical design is consistent with international best practice. Lastly, the design of the project is based on a model of competitive, private-sector delivery wherever possible. This is fully consistent with international experience which shows that this is a more cost-effective way of delivering digital services than the Government becoming directly involved in service delivery itself. 63. Project Structure – The Niger Smart Villages program is structured around the following components: a. Component 1: Enabling Environment – Creating an appropriate legal, regulatory and fiscal environment to promote growth of the ICT sector in favor of development and in favor of extending rural coverage of digital infrastructure; b. Component 2: Developing Rural Connectivity – Extending the number of villages in Niger that have access to digital connectivity (covering telephony, internet, mobile payments etc.); c. Component 3: Digital Financial inclusion – Promote financial inclusion in rural areas by supporting the development of digital financial services and their adoption; d. Component 4: Project Management and Stakeholder Capacity-Building—Efficient and effective management of the project, by a dedicated project implementation unit (PIU), with a focus also on providing relevant training for stakeholders. e. Component 5: Contingent Emergency Response Component (CERC) – An agile project structure that allows for additional funding as required to respond to unforeseen emergencies. 2. Component 1 will especially focus on creating a favorable environment for digital infrastructure and digital finance while Component 2 will seek to extend digital infrastructure in the targeted villages. These two components are designed around the MFD framework to leverage private investments and optimize the use of public funding. The MFD framework is supported by the Cascade approach, which is the “Operating System” for MFD . The WBG prioritizes private sector solutions, whenever possible, to promote judicious use of scarce public resources. This will also involve working in close cooperation with IFC where opportunities arise. 3. Where markets are not conducive to private investment, the WBG focuses on reforms that address market failures and constraints to private sector solutions at the country and sector level. This step relates to Component 1 of the project. 4. Where risks remain high, the cascade approach requires that priority applies to de-risking instruments such as guarantees and risk-sharing instruments. However, based on extensive discussions with the private sector in Niger – and also in other countries in the subregion – this step is less relevant in the context of rural areas in Niger, as these areas are highly unprofitable for telecommunications operators and require a more targeted measure to support the deployment of capital-intensive infrastructure. Thus, Component 2 proposes to use capital expenditures subsidies for construction of new communications antennae in rural areas as a way of bringing more people online. 5. Where market solutions are not possible through sector reform and risk mitigation, public resources will be used. The project will seek to establish a PPP framework with competitive awards of capital subsidies through a "least-cost subsidy auction" tender process to private operators. This step aligns with Component 2 of the project. The first cluster of activities will consist of technical assistance to strengthen key stakeholders (i.e. ANSI, the Ministry with responsibilities for ICT - MPTEN, the Regulatory Authority ARCEP) in defining, enhancing, and enforcing an enabling environment conducive to providing ICT coverage in rural areas. a. Activity 1.1. Technical assistance to perform a diagnostic of the legal bottlenecks and establish a strategy to increase private ICT investments, in collaboration with all key stakeholders – This technical assistance will provide a legal and regulatory due diligence to identify and alleviate the legal and regulatory bottlenecks for the extension of digital services in rural areas. It will focus on inter alia: (i) the extension and fostering of the general authorization regime for the electronic communications sector; (ii) the technological neutrality of telecom licenses; (iii) the opening and functioning of telecom wholesale markets, especially the national backbone and backhaul capacity wholesale market and the Tower Companies wholesale market; (iv) the possibility for third-parties to deploy Radio Access Networks (RAN) as a low-cost alternative to mobile cellular and open-source platforms for rural areas, and sell their technology and services to mobile network incumbents (whose supply chains may be closely tied to large equipment suppliers and may face regulatory constraints on spectrum usage); (v) the regulatory incentives for passive network-sharing (e.g. opening up a telecom tower (passive) to host multiple transmission equipment (active) from different mobile operators) and active network-sharing (e.g. with the provision of national roaming at the wholesale level); (vi) the increase in service competition with the possibility for all telecom actors – including Internet Service Providers – to provide voice services (e.g. Voice over IP (VoIP)); and (vii) the opportunity to adopt regulations to support the installation of telecommunications infrastructures in targeted rural areas. b. Activity 1.2. Technical assistance to increase the regulator’s capacity (ARCEP) in defining, monitoring and enforcing the coverage obligations of mobile operators – Defining, monitoring and enforcing the coverage obligations of mobile operators is one of the key objectives of the sectoral regulatory agency, ARCEP. Coverage obligations are meant to create incentives for operators to start commercially viable operations in less attractive markets. The technical assistance will provide a diagnostic of the existing coverage obligations and of the capacities of ARCEP in monitoring and enforcing them, and will provide recommendations on how the definition, monitoring, and enforcement of coverage obligations could be enhanced to further increase the coverage of mobile networks in rural areas. c. Activity 1.3. Technical assistance to increase the efficiency of the universal service fund (USF) and to develop a national digital equity strategy – this technical assistance will provide a diagnostic of the efficiency of the USF and will consider the opportunity for increasing the allocation of funds to rural connectivity projects, including through the implementation of a so-called “pay or play” mechanism for the USF, as defined in the new 2018 Law. The “pay or play” mechanism leaves the decision to operators: either they can contribute financially to the USF (“pay”), or they invest directly in projects in rural areas that are not commercially viable (“play”). By implementing the “pay or play” mechanism, Niger will ensure that operators invest effectively to provide digital connectivity in rural and remote areas. Associated with the work on universal service will be Technical Assistance to develop a National Digital Equality Strategy – this TA will provide a diagnostic of the digital gender gaps and accessibility gaps for other vulnerable populations (such as the elderly and those living with disabilities), with actionable recommendations that will inform policymakers and the support subsequent implementation of project components. Component 2 – Developing Rural Connectivity (US$63 million equivalent) 10. Guiding principles for designing the activities under Component 2 – The proposed component implies the use of project funds to support the extension of digital infrastructures through the award of “least-cost” capital subsidies through a competitive “reverse auction” tender process to private operators in order to extend digital connectivity in targeted areas which are commercially not viable. As this component relies on the use of project funds to stimulate Page 46 of 89 The World Bank Niger: Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion Project (P167543) private sector investment, its design was guided by four key principles – in line with international best practices and the cascade approach – that have been translated in the proposed activities: a. Principle of market failure – The proposed project will ensure that targeted rural areas are suffering from a market failure, i.e. that operators are not covering the targeted area and do not intend to do so in the medium term (even with the enhancement of the legal and regulatory framework or with a new round of private investments to increase coverage); b. Principle of general interest as set out by the PDO – The proposed project will target villages that best support the achievement of the PDO by striking a balance between targeting villages with the maximum social and economic impact (e.g., presence of schools or health centers), and the areas with the poorest and most vulnerable population. c. Principle of limiting public funding to the minimum necessary – The targeted villages should have, to the extent possible, the ‘enabling infrastructures’ to reduce deployment costs (i.e., access to the electrical network, a transport axis to convey equipment, and fiber backbone to connect the deployed ICT infrastructure to the core network), while taking into account that a relatively few villages in Niger currently benefit from of these ‘enabling infrastructures’, and some have none at all. The project design will ensure the best ‘value for money’ usage of project funds with the implementation of a PPP through a competitive tender process that will select the ‘most economically advantageous offer.’ It should be clear from the outset that the ‘most economically advantageous offer’ will not be solely based on final bid price to select the bidder requesting the lowest amount of project funds; rather, price and indicators measuring the different quality aspects of the offer will be considered (i.e., not a pure auction, but a hybrid process between a beauty contest and an auction). d. Principle of open access – The private entity deploying and managing the infrastructure with the support of public subsidies should respect the principle of ‘Open Access’, whereby the subsidized infrastructure is characterized by: (i) access provided at the wholesale level for all market players allowed to operate in the market; (ii) transparency on the terms of the open access arrangements; (iii) non-discriminatory terms between market players wishing to access the wireless network access infrastructure on the wholesale market; (iv) wholesale tariff-related obligations, with fair and reasonable wholesale tariffs in light of the costs incurred by the undertaking; and (v) enforcing effective access (to tackle potential deficient quality of service or delays in the provision of wholesale products or discrimination against a non-affiliated retail provider). 11. By connecting some 2,111 of these unserved villages to telecommunication networks, Niger will move closer to universal digital coverage, the goal set for 2030 in the AU Digital Transformation Strategy29, thereby bridging one of the key factors that underpins the urban rural divide and drives migration to the cities and abroad. The project will seek to promote the most cost-effective connectivity options in different regions (e.g. fiber optic, cellular communications, microwave and satellite coverage), with the private sector taking the lead (in line with the “cascade” approach from the MFD framework). 12. To support the extension of digital infrastructures, the project will implement a PPP framework with competitive awards of CAPEX subsidies through a "least-cost subsidy auction" tender process to private operators (see Box in Annex 1). The one-time CAPEX subsidies will support broadband infrastructure in targeted villages where market forces alone are insufficient to provide adequate broadband coverage (i.e. there is a clear market failure). As the digital infrastructures will be partly financed by public subsidies, the best practice is to implement “Open Access” PPP networks whereby the access to the infrastructure is characterized by five key principles: wholesale, transparent, non-discriminatory, fair, and effective access for all market players. IDA funds will be managed according to an open 29 https://au.int/en/pressreleases/20191026/african-digital-transformation-strategy-and-african-union- communication-and. Page 47 of 89 The World Bank Niger: Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion Project (P167543) access PPP Manual developed for the purpose of extending coverage. When designing the PPP, the guiding principle is that public intervention should serve to mobilize finance for development and limit as much as possible the risk of crowding out or replacing private investments, of altering commercial investment incentives, and ultimately of distorting competition (i.e. cascade principle). 13. For some of the targeted villages, and for those below 250 inhabitants, it is likely that a CAPEX subsidy will not be sufficient, and subsidies for OPEX may also be required for them to be viable. In this case, it is suggested that the Mobile Network Operators (MNO) take on the OPEX subsidies in lieu of their financial contributions to the FAU. Such a scheme is in line with the “pay or play” mechanism which is considered best practice for universal service fund management around the world. If operators choose to “play” rather than “pay”, then their contributions would need to be independently accounted, and the choice of villages to receive an OPEX subsidy would need to be pre-agreed with the Board of Management of the FAU. 14. For the 2,111 or so selected villages, an average investment of around US$60,000 per village is planned (based on a US$63 million budget for this component from IDA). Additional financing could come from the FAU, from other donors and mainly from the operators themselves through parallel financing, based on their competitive bids in the reverse auction (see Box 3). Most of this would go into a CAPEX subsidy to attract investment from the private mobile operators and tower companies and should be sufficient to provide the “light” technical solution to cover a village. Villages could be grouped in batches according to their characteristics and / or degree of priority and / or geographical area. The lots would be tendered in a sequential manner to take advantage of preliminary lessons learned from the ongoing pilot studies, as well as limitations and difficulties of implementation. It will be important to study the economic sustainability of different models of intervention, both for the mobile operators, and for digital entrepreneurs. For that reason, it is proposed that in the initial phase, different solutions may be provided in four sample zones – each of around 50 villages – to cover an initial set of 200 villages. In this pilot phase, commercial bids from operators would be evaluated primarily through a “beauty contest” approach, based on the innovativeness of the business model and technology proposed. Then, based on an evaluation of progress, the solution that proves the most sustainable would be used in the remaining 1,900 or so villages, divided into four lots of approximately 475 each. 15. In addition to basic connectivity, the project will also seek to moderately over-dimension the power supply installed to run the local digital connectivity infrastructure, so that spare electric power could be used for some essential needs (i.e. lights or fans in schools, health centre fridges for the conservation of medicine and food, mobile and electronic equipment recharging stations, etc.). Indeed, as the connectivity infrastructure would most likely be the only powered equipment in many of the targeted villages, the project will ensure that the benefits of sharing the electricity provided for the connectivity infrastructure will spread to the rural communities where they are deployed. One option could be to install moderately over-dimensioned additional solar and battery capacity, allowing for the extra available energy to be purchased by citizen and local businesses. Another option could be to encourage subproject developers and investors in the Smart Villages to submit unsolicited proposals to the National Rural Electrification Promotion Agency (Agence Nationale pour la Promotion de l’Electrification en Milieu Rural, ANPER) for electrification. This will build on Component 2 of the Niger Solar Electricity Access Project (NESAP, P160170) which was designed to create the environment and promote private sector investments in rural electrification. The technology solution would be resolved on a case by case basis, but it is likely to include isolated solar photovoltaic (PV) systems, with diesel mini-grids as back-up. Component 2 will consist of a series of technical assistance activities and financial support to increase the extension of ICT coverage in rural areas targeted by the project. 18. Activity 2.1. Technical Assistance to map the ICT availability in the country, and more specifically in the targeted rural areas – The ICT mapping technical assistance will focus on gathering data related to the deployment of digital services in Niger and presenting them in a friendly manner to policy makers. The mapping will also precisely pinpoint the villages of Niger, and more specifically the 2’100 villages targeted by the project. The technical assistance will focus on: a. infrastructure-mapping and service-mapping, with the current status of the digital infrastructure itself (i.e. mobile sites, power electricity availability, backhauling availability) and the service availability (i.e. 2G, 3G and 4G availability, bandwidth, retail prices); b. demand-mapping, with the demand typology and quantification for digital services (voice, SMS, data) to address end-users needs (e.g. rural smallholders and local public administrations); c. investment-mapping, with the existing and potentially planned investments in digital infrastructure by the private and public sector (e.g. fiber backbone extension, deployment of new mobile sites). 19. Activity 2.2. Technical assistance to elaborate an “Open Access PPP Manual” for use of project funds for supporting digital access infrastructure deployment in rural areas – The Government will enable open access wireless network rollout in rural villages targeted by the project with strong evidence of market failure by providing the awards of “least-cost” capital subsidies through a competitive “reverse auction” tender process to private operators. The technical assistance will guide the action of the Government by defining the short-list of key features of the PPP projects to be supported by project funds for the targeted areas. a. The key characteristics of the competitive “reverse auction” tender process to award “least-cost” capital subsidies to private operator. In this investment gap funding mode, the public authority contracts with a private sector partner who finances, designs, builds, owns and operates the wireless network access infrastructure on an open access basis. A capital subsidy is provided to the private sector operator through grants which are paid during deployment and/or through the operational life of the contract. b. Box 4: Towards an e-KYC (Know-Your-Customer) registry To facilitate the opening of accounts in financial institutions for financially excluded populations, this digital data platform will host an e-KYC (Know-Your-Customer) registry. The registry will help financial institutions to identify their customers and comply with Customer Due Diligence (CDD) regulation which constitute a key Page 62 of 89 The World Bank Niger: Smart Villages for Rural Growth and Digital Inclusion Project (P167543) component for compliance with AML/CFT requirements. Indeed, high costs incurred by complex and time- consuming KYC controls and diligences are often considered as main barriers to financial inclusion. These costs are likely to keep financial institutions from providing financial services to the disadvantaged population which may not be considered sufficiently profitable. Recognizing this critical issue, the G20 High Level Principles on Digital Financial Inclusion issued by the Global Partnership for Financial Inclusion (GPFI) in 2016 included the need to facilitate customer ID for digital financial services as a key pillar for financial inclusion under its principle 7. The e-KYC platform intends to help authorities of Niger to comply with this principle, by designing and providing an e-KYC registry which will facilitate the ID process of customers (whether permanent of occasional, and whether natural or legal person), the verification of customer’s identity, and the implementation of on-going customer monitoring. In addition, supervisors will also take benefit of the information stored in the e-KYC registry which may facilitate their controls (on documentary fraud or suspicious activity reports for instance). This registry could lead the supervisors to adjust their internal procedures in terms of controls and propose legal amendments to the AML/CFT law if necessary, contributing therein to the implementation of an effective AML/CFT risk-based supervisory approach. From a technical point of view, this e-KYC registry may take different forms and will have to be adapted to the Nigerien context after considering the main conclusions issued by the WBG Policy Note on e-KYC registries that is currently being finalized, as well as knowledge about all potential tools existing on the market. However, as potential characteristics (provided below only as examples and close to the Indian model), the e-KYC registry could enable new clients to submit directly on the digital platform a dully filled and signed KYC form (with supporting documents to verify proof of identity and proof of address and potentially linked to digital ID) before being allotted via SMS or email a single digit KYC ID number (KIN) which would need to be quoted by the client while conducting any financial transaction. For existing clients, financial institutions could be able to submit directly on the platform the required documents using bulk upload or file transfer mechanisms. The technical modalities of access of the e-KYC registry by the financial institutions will have to be determined by the technological solutions’ provider. The e-KYC registry will be applicable in the first place only to individuals (population of Smart Villages) but could be extended to the entire population of Niger in the medium term. The authorities will have to define the business model of the e-KYC registry, as to whether it should be financially sustainable (charging fees for financial institutions or/and administrative or judicial authorities to access the data) or encourage financial inclusion through the covering by the national budget of the maintenance costs. According to a new regulation that will have to be adopted by the Minister of Finance before the creation of the e-KYC registry, the digital data platform held by ANSI will receive, store, safeguard and retrieve KYC records in a digital form for every client of financial institutions. The bidders will propose the best technological solutions for the defined problems and goals, enabling 1) an easy access to the platform by financial institutions; 2) a high degree of flexibility to changes in functions, structure, integration with other systems; 3) use of standardized protocols and technologies; 4) an extensive capability for scaling; 5) a high level of performance in terms of data processing and analysis; and 6) a high level of data protection. Niger is ranked as one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change (180th out of 181 countries), according to the 2017 Notre Dame Global Adaptation Index41. Mean annual temperature in Niger increased by 0.6°–0.8°C between 1970 and 2010, slightly higher than the global average42. The country has been affected by several multi- year droughts over the last decades (1966-1967, 1973-1974, 1983-1984, 2004-2005) which led to failure of harvests and food insecurity43. An increase in frequency and severity of extreme rainfall events and flooding has also been observed44, which caused less widespread damage than droughts, but destroyed crops and livelihoods in certain parts of the country. 2. Natural disasters and climate-related shocks pose a large threat to Niger’s economic stability and resilience. More than 40 per cent of GDP comes from the agriculture and livestock sectors, and more than 90 per cent of the population relies on agricultural or pastoral activities for their livelihoods45. A strong correlation exists between changes in GDP and the meteorological conditions, demonstrating the vulnerability of the economy to weather- related shocks46. Climate change is expected to worsen over the next decades and could severely damage Niger’s prospects for future growth. Niger is likely to be exposed to recurring droughts and floods, which could decrease agricultural production and increase grazing pressure on pastoral ecosystems, consequently devastating whole communities and reversing hard-won development gains. 3. The Smart Villages Project could qualify for climate change mitigation co-benefits. Under Component 2 – Activity 2.5., it focuses on the use of renewable energy (solar power) for the cellular base stations and electricity generation for charging of mobile phones and other services in the smart villages. The use of renewable energy could be eligible to be counted towards mitigation finance, provided greenhouse gas (GHG) reductions can be quantified, for instance from reduction in travel time47. 4. In addition, the social benefits of the Smart Villages Project related to reduced poverty and increased incomes will support vulnerable Nigeriens who are at risk of chronic climatic shocks. The Project will help build the adaptive capacity of rural residents, particularly farmers, women and economically disadvantaged persons, by giving them access to services that could foster their resilience to shocks. Climate change adaptation is embedded in the design of the project, as exposure and vulnerability to the impacts of climate change is one of the criteria to select villages to be targeted as part of the intervention (see paragraph 43 above). A number of activities will specifically help mitigate the adverse effects of climate change: 5.
