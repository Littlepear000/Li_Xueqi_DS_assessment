STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. India’s economy may slow down, coming off a strong recovery in FY21/22 (April 2021-March 2022). The spillovers from global geopolitical tensions and the global monetary policy tightening cycle are expected to weigh on India’s economic outlook: elevated inflation on the back of higher prices of key commodities, heightened global uncertainty, and rising borrowing costs could affect domestic demand, while slowing global growth may dampen India’s export growth. Despite these challenges, India is expected to remain one of the fasted growing major economies in the world, due to robust domestic demand. The growth in FY22/23 is likely to moderate to 6.9 percent from 8.7 percent in FY21/221. Domestic demand is expected to remain on a recovery path, despite external headwinds. The government’s strong capex program will support investment, while private consumption will benefit from consumer spending in high- and middle-income groups. Net exports may continue to drag on growth due to slowing global growth and high commodity prices. The rising merchandise trade deficit is likely to push the current account deficit to 3.2 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) in FY22/23. Due to recovering demand, elevated global commodity prices and adverse weather conditions, headline inflation crept above the Reserve Bank of India’s tolerance range in January 2022. It remained above the target range for ten months before returning to below the upper end of the target range of 6 per cent in November 2022 and is expected to gradually ease further next year2. The government’s gradual fiscal consolidation efforts will be bolstered by strong revenue performance. Goods and Services Tax collections continue to be strong, having crossed the INR 1 trillion mark every month since July 2021, reaching as high as INR 1.67 trillion in April 2022. 2. Although India has made remarkable progress in reducing extreme poverty over the past two decades, the COVID-19 pandemic has slowed progress, and poses risks to welfare. Prior to the pandemic, the share of the population living below US$2.15 per person per day (2017 PPP) is estimated to have fallen from 22.5 in 2011 to 10 percent 2019.3 This was accompanied by a sharp decline in the incidence of multidimensional poverty, from 27.7 percent in 2005/06 to 16.4 percent in 2019/21.4 However, the pace of poverty reduction has slowed in recent years, with key welfare indicators being slow to improve.5 More than 40 percent of India’s population lived below the lower-middle income poverty line even before the pandemic.6 Inequality in consumption has remained stable, with a Gini index of around 35 over the past two decades. The State of Assam is one of the least economically developed parts of the country . Assam ranks 26th among 28 states in NITI Aayog's Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Index (2020-21),9 with 32 percent of its 31.2 million10 population living below the national poverty line. Per capita GDP (constant prices) grew by 5.26 percent in 2019-20, which was just over the national average of 5.1 percent. Eighty-five percent of Assam's population lives in rural areas, and 65 percent of them depend on agriculture as their primary source of income. A significant portion of this is subsistence farming with low cropping intensity due to limited irrigation availability, among other factors, which results in low productivity per unit area compared to the average across India. 4. Assam possesses several attributes that, under a supportive economic policy, could contribute significantly to its growth and raise standards of living. This includes a rich cultural heritage, vast natural resources (particularly in water and forests), high levels of biodiversity, significant opportunities for increasing inland water transport and high agricultural potential. Assam is strategically important as the largest and most populous State in the Northeast region. The Northeast is a gateway to Bangladesh and Southeast Asia and has the potential to transform into a commercial and tourism hub. The Brahmaputra River, which traverses through Assam on its way from China to Bangladesh, carries a third of India's freshwater runoffs and is one of the few ‘open basins’ in India where renewable water availability far exceeds current demands. The river is also an important transport artery and is designated as India’s National Waterway 2, while the smaller Barak River that also traverses Assam and parts of the Northeast constitutes National Waterway 6. The Brahmaputra River is a vital asset for Assam's people, and its abundant water resources could be productively used to promote economic development. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. Approximately 80 percent of the annual precipitation occurs within the four-month monsoon period, which is becoming longer due to climate change, causing significant flooding during the rainy season, and, because of limited capacity to store and manage water, shortages in the dry period. In spite of these challenges, Assam has great potential to tap into its abundant water resources to promote agricultural development and water-intensive industries. 6. Assam is among the Indian States hardest hit by erosion and flood hazards. Almost half of Assam's landmass is prone to floods. Between 1953 and 2020, an estimated 178 million people were impacted by floods in virtually all districts of the State. Over 50 million hectares in Assam were affected by floods during this same period, which represents over 10 percent of the total area affected by floods in all of India. Approximately 386,476 hectares, equivalent to approximately 7 percent of the State's landmass in 17 riverine districts, was lost due to river erosion between 1954 and 2019.12 The devastating 2022 floods affected 34 of Assam's 35 districts, claimed 201 lives, damaged 315,641 houses and 243,929 hectares of cropped area, forced 743,528 people into relief camps, and adversely impacted the lives and livelihoods of around 8.8 million people across 10,008 villages.13 A high percentage of Assam’s landmass suffers from moderate to extremely severe soil loss (i.e., greater than 10 tons/ha/year) due to water erosion, and over 65 percent of the Assam's landmass has moderate to extremely severe soil loss, well over the national average of 38 percent. Another almost 30 percent faces very severe to extremely severe soil loss (i.e., greater than 40 tons/ha/year) due to water erosion, significantly higher than the national average of 5 percent.14 7. Climate change has shifted the monsoon precipitation pattern and is expected to exacerbate current hazards and lead to more frequent floods and accelerated soil erosion. Climate modeling studies consistently project an increase in the frequency of extreme flooding events over the Indus-Ganga-Brahmaputra River Basins for the period 2020-2059 due to higher monsoon precipitation and accelerated glacial melting in the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau due to warmer temperatures. The Brahmaputra River basin, 36 percent of whose drainage area in India lies within Assam, is amongst the river basins with the highest projected increase in extreme flooding events.15 This increase is expected to accelerate the rate of soil erosion16 and sedimentation in Assam, contribute to further wetland degradation, and amplify adverse impacts on the State's agricultural sector. There are several factors that constrain Assam’s ability to mitigate flood and erosion risk and sustainably increase the productivity of water resources. The State has low capacity in integrated water resources management (IWRM)19 and disaster risk management (DRM). There is institutional fragmentation and limited coordination across the multiplicity of agencies working at the central, regional (northeast India), state, and local levels that are responsible for water management and disaster risk reduction. Top-down planning dominates, often with inadequate connection to the communities and people who are most directly affected. Institutional weaknesses are compounded by a dispersed and outdated knowledge base; there are serious gaps in data and information required for scientific analysis and informed decision-making on managing risks and making more productive use of water resources. Responses to routine flooding and rampant erosion have largely been piecemeal, reactive, and inflexible in dealing with the dynamic nature of the river systems and adapting to climate change. Primarily gray infrastructure solutions – which are not appropriate in all cases, are often expensive, and can actually increase risk20 – have been designed and executed with limited consideration of the natural variation and dynamics inherent in these complex river systems.21 Integrated investment planning is in a nascent stage and needs to be significantly strengthened. The Brahmaputra Board,22which has the mandate for the planning and integrated implementation of measures for the control of floods and bank erosion in the Brahmaputra Valley, has not been able to fully deliver on this mandate, including strategic planning and management of the basin. Its linkages with the basin states are tenuous, affecting uptake and “ownership” of the basin plans produced to date. In addition, gender equality, citizen engagement (CE) and ownership in water resources planning and flood and erosion management in the state is weak. 9. A progressive and systematic approach to IWRM and DRM is needed to address the key climate- related water risks and opportunities in Assam. A critical first step is the development of an enabling institutional framework and building the capacities of relevant agencies for IWRM and DRM. These include the Assam Water Resources Department (WRD), Assam State Disaster Management Authority (ASDMA), and Flood and River Erosion Management Agency of Assam (FREMAA). Examples of nature-based solutions (NBS), or ‘green’ infrastructure that harness natural systems and have been successfully employed in various parts of the world include watershed management, including forest management; river floodplain management; flood retention and flood bypass channels; aquifer and wetlands management; and soil management. NBS are particularly useful tools for adapting to a changing climate as they are generally robust to climate shocks and generate a host of co-benefits such as improving biodiversity and providing environmental amenities, and can often be used as multi-functional areas. An IWRM approach that integrates green infrastructure with gray infrastructure such as flood embankments, river erosion works, and dams offers the best approach for cost effective and resilient infrastructure. Non-structural solutions such as flood forecasting and early warning, emergency preparation, disaster response and recovery are also necessary to fully address all elements in the DRM cycle of mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery. 10. The institutional framework for DRM is generally sound while IWRM is in the incipient phase. The 2005 Indian Disaster Management Act required all Indian states to adopt a DRM system. ASDMA was created in 2007, and the Assam Disaster Management Rules and the Assam Disaster Management Policy were adopted in 2010. The second version of the Assam State Disaster Management Plan was issued in 2022.24 In contrast, the legal, regulatory, and planning framework for IWRM in Assam is still in an incipient phase. There is no overarching IWRM policy or law, and WRD focuses almost exclusively on floods and riverbank erosion. 11. Assam has recently upgraded its climate change plan. Strengthening women’s role in disaster risk management is central to the Program. Global studies have shown that disasters often have greater impacts on women and children due to their higher levels of exposure and vulnerability, and lower states of preparedness and coping capacity.27 In South Asia, generally, and in India, specifically, available data shows that women have been more severely affected than men in disasters over the last few decades.28 For example, the death toll of women was four times higher than men in recent tsunamis, floods and cyclones in India and Bangladesh.29 In Assam, women are underrepresented in village level disaster management committees with less than 40 percent female representation in most disaster management committees30 (for example shelter management). Further, leadership and decision-making positions in these village-level committees such as (i) shelter management, (ii) relief distribution, (ii) organization of mock drills, and (iv) water and sanitation management are mostly occupied by men. 14. Evidence suggests that existing public decision-making mechanisms and community bodies at the local level constituted to plan disaster response and prevention interventions have not been able to address the differentiated impacts of disasters on women, which in turn influences how disasters impact women.31 Disaster needs assessment (DNA) exercises conducted in India suggest that women’s participation in planning, implementing, and monitoring emergency programs and rehabilitation projects remains low.32 Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) and consultations suggest that planning and budgeting exercises in project areas do not reflect women’s needs with regard to safety, sanitation and hygiene, and reproductive health-related priorities in flood shelter management infrastructure. Women and girls are reluctant to use shelters because they feel insecure in the flood shelters where facilities are not designed to address women’s priorities. This includes private spaces where women can breastfeed, as well as WASH facilities where women have adequate privacy for hygiene (including menstrual hygiene)33. D. Multiphase Programmatic Approach (i) Rationale for Using MPA 20. A ten-year, overlapping three-phase Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA) will support Assam to reduce the vulnerability of people to climate-related disasters and improve IWRM. The MPA provides the following advantages over a standard project approach: • The GoI and the GoA have formally requested US$500 million in financing from the Bank for a long-term program to help Assam confront their complex challenges. An MPA signals the Bank’s support to a long- term partnership that will help meet Assam’s needs. A single project, with a shorter time span and smaller financing package, would not be responsive to the Borrower’s needs or expectations. • The MPA offers the opportunity to provide long-term continuous support for institutional development and capacity building of GoA agencies. It also allows time to build the coalitions of stakeholders, including local governments, the private sector, Non-Governmental Organizations, academia, and the public, that are crucial to mount a “whole-of-society” approach to reducing climate-related disaster risks and improving IWRM. The time span of a single project is too short to achieve sustainable progress on these fronts. • The MPA is designed as set of modular and scalable investments that are rolled out over time, whereby each phase prepares investments for the subsequent phase. This helps to accelerate disbursements and reduce uncommitted funds. An MPA provides confidence to the GoA that investment preparation 36 Neumayer, E., and T. Phase 1, which is the smallest phase, will pilot different approaches and then, based upon lessons learned, these activities will be adjusted and scaled up in subsequent phases. Focusing on the Water-DRM Nexus. As highlighted in the World Bank report (2021) entitled “An EPIC Response: Innovative Governance for Flood and Drought Risk Management,” water resources and disaster management agencies need to collaborate to provide a joined-up government response to managing hydro- climatic risks.43 Each agency has its specific mandate, but it must work with other agencies to holistically address complex flood management problems. The project design includes both WRD and ASDMA activities that are synergistically linked for maximum impact. Government leadership is important, but it must also bring along the rest of society, including local governments, civil society, academia, the private sectors, and the public. The project design incorporates extensive stakeholder engagement in planning and implementation at all levels, ranging from IWRM planning to enhancing village climate resilience. 72. Adopting an IWRM Approach. This is required to sustainably develop Assam’s abundant water resources and simultaneously reduce flood and river erosion risks. Traditionally, flood embankment and river erosion work undertaken by WRD focused on local problem areas without considering the river basin. The project design thus calls for the formulation of IFRMPs in the eight most critical tributary basins. The IFRMPs will consider the whole basin and integrate green (i.e nature-based) and gray infrastructure to identify the optimal mix of structural measures; the plans will also consider non-structural approaches such as land use zoning, flood proofing, and emergency preparedness and response. At a larger scale, the Assam Brahmaputra and Assam Barak strategic water plans will explore options for sustainably managing and developing water resources for economic development while protecting the environment and managing flood and river erosion risks. 73. Building Community Resilience. Community level interventions are fundamental to building climate resilience, and in Assam it is the rural poor who are disproportionally affected. The project includes activities to improve the resilience of village infrastructure through the VDMPs and enhance local emergency preparedness and response by strengthening CQRTs. The flood shelter activities are targeted to help support the most flood vulnerable rural populations. The Program will upgrade Assam’s flood forecasting and EWDS to ensure that necessary information is provided to rural households in a timely manner. 74. Transboundary Water Management. A core lesson from the SAWI program is that integrated river basin planning in a transboundary context is a very complicated process, and the technical aspects (modeling frameworks, analytical tools, data, etc.) represent only part of the challenge. Such exercises require intensive consultation with multiple stakeholders at various levels and across multiple sectors. Economic: An economic cost-benefit analysis (CBA) was undertaken for Component 2 river works and is included in the World Bank Project files. The CBA uses a standard “avoided cost” approach to estimate benefits. The primary benefits are the avoided costs of replacing road and rail infrastructure from river erosion, and the avoided costs of replacing structures and agricultural damages from flooding. A greenhouse gas (GHG) emission analysis concluded that there will be a slight increase in GHG emissions, approximately 27,000 tons, CO2eq, stemming from use of different types of construction material such as concrete and geobags. The Program will promote the use of nature-based solutions in the future which should help reduce GHG emissions from construction materials. The shadow price of carbon from these emissions was included in the CBA and had an insignificant impact on the analysis. The CBA estimated the economic internal rate of return (EIRR) to be around 15 percent under the base case scenario. Under the base case scenario, it is assumed that river erosion would undermine critical transportation infrastructure in Year 10. Sensitivity analysis was undertaken using a Year 5 scenario (18.9 percent EIRR) and a Year 15 (13 percent) scenario. Since river erosion is inherently unpredictable, and a changing climate is altering historical patterns, there are inherent limitations in this type of analysis. Moreover, the CBA is conservative as it does not account for costs that are difficult to quantify, such as increased costs due to transportation interruptions and the costs of social dislocation of people who lose their land. 81.
