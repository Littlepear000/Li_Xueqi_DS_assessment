STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Pakistan is at a crossroads as it deals with the coronavirus (COVID-19) pandemic. Over the last two decades, economic growth in Pakistan has averaged 4.4 percent a year, below the South Asian annual average of 6.3 percent. The country was making good progress in stabilizing its economy and implementing much needed structural reforms. However, real Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth (at factor cost) is estimated to have declined from 1.9 percent in FY19 to -1.5 percent in FY20, the first contraction in decades, reflecting monetary and fiscal tightening prior to the outbreak and the effects of COVID-19 containment measures that followed. 2. In response to COVID-19, the Government announced a fiscal stimulus package of US$7.5 1 billion, equivalent to 2.6 percent of gross domestic product (GDP). This aimed to: (i) support the health sector in combatting and mitigating the virus; (ii) implement social assistance measures for those adversely affected; and (iii) provide stimulus to businesses and industries to protect productive assets. The financing comprises approximately US$2.5 billion of additional resources and a re-appropriation from the existing budget. Pakistan has availed of the Debt Service Suspension Initiative (DSSI) and expects US$1.6 billion to US$2.4 billion in temporary fiscal space due to the debt service standstill. 3. Pakistan is facing severe health and economic consequences from the COVID-19 pandemic. The crisis is expected to lead to a sizeable increase in poverty, reversing the sustained reduction observed over the past 20 years. Deterioration of health indicators is expected due to demand-side issues induced by the crisis, such as lower utilization of non-COVID-19 healthcare due to fear of contagion and income constraints. The pandemic has also resulted in an increase in the number of out of school children. B. Sectoral (or Multi-Sectoral) and Institutional Context2 4. Human capital challenges such as high stunting rates and large gender gaps in schooling persist, particularly for low-income households. Poverty is a strong correlate of stunting, and more than a third (37.6 percent) of children under five are stunted. Poor nutritional status is also observed among pregnant women and mothers, which affects birth weights and increases child morbidity. Climatic shocks are expected to increase risks of malnutrition through crop failures, inundated or unsanitary environments, contaminated water sources, and premature infant weaning. In addition, there are around 11.5 million out of school primary level children.3 There is a large gender differential in enrolment rates, which accentuates with age. These issues stem in part from underutilization of basic services due to demand side constraints. 5. Pakistan is highly vulnerable to the impacts of climatic shocks,4 with extreme events frequently disrupting human and physical capital formation. Climatic shocks have caused significant loss of life, economic damage, and reversal of development gains over the last 15 years. Prior to COVID-19, Pakistan had spent over a decade making substantial investments in its Social Protection (SP) system, which consists of several flagship programs. These include: Kafaalat, a cash transfer (CT) program managed by BISP covering 4.6 million regular beneficiaries; Waseela-e-Taleem (WeT), a conditional cash transfer (CCT) linked to primary school attendance in 50 districts; and a new nutrition focused CCT linked to maternal and child health. These are administered at the federal level and are supported by delivery systems including the National Socio-Economic Registry (NSER), covering 85 percent of the population, and a biometrically authenticated digital payment system linked to the national identity system managed by the National Database and Registration Authority (NADRA). 7. In March 2019, the Prime Minister launched the National Poverty Alleviation Program, Ehsaas, that consolidates and expands SP coverage at both federal and provincial level. Ehsaas also created: (i) the Poverty Alleviation and Social Safety (PASS) Division to consolidate federal SP initiatives, bringing institutions such as BISP and the Pakistan Poverty Alleviation Fund (PPAF) under a single umbrella; and (ii) a Labor Expert Group (LEG), tasked with developing a strategy for tackling issues around informality. LEG’s recommendations included extending SP coverage to informal workers. 8. The Government has demonstrated its commitment to financial inclusion through the Ehsaas Financial Inclusion Strategy, focusing on poor women and informal workers. It explicitly links to Kafaalat via the One Woman One Account Policy, which aims to provide each Kafaalat beneficiary with a savings account into which unconditional cash transfers (UCT) are made. The strategy also emphasizes use of digital approaches to promote financial inclusion. 9. Pakistan was able to expand safety nets vertically and horizontally to respond rapidly to COVID- 19. In March 2020, the Government provided temporary income support to 15 million beneficiaries via Ehsaas. BISP continued to support families with children enrolled in WeT during the seven months that education institutions remained closed. These transfers were intended to safeguard against income losses and prevent households from resorting to negative coping strategies such as decreasing investments in human capital or productive assets. 10. COVID-19 highlighted the need for more effective crisis response and to build household resilience to future shocks. This is particularly important as climate and other shocks are impacting vulnerable population groups with increasing frequency and intensity. Adaptive SP systems can significantly reduce the lead time for response, which has direct implications on the pace of recovery. Lessons include: (i) The social registry and flagship safety nets must evolve away from static processes to respond to the dynamic nature of poverty and vulnerability. As a result, programs that rely on NSER for eligibility decisions lack regular entry–exit processes, which limits Government’s ability to use NSER to respond to shocks with effective targeting. (ii) The vulnerability of the missing middle needs to be addressed. Due to COVID-19 millions of people have lost their jobs and have no access to insurance or savings mechanisms. The “missing middle”, fall outside of existing risk sharing and risk mitigation mechanisms, vastly exceeds SP coverage. In 2017, the informal sector accounted for 72 percent of non-agricultural workers and 81.6 percent of private sector employment, comprised of own account workers who are micro-enterprise employees or employers. Most are not eligible for existing safety net programs targeted to the very poor, nor covered by social insurance, including formal pensions, which have a very low coverage of 2.3 percent.6 Climate- related disasters will likely create similar challenges. By integrating the principles of the Adaptive Social Protection System, swift support can be provided to vulnerable groups. (iii) Broader coverage of demand-side interventions is required to address lagging human capital accumulation and facilitate better SP interventions. COVID-19 has exacerbated the persistent lag in schooling and health and nutrition (H&N) from both the supply and demand side,7 which could exacerbate generational poverty cycles. The Government seeks to tackle SP issues through various human capital angles, including linking SP interventions with health, education, and financial inclusion services. Effective implementation requires national, federal and provincial, coordination. 11. The Government is currently revising the Ehsaas strategy to make it more specific to the post- COVID-19 scenario, which will require interventions addressing multiple vulnerabilities . Under PASS Division’s leadership and the evolving Ehsaas strategy, Pakistan can begin to address the challenges and gaps highlighted by the crisis by (i) expanding the coverage and types of SP interventions to help build household resilience, (ii) transforming delivery systems to make them truly adaptive and responsive to crises, and (iii) stimulating household demand for basic education and health related services to protect human capital from depletion. The Government’s Vision 2025, focusing on human development and inclusive growth, is informed by four pillars of the Ehsaas Strategy. Pillar 1 aims to build an enabling environment by creating fiscal space and improving governance. Pillar 2 focuses on enhancing safety net support, including vertical and horizontal expansion of cash-based income support programs, improvement in delivery mechanisms, and implementation of demand side interventions. Pillar 3 focuses on human capital accumulation among the next generation. Pillar 4 covers jobs and livelihoods. 15. The Government has recently made substantial investments in safety nets and is ramping up support despite fiscal constraints. The Government has invested US$3 billion at the federal level since 2017 and plans to spend US$6.5 billion over the next five years under Ehsaas. Most of this (99.8 percent) will be spent through BISP, with the remaining 0.20 percent to be spent through other SP interventions: PPAF, Pakistan Bait-ul-Mal, and PASS Division itself.8 The Government also intends to increase the total number of BISP beneficiaries from 4.6 million to 7 million families in 2022. The increased spending will improve the adequacy of the income support portion of the CTs and restructure the conditional benefits to better align them with differential constraints to accessing human capital services. 16. The Government’s investments have had significant impacts, which are expected to be enhanced by this program. The positive impacts have been demonstrated by multiple impact evaluations of the BISP UCT and CCT interventions.9 B. Theory of Change 17. The program seeks to make Pakistan’s SP system more responsive and increase crisis resilience among poor and vulnerable households. Specifically, the program would: (i) Build-in greater innovation and technology within SP systems to improve the social registry’s capacity to maintain updated household data, exchange data with social programs, optimize use of cross- checks, improve data and analytics for targeting, and provide greater beneficiary choice in the biometric payment systems. (ii) Begin the expansion of SP coverage to the missing middle through an innovative hybrid SP savings mechanism. C. PforR Program Scope 19. The PforR is focused on the key initiatives being undertaken by BISP under the Ehaas CCT programs, namely Kafaalat (the base CT program providing income support), WeT (the education linked CCT), and Nashonuma (the CCT linked to nutrition and health). The two CCTs are directly under the ambit of the PforR by virtue of its support through RA3, whereas Kafaalat is included due to its dependence and strong linkages with the delivery systems being improved under RA1. The financial inclusion and informal worker support initiatives of Ehsaas have also been included as part of the PforR as RA2. 20. The program will span FY2021–25 with a nationwide spread of activities. No procurement activities would be required under the PforR in accordance with the Bank’s Policy and Directive on PforR Financing. The program is structured around three main RAs derived from current Government efforts in the SP sector: RA1 Building Crisis-Resilient Delivery Systems; RA2 Developing Innovative Hybrid Social Protection Scheme; and RA3 Protecting human capital in the early and primary school years. Results Area 2: Developing Innovative Hybrid Social Protection Scheme (US$95 million) 25. This RA will support a hybrid scheme that blends social assistance with social risk mitigation elements to promote savings that informal sector workers can fall back on in case of shocks, while also providing a platform through which the government can more rapidly deploy additional support during a crisis. This will expand SP coverage through a hybrid SP scheme supporting Government’s risk mitigation and financial inclusion among informal workers. COVID-19 has highlighted widespread socioeconomic vulnerability, and climate change is expected to create similar systemic shocks. Climate factors may also indirectly increase idiosyncratic shocks from illnesses.11 Swift provision of ex-post liquidity can significantly enhance coping capacity and expedite recovery for vulnerable populations. 26. The basic model, drawing from global experiences, will be a contributory savings scheme with matching incentives, with a short-to-medium term horizon for withdrawals. Individuals would not be penalized for early withdrawal but will not have access to contributions before reaching the minimum time commitment. This model is appropriate given existing liquidity and savings constraints among informal workers and the nature and frequency of shocks. The hybrid SP scheme will allow exiting BISP beneficiaries to receive support by way of fiscal incentives to save in a platform created to meet their needs. Eligibility for the scheme will be limited to current and exiting BISP beneficiaries in socioeconomic bands I and II of the updated NSER.12 Current BISP beneficiaries would be able to start saving a small proportion with Government incentives, thereby building their financial resilience, which would help them graduate.13 It is anticipated that only women will be initially eligible, but the scheme could be progressively extended to other vulnerable workers. 28. Some of the essential roles and responsibilities can be shared with financial institutions, while others would require capacity building within BISP or other institutions. The four key functions required to administer the scheme are: (i) setting up contribution collection channels; (ii) central record keeping to track contributions and withdrawals of individuals; (iii) funds flow management function; and (iv) investment of savings. The administration of this scheme needs to be efficient because the target group will have limited savings and the lower the operating costs the higher the returns for participants. 29. The scheme will rely on existing institutional capacity and digital approaches for efficient delivery and promoting behavioral change. It is expected that BISP’s partnered institutions will develop and manage a specialized savings product that enables matching, incentives, and time commitments. Although similar products are being offered in the market, they are without Government contribution and are not tailored. Initial costs to BISP are expected to be low. BISP will also develop a digital platform for tracking the hybrid scheme, which would include selection and enrollment status of beneficiaries, and information received from financial institutions on contributions, withdrawals, and shared profits of individual accounts. This RA would also support operationalizing of the beneficiary registry (digital platform) for the hybrid scheme linked to NSER. SUMMARY FIDUCIARY SYSTEMS ASSESSMENT 1. BISP follows the accounting system prescribed by the Federal Government and Financial Management and Procurement systems are found to adequate and reasonably advanced, based on ongoing experience under NSPP. BISP has taken significant steps to adopt International Public Sector Accounting Standards accrual basis of accounting. Some of the areas for further improvement include: development and institutionalization of Reporting templates; more precise capturing of the SP expenditure in the national accounting system; improved inhouse capacity for information system audits; development of an interface with the banks to detect and rectify improper payments due to fraud or errors must be implemented. Monitoring needs to be further strengthened to prevent, detect, and rectify improprieties. Although BISP is subjected to external audit by the Auditor General of Pakistan, reports need to be made public on BISP’s website. The follow-up with external audit recommendations needs to be streamlined. Delays in awarding contracts due to high staff turnover rates will be addressed by ensuring institutionalization of the processes. Perceptions of delayed payments which may lead to limited participation of bidders in procurement opportunities will be addressed by delegating financial powers for more efficient contract payment. 2. Past experience with procurement under NSPP has been positive. Sufficient capacity to manage procurement has been built. Contract management could be improved but BISP has made considerable improvements through internal strengthening. The Procurement Manual has been periodically revised. BISP also maintains an online complaint management system for procurements. 3. Pakistan has a legal framework and agencies to deal with abuse of authority and is also a signatory to the UN Convention against Corruption, and is committed to the Ehsaas Governance and Integrity Policy, BISP’s Whistleblowing Policy, Conflict of Interest Policy, and WB’s Anti Corruption Guidelines. Page 45 of 50 The World Bank Pakistan Crisis-Resilient Social Protection (CRISP) (P174484) ANNEX 5. SUMMARY ENVIRONMENTAL AND SOCIAL SYSTEMS ASSESSMENT 1. Under RA1, associated environmental risks were assessed to be Low. These risks are attributed to the small amount of e-waste generated by ICT. Activities under RA2 will improve SP systems, with higher coverage across several programs. No adverse environmental impacts are envisaged. Social risks relate to targeting and identification of vulnerable groups. There is the potential of both exclusion and inclusion errors in the development of new assistance programs. Institutional arrangements between service providers should be clarified and streamlined. ESSA determined activities under RA3 would improve environmental and public health. The key social effects relate to the needs and attitudes towards CCT schemes, and to issues of inclusion and vulnerability, particularly for women. 2. Institutional assessment to ascertain the adequate implementation of E&S concerns was conducted. There is need to develop and implement Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) for the sale and procurement of ICT equipment under the function of “Framing of Financial Rules for Guidance of Departments” along with program specific ICT SOPs for handling e-waste based on current WB Environment, Health and Safety Guidelines. 3. ESSA identified institutional and capacity gaps that will be addressed through the Program Action Plan. Three recommendations were put forth: (i) BISP and partner organizations should prepare SOPs for environmental management of e-waste; (ii) BISP should share the SOP with Provincial EPAs for securing EPA’s consent, and its expanded use by EPAs in future throughout; and (iii) program teams in BISP and NADRA and EPA staff should be made aware of SOPs by organizing e-waste management training.
