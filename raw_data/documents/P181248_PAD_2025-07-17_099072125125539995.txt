STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. The project will focus on improving basic services and economic opportunities for the Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) and host community members in Northern Nigeria which are estimated to be over 3.5 million—one of the largest and fastest-growing displaced populations globally.1 Insecurity, driven by conflict, violence, and climate- induced disasters, has fueled large waves of internal displacement across the North-East (NE), North-West (NW), and North-Central (NC) regions.2 Nigeria, Africa’s second largest economy, also has the world's second-largest population living in extreme poverty. In 2024, approximately 46 percent of Nigerians lived below the international poverty line of US$2.15 per day (2017 PPP).3 The poverty rate in Northern Nigeria (covering the NE, NW, and NC regions) is estimated to be even higher at 73 percent (2024).4 Establishing direct causal links between conflict, displacement, and poverty is complex, but there are areas in Nigeria where these factors clearly intersect, especially in Sokoto in the NW and Taraba in the NE, two states affected by displacement that show the highest levels of poverty in the country (over 90 percent).5 Due to Nigeria’s size and role in Africa, addressing development challenges in Northern Nigeria supports the World Bank’s mission to reduce poverty. The project is consistent with Nigeria's broader development objectives by encouraging inclusive growth and stability through a strategic framework aimed at providing sustainable solutions for IDPs and host communities. 2. The Solutions for the Internally Displaced and Host Communities Project (SOLID) aims to improve access to basic services and economic opportunities for IDPs and host communities in target Local Government Areas (LGAs) in Northern Nigeria. The project will contribute to these goals by making strategic investments in the physical infrastructure of host communities to reduce infrastructure and basic service gaps that were already strained before the influx of IDPs. Complementing infrastructure improvements, the project will also make investments to increase economic activity and inclusion of IDPs in economic opportunities. This operation is crucial for addressing the protracted displacement crisis in the region, where communities have been severely impacted by insecurity, rapid population growth, unplanned physical development, and limited economic opportunities. The SOLID project shifts from a short-term emergency response approach to displacement focused on humanitarian assistance, to a long-term development approach focused on system changes (physical infrastructure and governance) and local capacity building to foster resilient and inclusive development and to improve livelihoods in displacement-affected communities. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Northern Nigeria faces four significant development challenges that hinder durable solutions for IDPs and host communities; the first challenge, which is a major driver of the other three challenges, is spatial. IDP inflows have reshaped state capital cities, such as Maiduguri (Borno), Yola (Adamawa), and Makurdi (Benue) and other towns (centers of other LGAs within the states), contributing to urban sprawl,6 poor access to services, and increased vulnerability to natural disasters. The second challenge is physical—IDP inflows have added pressure on already strained infrastructure in host communities. For example, in Maiduguri and other Borno LGAs,7 residents lack access to basic services such as water and Solid Waste Management (SWM). In Adamawa, insufficient water supply systems have resulted in IDPs having to travel 10 minutes or more to access drinking water, relying mainly on handpumps.8 Spatial mapping reveals limited access to toilets and garbage disposal at the LGA level in Adamawa. Additionally, field visits have pointed to a general lack of communal spaces and facilities for collective social activities and gatherings within host communities, which are essential for supporting community building and stabilization efforts. Under future climate change scenarios, highly variable and erratic but incrementally decreasing precipitation and increasing temperatures are predicted within Northern Nigeria. Climate-induced droughts combined with limited water availability or scarcity is another challenge faced by IDPs and host communities which is driving the need for increased water supply capacity and a transition to climate- resilient water management systems. 5. The third challenge is weak institutional capacity to deliver services in host communities. LGAs face overlapping governance structures that complicate service delivery and planning.10 Local Governments (LGs) lack financial and staffing autonomy, limiting their ability to implement long-term development strategies.11 Decision-making is highly centralized at the state level, and local development plans are outdated,12 with insufficient representation from local communities and IDPs in planning processes.13 This lack of inclusion weakens governance effectiveness to deliver services that address the specific needs of each community. 6. II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Development Objective 9. The Project Development Objective (PDO) is to improve access to basic services and economic opportunities for Internally Displaced Persons (IDPs) and host communities in target Local Government Areas (LGAs) in Northern Nigeria. B. Theory of Change and PDO Indicators 10. The Theory of Change for the project (Figure 1) is based on addressing the development challenges of displacement-affected states, LGAs, and communities in Northern Nigeria through (i) investing in climate-resilient infrastructure and strengthening the capacity of governments and communities to improve service delivery; and (ii) providing livelihood support and community integration for IDPs and local residents. The project adopts a three-pronged approach combining a whole-of-government strategy, people-in-place methods, and community engagement for tailored solutions. This approach ensures coordination between national, state, and local governments to implement solutions for IDPs and host communities. It integrates the needs of displaced people and the impacts on the place where they settle while fostering active and inclusive community participation, including vulnerable groups such as women and youth. The project addresses climate vulnerabilities by reducing climate risks, such as drainage and waste issues, and building adaptive capacities through climate-resilient infrastructure and adaptive livelihoods. It also integrates gender dimensions and citizen engagement, as discussed in the next sections. 11. III. PROJECT IMPLEMENTATION A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 30. At the national level, the Federal Ministry of Budget and Economic Planning (FMBEP) will establish an FPMU for overall project implementation, coordination, fiduciary responsibilities (FM and procurement), M&E, and E&S management. The FPMU will be headed by a National Project Coordinator supported by technical experts on need basis from relevant Federal Agencies such as NCFRMI and Regional Institutions, with roles and responsibilities further detailed in the PIM.50 The FPMU will oversee a single Designated (Special) Account for project funds and ensure the required FM infrastructure is in place. The project has been prepared under a framework approach. Specific investments and TA support interventions will be determined by fulfillment of eligibility criteria and results of technical needs assessments. Screening of investments subprojects will be based on criteria to exclude those that entail significant long term and irreversible E&S impacts. For each investment/subproject, the selection criteria will include alignment with the PDO, enhancement of the services provided, implementation readiness, absence of major or irreversible E&S impacts, and economic viability. The criteria will identify subprojects with (i) potential demonstration effects of livability and livelihood improvements; (ii) complementarity with ongoing or future interventions in selected LGAs and wards; and (iii) community and beneficiary support. Subprojects under the framework should be feasible and implementable within the project time frame. They must meet international technical standards and best practices. The PIM will stipulate, as part of the investment selection criteria, that any investment proposed under the SOLID project must not be encompassed by another project. (a) The project will make notable contributions to high-priority areas58 that support the stabilization, recovery, and durable solutions initiatives of their respective states and will target (i) employment generation and economic recovery and (ii) preparedness for natural disasters by upgrading selected facilities and strengthening the climate resilience of infrastructure. Accordingly, the SOLID Project will partially address the complex challenges at the intersection of disasters in FCV settings, recognizing that the displacement situation in Northern Nigeria is shifting as a result of climatic changes. The project factors in the evolving situation and planned infrastructure investments will be designed to be climate resilient through proper planning, appropriate engineering standards, and consideration of increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events. Technical design studies will be prepared for prioritized investments under the project as well as for future potential investments. Aside from investments in climate-resilient infrastructure, which is more crucial than ever considering the recent flood that affected more than 500,000 people in Maiduguri alone, the project will provide strategic guidance and plans to inform future development to enhance climate resilience in the FCV environment of Northern Nigeria. (b) The project will promote the use of NbS to provide flood protection, mitigate urban heat, improve community well-being, and capture emissions. All infrastructure investments will undergo a climate risk assessment59 to ensure that proposed investments are resilient to climate hazards. The assessment will consider the location of each proposed infrastructure investment with respect to flood and heat exposure to inform its design and ensure resilience to climate change impacts. Rehabilitation of roads to climate proof standards will further enhance the adaptive capacity of the road network to withstand climate risks such as flooding. Economic Analysis 41. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected by a project supported by the World Bank may submit complaints to existing project-level grievance mechanisms or the World Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the World Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). Complaints may be submitted to the AM at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the attention of World Bank Management and after Management has been given an opportunity to respond. Nigeria is classified as one of the 10 most vulnerable countries to the impacts of climate change and natural hazards81 and is one of the least ready to adapt to or cope with climate change. In the 2022 ND-Gain Index, 82 which ranked 187 countries with respect to their vulnerability to climate change, Nigeria ranked high (126 out of the 187 countries). The ND-Gain Index ranked Nigeria very low with respect to readiness to improve resilience (180). The country is highly exposed to natural hazards like pluvial, fluvial, and coastal floods; storms; water scarcity; extreme heat; and wildfires and has medium-level exposure to landslides. In addition, epidemics are relatively frequent and disease outbreaks (for example, cholera) are often linked to flood events. The northern areas are facing the double shock of chronic aridity (or severe droughts and heat) and recurrent, often widespread riverine flooding.83 Nigeria is dealing with increased frequency and severity of climate-related disasters and current temperature and precipitation trends have contributed to worsening food insecurity and negatively affecting livelihoods and the broader economy, particularly the agriculture, water, and infrastructure sectors.84 Temperatures in Northern Nigeria have increased in the past decades, reaching up to 45°C, and climate models85 predict more rapid temperature increases and longer extreme heat periods in the North. While precipitation trends have been highly variable, overall rainfall has decreased incrementally across the country since the 1960s.86 Despite unclear overall precipitation trends under future climate scenarios, rainfall is expected to be highly variable and erratic, with an increase in the frequency of extreme events and intensification of heavy rainfall.87 This may lead to increased flooding but also negatively affect water availability, and thus agriculture and food security in the North. Land degradation, erosion, and desertification are also expected to intensify with climate change. As agriculture is the main source of income for most people in the North, particularly low-income households, the vulnerability of Northern Nigerians will increase with climate change. According to the Inform Risk Index,88 Nigeria’s high- risk rating is attributed to significant vulnerability driven by high inequalities, underdevelopment of certain regions including the North, large numbers of unprotected people, and lack of coping capacity—including lack of governance and lack of physical infrastructures. 2. Northern Nigeria is particularly exposed to climate and disaster risks. Nigeria has three climate zones: (i) a tropical wet climate in the south, with heavy rainfall events occurring during the rainy season from March to October; (ii) a tropical savannah climate for most of the central regions, with the rainy season from April to September and the dry season from December to March; and (iii) a Sahelian hot and semi-arid climate in the north of the country, with heavy rainfall events from June to September and a dry season for the rest of the year. With changing and more erratic rainfall patterns in northern Nigeria, due to climate change, water and food security is deteriorating, which adds to IDPs’ and host communities’ vulnerabilities and is creating tension and conflict. Thus, the project will target drought-prone wards and water-stressed communities (identified through a climate vulnerability assessment) and help them adapt to these circumstances and alleviate pressure on water availability and food security by securing and improving access to climate-resilient water systems (for drinking and irrigation) at the community and ward level. The location and design of water supply infrastructure will be assessed to ensure it is adequate, safe from climate hazards (for example, flooding, heat for increased evapotranspiration), and sustainable (including no environmental risk or harm, for example, to groundwater/surface water). Boreholes and pumps will be powered by solar energy exclusively. This will avoid the use of fossil energy and thus emissions. • Development and upgrading of public spaces such as parks, riverbank enhancements, community parks and sports fields (US$15 million). IDPs often end up settling in dense urban areas that are vulnerable to climate risk such as flooding, extreme heat, and drought. To reduce the risk of flooding and heat impacts, the project will develop and/or upgrade green spaces in targeted wards and communities (identified through the climate vulnerability assessment). This intervention will mainly include NBS measures (such as tree planting, extension of vegetation/grass and permeable grounds for better infiltration, natural drainage, and so on) to address the UHI effect and improve infiltration of run-off water to reduce flood risk. • Rehabilitation and development of public facilities such as community centers, public buildings, and sanitary facilities (US$10 million). Retrofitting will include improved climate adaptation through climate-resilience standards in structural measures (for example, building material use and construction foundations, and so on, that make the building more resilient to flood and heat impacts), improving the drainage of the facility and location to reduce flood risk, design, and operational interventions that improve cooling and ventilation as well as energy efficiency to reduce heat impacts to the facility and its users. As highlighted above, the identification of target wards and communities for interventions includes a climate-vulnerability criterion which will ensure that this activity supports the reduction of climate risk respectively adaptation of beneficiary communities.
