IBRD/IDA v The World Bank Maldives Emergency Support for Critical Services Project (P516182) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Given its heavy dependence on tourism and imported energy, the Maldives has been severely hit by the economic spillovers from the conflict in the Middle East. Since February 28, 2026, global oil prices surged by more than 32 percent while tourist arrivals to the Maldives have declined by 21.6 percent (y-o-y). Although the country has made progress in expanding renewable energy in recent years – particularly solar – the energy system remains overwhelmingly reliant on imported fuels, with fuel imports averaging about US$60 million per month in 2025. Imported fuel is critical for electricity generation and underpins essential services, including desalinated water supply (which accounts for 100 percent of potable water), hospital operations, and domestic transportation. At the same time, tourism— the main source of foreign exchange (FX) liquidity, and government revenue— has been adversely affected. Around 35 percent of visitors — especially from European markets— transit through the Middle East and disruptions at major aviation hubs have already curtailed arrivals. 2. The shock is compounding Maldives’ pre-existing macroeconomic vulnerabilities, which constrain the country’s ability to respond to such a significant external shock. Widening fiscal and external imbalances, driven by high public spending, an expanded role of state-owned enterprises, and increased reliance on non-concessional external borrowing, have led to a high risk of debt distress and FX liquidity constraints in the private sector. Debt to Gross Domestic Product (GDP) remains elevated at 133.1 percent of GDP in 2025. Usable FX reserves are estimated at US$327.9 million1 as of March 2026 and debt repayment needs are elevated in 2026 with total debt service of US$1.7 billion2 including US$525 million for the servicing of Islamic Bond (Sukuk) during the year. 3. The nature of the shock and the country’s dependence on imported fuel will pose challenges to the provision of critical public services. Continued access to imported fuel is critical to maintain electricity generation, desalination and fresh water supply, and basic transportation services across the islands. Fuel imports are also essential to ensure the availability of medicines and other essential goods such as food and bottled water at reasonable prices, especially for poorer households in remote outer islands. 4. The government has taken steps to respond to the shock, but a deeper adjustment is needed to address vulnerabilities. To date, the Government of Maldives (GoM) has focused on preventive stop-gap measures, including actions to secure fuel shipments—primarily from Oman— and maintain available stock buffers. The authorities have also increased petrol, diesel and jet fuel prices, by 19 percent, 26 percent and 42 percent respectively, in early March; and announced a temporary rationing of cooking gas. Nevertheless, given pre-existing vulnerabilities and the significant impact of the conflict on the external balance, a deep fiscal and external adjustment is urgently needed to reduce the risk of shock translating into an economic crisis. 5. This proposed Project is designed as a time-bound emergency liquidity support that finances critical fuel imports. It does not constitute a long-term investment in the energy sector; nor is it intended to cover Maldives’ full fuel import requirements on a sustained basis. While this operation focuses on addressing the near-term shock, the WB has been supporting Maldives’ energy transition to reduce dependence on fuel imports and providing policy advice to address macro-fiscal vulnerabilities. Through a series of projects, the Bank has been a central partner in advocating and financing the transition to solar energy as a replacement for more expensive imported fossil fuels. The WB will continue close policy dialogue and targeted technical assistance to support the transition, including in areas of fuel pricing mechanisms, demand‑side management, energy efficiency, and risk‑management tools. This will help ensure that the short‑term crisis response remains aligned with Maldives’ medium‑term energy transition and fiscal consolidation objectives. Addressing broader macro-fiscal vulnerabilities requires structural and fiscal reforms, notably replacing untargeted subsidies with targeted social assistance, rationalizing public capital expenditures, reforming state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and reforming healthcare financing, as identified in the Maldives Public Expenditure Review (2022). The Bank has been involved in continuous policy dialogue and provided technical assistance in each of these reform areas. This has been done in coordination with other development partners through the Multilateral Development Bank (MDB+) platform and through the Maldives Competitiveness and Growth Project (MCGP, P179286) currently under implementation. The WB is well placed to provide necessary analytical, technical, and implementation support for such reforms. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. Though Maldives has increased the share of its energy from renewable sources in recent years, particularly solar, electricity supply still relies almost entirely on imported diesel fuel. Two state‑owned utilities serve most inhabited islands. The dispersed geography of the archipelago creates significant logistical complexity and cost, making the continuity of fuel supply critical for electricity generation, water desalination, health services, and inter‑island transport. The cost of electricity production varies significantly across islands, with generation costs for the most efficient generators estimated at between US$0.19 and US$0.33 per kWh, rising to as high as US$0.69 per kWh for more remote outer islands. The WB has been supporting efforts to increase the share of renewable energy through several projects and is working towards scaling up support in this area in order to reduce the risks associated with fossil fuel dependence. 9. The State Trading Organization (STO), an entity where the Maldives government is the majority shareholder, holds exclusive mandates for the import and distribution of petroleum products for energy generation.4 This is through its subsidiary for power generation and direct retail operations. STO also operates distribution networks across all 20 administrative atolls, serving as the critical supply chain backbone for an archipelago where private sector import logistics are limited by small island scale and high per-unit distribution costs. 3 Due to exchange rate fluctuations during the process of cancellation and processing of operation, a small amount of approximately US$200,000 from IDA PBA would complement the cancellations. 4 STO’s annual reports highlight key features including ownership and its mandate available at STO’s website. The crisis-related shock is already beginning to challenge the GoM’s ability to import fuel through STO. Fuel imports are financed through a combination of (i) revolving commercial credit lines provided by international commodity traders and petroleum suppliers; and (ii) a revolving trade finance facility from an investment vehicle associated with a Multilateral Development Bank. Financed fuel invoices from suppliers and the facility are settled through the Bank of Maldives and other domestic banks, supported by FX allocations from the Maldives Monetary Authority (MMA). Under the current supply agreement, STO is granted a supplier credit period within an agreed credit limit and tenor. While MMA currently provides FX allocations to other essential import orders including food and medicine as well, the allocation from this project will be important in supporting MMA in ensuring uninterrupted supply and alleviating pressure on the timely settlement of supplier obligations during periods of fuel price surges. 11. In this context, ensuring the continued availability of fuel for essential utilities and logistics represents the most urgent priority for maintaining basic service delivery and economic functioning in Maldives during the current crisis. The proposed operation therefore focuses on financing critical fuel imports. While the Project financing will be disbursed against fuel imports, the development impact extends beyond the commodity itself, as maintaining energy availability is fundamental to sustaining essential services and economic activity. II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Development Objective 12. The Project Development Objective (PDO) is to support the continuity of essential services and logistics by maintaining critical fuel supplies in response to external shocks and declining tourism revenues. B. Theory of Change and PDO Indicator 13. The Project addresses an acute external shock that constrains the availability of FX needed to finance critical fuel imports to ensure continuity of essential services and logistics in Maldives (Figure 1). The Project’s theory of change is simple: by providing time-bound emergency financing to reimburse fuel imports, the project alleviates FX constraints, enabling continued fuel supply for essential utilities and logistics, thereby preventing disruptions to electricity, water, transport and healthcare services and safeguarding household welfare and economic activity during the shock. The Critical Assumptions for the Theory of Change are: • Potential disruptions to essential utility operations, logistics and transport services by decline in tourism, FX constraints, and higher fuel prices. • The GoM continues to prioritize the procurement and distribution of fuel required for essential services, including electricity generation, water desalination, and inter-island transport. • The STO, together with utilities, maintains operational capacity to import, transport, and distribute fuel across the archipelago. • Domestic financial institutions, including the MMA and commercial banks, continue to facilitate the trade finance and FX arrangements required for fuel imports. • Maritime fuel supply chains and inter-island distribution logistics remain operational, enabling timely delivery of imported fuel to island power systems and other critical users. 15. The Project provides time-bound emergency financing to safeguard the continuity of essential public services and is expected to benefit approximately 500,000 Maldivians, including households, public service providers, and firms across the Maldives that rely on uninterrupted electricity, water, and transport services. By supporting the critical continued availability of fuel for essential utilities and logistics, the Project helps prevent service disruptions that would disproportionately affect residents of outer islands and vulnerable populations during the period of external shock. D. Project Component 17. The operation provides financing to the GoM to ensure the continued availability of petroleum products required for electricity generation, water desalination, and inter-island transport, which are critical for the functioning of the Maldivian economy and basic service delivery across the archipelago. The Project comprises one component. 18. Component: Critical Goods Imports (Fuel) (IDA US$40,000,000). This component makes use of recommitted IDA funds to finance the importation of eligible petroleum products required for the continued operation of essential utilities and logistics services in Maldives. It provides time-bound emergency financing to reimburse eligible fuel import payments. Disbursements will be made against verified documentation of fuel import payments, ensuring rapid implementation while maintaining appropriate fiduciary control. While this component addresses immediate liquidity constraints affecting fuel imports, WB will continue to engage with the GoM on a broader energy security and mitigation agenda through its ongoing renewable energy program, which serves as the country’s medium to long-term anchor for reducing dependence on imported fuels and easing fiscal pressures. Existing operations supporting solar generation, battery storage, and grid integration are already helping lower fuel import needs and associated subsidy costs, thereby complementing short-term fuel support. Future potential areas of technical support from the Bank, including under the ongoing energy program, could include analytics and advisory work to assist the Government assess policy options that strengthen energy security during the transition. A comprehensive approach to sector development would help link short-term fuel stabilization with a clear pathway toward a more resilient, lower-cost, and fiscally sustainable energy system anchored in renewable energy. E. Role of Partners 19. While the Project is a standalone WB-financed operation, opportunities for future synergies with development partners are being explored. F. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 20. The Project design reflects lessons from WB emergency IPF operations in Small Island Developing States and from previous commodity import support operations implemented over the past two decades such as Ukraine Agriculture Recovery Inclusive Support Emergency Project (P180732) and Sri Lanka COVID‑19 Emergency Response and Health Systems Preparedness Project (P173867). Key lessons and their incorporation into the project design include the following: • Simplicity and speed of disbursement mechanisms are critical in emergency operations. Experience from emergency operations shows that complex disbursement mechanisms can significantly delay implementation and undermine the effectiveness of crisis response. The Project therefore adopts a simple reimbursement mechanism Page 5 The World Bank Maldives Emergency Support for Critical Services Project (P516182) PROJECT APPRAISAL DOCUMENT based on verified fuel import payment receipts from the STO, relying on documentation that is routinely produced and can be readily verified by the WB financial management teams. • Leveraging existing implementation arrangements improves readiness and execution. Emergency operations perform better when they are built on established institutional structures rather than creating new project implementation arrangements. The Project will therefore utilize the existing Project Management Unit (PMU) under the MCGP within the Ministry of Finance and Public Enterprises (MoFPE). The MoFPE will provide comprehensive oversight and strategic support required to ensure all project activities are executed. • Clearly defined eligible expenditures are essential to ensure fiduciary clarity and timely disbursement. Previous operations have demonstrated that broad or ambiguous eligibility criteria can lead to delays in verification and audit processes. The Project addresses this risk by adopting a positive list approach based on clearly defined petroleum product categories (Harmonized System codes), ensuring transparent and verifiable eligibility for reimbursement. III. PROJECT IMPLEMENTATION A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 21. The Project will utilize the existing PMU under the MCGP within the MoFPE which will serve as the Implementing Agency (IA) for the Project and will have overall responsibility for project coordination, fiduciary oversight, and compliance with WB requirements. The PMU will oversee Project implementation, including financial management, procurement aspects, monitoring and reporting, and adherence to environmental and social commitments. The PMU will be responsible for liaising with the WB, consolidating project documentation, and ensuring timely submission of reports and withdrawal applications. The PMU is well placed to handle this function given familiarity with WB requirements and adequate staffing (with no additional hiring anticipated). Creating a new project implementation modality for this project is not feasible given the compressed, time-bound and task-specific nature of implementation and other alternative options within the MoFPE are not feasible. 22. The State Trading Organization (STO)5, a majority government-owned enterprise, will support the MoFPE in Project implementation, serving as the operational entity responsible for the procurement and importation of eligible fuel under the Project. The STO will leverage its existing long-term contractual arrangements with an existing fuel supplier and its nationwide fuel distribution network to procure petroleum products required for electricity generation, water desalination, and inter-island transport. The STO will execute fuel purchase transactions, make payments to the supplier, and maintain complete and verifiable documentation related to the defined positive list of eligible petroleum expenditures financed under the supply contracts. This documentation will be submitted to the IA to support reimbursement under the Project. Fuel purchases financed under the operation will be limited to eligible petroleum expenditures under existing and any potential future supply contracts and will be subject to applicable WB requirements, including documentation verification and relevant fiduciary and integrity provisions. All relevant contracts will be reviewed and approved by the WB to confirm their eligibility for Bank financing and compliance with applicable fiduciary and integrity requirements. Results Monitoring, Evaluation, and Verification Arrangements 24. Project monitoring and evaluation will focus on tracking the continuity of fuel supply for essential utilities and logistics services during the period of external shock. The primary PDO indicator will measure the extent to which average daily fuel supply to island power grids is maintained relative to the precrisis baseline. Data for results monitoring will be drawn from STO operational records, including fuel import, distribution, and delivery data, and consolidated by the MoFPE for reporting to the WB. 25. STO will provide periodic operational data and supporting documentation to the IA (the existing MCGP PMU) within the MoFPE, which will be responsible for consolidating results information and submitting semi-annual progress reports to the WB. Verification of results will rely on documentary evidence from the STO, including supplier invoices, payment records, and fuel delivery logs, supplemented by WB implementation support and spot checks as needed. The IA will also coordinate with state-owned utilities to gather and share information with the WB on outcome indicators related to availability and beneficiaries of utility services. C. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis Climate Change and Paris Alignment 29. The proposed operation is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement. The operation is assessed as consistent with the country climate commitments including Maldives Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), Energy Roadmap (2024-2033) and the Maldives Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR, 2024). The project does not materially affect the GoM’s pursuit of its third NDC commitments under the Paris Agreement or its broader low-carbon development trajectory and is assessed as consistent with the country’s climate strategies. In the energy sector specifically, the Maldives NDC aims to increase the share of renewables in electricity generation to 30 percent by 2030. The Maldives Energy Roadmap reinforces this, targeting 33 percent of national electricity demand from renewable sources by 2028. The CCDR identifies macroeconomic stability and the creation of fiscal space as a prerequisite for climate investment, noting that high public debt (133.4 percent of GDP in 2024) and dependence on volatile tourism revenues severely constrain the country's capacity to finance the green transition. The emergency financing is temporary and sustains existing systems rather than building new fossil fuel infrastructures. A technical assistance window shall be utilized using the existing MCGP project to assess options for demand-side management, energy efficiency, and fuel supply diversification, which may contribute to preparing the ground for the renewable transition envisaged under the NDC, Energy Roadmap and CCDR. On Mitigation, the risk of carbon lock-in arising from this operation is low, as the intervention is explicitly emergency in nature and time-bound, designed to bridge an acute financing gap caused by an external shock rather than to expand or entrench fossil fuel consumption patterns. The operation does not involve the construction or commissioning of new fossil fuel infrastructure, does not support the expansion of diesel-based generation capacity, and does not establish institutions or regulatory frameworks that would reinforce long-term dependence on fossil fuels. 30. The operation is also assessed as aligned with the adaptation and resilience objectives of the Paris Agreement. While the project finances the emergency procurement of fossil fuel and does not directly support climate mitigation or adaptation activities, Maldives is among the world’s most climate-vulnerable countries. The CCDR identifies coastal flooding, erosion, and ocean warming as the primary climate hazards facing the country and highlights that inadequate climate risk management could significantly undermine development outcomes. As the Project finances fuel procurement rather than physical assets, the direct exposure of project investments to climate hazards is limited. The operation does not involve the construction of physical facilities in climate-exposed locations. The main climate-related risk is the potential disruption of maritime fuel delivery due to extreme weather events; however, this represents an existing and manageable operational risk and does not pose a material threat to the Project’s development objective. The Project’s ESCP addresses risks related to fuel spills, marine pollution, and occupational health and safety during handling and transport, consistent with international standards. Overall, climate-related risks of Project investments are low, and the operation indirectly contributes to climate resilience by supporting the continuity of essential services, particularly electricity and water, for a population highly exposed to climate-related shocks. Female‑headed households (FHHs) in Maldives are persistently poorer, face a higher energy burden, and have fewer financial reserves to absorb economic shocks than male‑headed households. As primary managers of household energy, women in these households are disproportionately exposed to electricity tariff increases and supply disruptions as there is high adoption of household electric appliances such as air conditioners and refrigerators for cooling needs7 (Household Income and Expenditure Survey 2019; Maldives Gender Energy Roadmap (forthcoming); El‑Horr, 2015). These represent structural vulnerabilities under normal conditions. The current crisis risks widening existing gaps and exacerbating vulnerability for FHHs. Evidence consistently shows that emergency situations amplify pre‑existing gender disparities: FHHs typically have more limited access to savings, insurance, and social protection, leaving them less equipped to cope when utility costs spike or services are disrupted. In Maldives, where electricity and water supply depend almost entirely on imported fuel, a sustained fuel shortfall would disproportionately affect women, particularly in remote areas where concentrations of FHHs are highest and energy costs are already most burdensome. By financing the emergency importation of petroleum products to ensure the continued operation of essential utilities, the Project helps prevent a further widening of this gender gap by helping preserve access to electricity and water for households most at risk. The Project will track impact by people benefiting from utilities provided from fuel supplied, with targeting FHHs. B. Fiduciary Procurement 32.
