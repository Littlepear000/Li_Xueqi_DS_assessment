STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Project Strategic Context 1. Angola is characterized by limited economic diversification and low labor productivity, which, combined with its poverty levels, climate vulnerability and weak human capital outcomes, further constrain its growth prospects. As the fifth-largest economy and second-largest oil producer in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), Angola's growth was sustained by oil revenue until recent years, resulting in volatile growth and limited economic diversification. The share of wage jobs remains limited, with more than 73 percent of new jobs in the non-wage sector, mainly in low-productivity micro- businesses. These trends contribute to high levels of poverty and inequality: 31.1 percent of Angolans were considered ‘extremely poor’, (that is, below the international poverty line of US$2.15 per day) in 2018, significantly above the lower- middle-income country (LMIC) average of 11.3 percent, and its Gini coefficient of 0.51 is one of the highest globally.1 Angola’s Human Capital Index (HCI) of 0.36 is among the lowest in SSA (below SSA and LMIC averages of 0.40 and 0.48, respectively), hindering current and future productivity and growth. Households experiencing poverty are often larger, with female-headed households more likely to live in poverty, and with girls having a lower Human Capital Index (HCI) than boys. Achieving climate resilience is crucial for Angola's economic diversification, as key non-extractive sectors are highly climate sensitive and already under increased stress from climate variability.2 Projected increases in rainfall variability threaten agriculture, fisheries, energy production, and urban areas. Droughts threaten agricultural and fisheries productivity3,4, affecting food security and livelihoods of millions. Meanwhile, the poor, five times more vulnerable to increasing riverine and coastal flooding, risk losing 70 percent of their assets. Limited structural transformation and low labor productivity limit Angola’s prospects for increasing earnings and job quality, thereby reducing its opportunities for sustained, inclusive, and resilient economic growth. 2. Global trends and regional investments, especially in Angola’s Lobito corridor,5 can be leveraged with a convergence approach to enhance growth, employment, and human capital development while contributing to low carbon and climate-resilient development. Adoption of digital technologies and transition to a low carbon (green) economy are transforming the world of work. Online gig jobs, engaging up to 12 percent of the global labor force, offer income and digital skills development opportunities for youth, especially in developing countries like Angola.6 With rising mobile penetration (from 49 percent in 2020 to 67 percent by 2022), urban areas with higher mobile and internet access stand to benefit, enabling youth to explore new work opportunities. Angola, rich in mineral resources, expects an increased demand for green minerals and jobs due to global energy transitions, potentially boosting economic growth and employment in the medium term, if managed well. Additionally, agribusiness presents a significant opportunity for job creation by tapping into Angola’s agricultural potential, stimulating rural economies, and driving employment across the agricultural value chain, particularly for rural youth.7 Investments in the Lobito corridor, critical for transporting strategic minerals, are expected to bring economic and social benefits through upstream linkages and demand from local and international industries. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 3. Angola created 3.5 million jobs in the last decade, but most new jobs were non-wage jobs in low productivity sectors. Despite an economic slowdown since 2015, an average of 350,000 jobs were created annually between 2009 and 2019, driven by an expansion in the working population, rather than by productivity (output per capita) increases.9 More than 72 percent of these jobs were in the non-wage sector (that is, micro-businesses in the form of self-employment, unpaid family labor, and so on), and most were in unskilled occupations in the agriculture and commerce sectors, which contribute little to economic growth or worker welfare due to low wages, poor on-the-job learning opportunities, no job- linked social benefits, and increased vulnerability to the impacts of climate change. Large, modern firms create jobs slowly and employ relatively few people, while microenterprises dominate the private sector, with 55 percent being sole proprietorships. Nearly 60 percent of businesses are concentrated in Luanda. Public sector employment made substantial contributions, providing 1 million jobs (11.1 percent of the total) in 2019, an expansion from 614,000 in 2009. Since 2002, Angola's economy has shifted toward services, consumption, and construction sectors. However, agriculture remains the primary sector of employment (46.3 percent of employed), followed by the commerce and hospitality sector (22.3 percent). The lucrative extractives sector only employs 0.8 percent of workers. 4. Youth employment is a pressing challenge, driven by a rapidly growing young population that faces multiple constraints and struggles to secure the limited quality jobs.10 Youth, ages 15–34 years, constitute 30 percent of the total population11 and comprise over half of the labor force, with 6 million out of 8.9 million residing in urban areas. About 600,000 youth enter the labor force annually, many leaving school early in search of economic opportunities, but only 4 percent of them are full-time students or hold a quality job. Most (76 percent), work in poor quality jobs, predominantly in low-productivity self-employment in urban areas, less resilient to the impacts of climate change. The remaining 20 percent are considered inactive (not in education, employment, or training [NEET]) mostly in rural areas and more vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. Youth face higher unemployment rates (22 percent versus the national average of 15 percent) and represent 83 percent of the unemployed in Angola. Youth are less likely to hold wage-paying jobs in the private or public sectors. Macroeconomic conditions, firm-specific constraints, and entrepreneurship challenges mostly affect urban youth, limiting their potential as innovators and job creators. All youth face supply-side factors, such as lack of relevant skills and work experience, social barriers, information gaps, and spatial mismatches, that prevent them from being ready for quality jobs, including as potential job generators. 5. Self-employed youth and small businesses, as well as youth-led start-ups, are critical for Angola's economy and jobs but face significant productivity challenges. The average age of start-up founders in Angola is 34 years, below the global average of 37 years, and 25 percent of the founders are women (versus a global average of 16 percent). Angola’s youth lack relevant skills and experience. Youth show major deficiencies in technical skills and workplace behavior.15 Soft skills16 consistently pose a hiring constraint17 and limit youth’s entrepreneurial capacity and management efficiency. Companies report investing—at high cost—in training staff to develop skills that should have been acquired in school.18 A mismatch between the technical and professional training curricula and labor market needs, along with limited courses and spots, exacerbate the skills gap. Moreover, youth often lack work experience. Over 70 percent of private sector employers in Angola ask for at least three years of experience when hiring new workers; a lack of internship and apprenticeship opportunities further widens this gap. 7. The Government of Angola (GoA) prioritizes youth employment through coordinated efforts by multiple government institutions, as outlined in its key development strategies . The Long-Term Development Strategy (LTDS) 2025, National Development Plan (NDP, 2023–2027), and National Agenda for Employment (2023–2027) engage several government institutions to promote productive employment, job creation, and skills development across key sectors like transportation, agriculture, and manufacturing. The NDP also prioritizes digitization to create new work opportunities and foster skills development of youth. Institutionally, the Ministry of Public Administration, Labor, and Social Security (Ministério da Administração Publica Trabalho e Segurança Social [MAPTSS]) is responsible for overseeing policies related to public administration, labor, and social security. The National Institute of Employment and Professional Training (Instituto Nacional de Emprego e Formação Profissional [INEFOP]), mapped to MAPTSS, is responsible for providing and coordinating short-term vocational and non-formal professional training, implementing employment programs, and offering intermediation services. Technical education is led by the Ministry of Education (MED) and tertiary education by the Ministry of Tertiary Education, Science, Technology, and Innovation (MESCTI). The newly established National Institute of Qualifications (INQ), under MAPTSS, coordinates the new National Qualifications System (SNQ) to align technical, vocational, and professional training with the labor market by identifying, categorizing, and validating professional skills. MAPTSS, along with the Ministry of Planning (MINPLAN), implements job creation programs. Other sectoral ministries are involved in specific employment and technical-professional training programs (for example, Ministry of Agriculture and Forests [MINAGRIF]). 9. Despite this prioritization, Angola’s policies and institutional capacity limit effective outreach to provide employment opportunities at scale. Despite significant spending on active labor market programs (ALMPs) in Angola, restrictive eligibility criteria, limited scale, and overlapping objectives hinder efficient fund use and restrict access for youth, in particular for young women. The project seeks to expand employment opportunities for targeted youth in Angola by addressing the barriers that limit their access to wage jobs and entrepreneurship. Aside from the fact that the modern, formal firms create few wage jobs, skill gaps, limited work experience, and lack of networks prevent many young people—particularly women and PWD who face additional barriers and experience heightened climate vulnerability—from securing wage jobs. Additionally, limited entrepreneurship skills and financial constraints hinder youth from building successful businesses that are resilient to the impacts of local climate hazards. Inadequate institutional capacity limit labor market program delivery. Illustrated in Figure 1, the project’s theory of change outlines a targeted approach to overcome these difficulties and enhance both employability and labor productivity for Angola’s youth. 16. The project proposes a multi-faceted approach with multiple, coordinated interventions for improving beneficiaries’ labor market readiness by tackling the identified obstacles to youth productivity and employment. First, it will support current and aspiring young entrepreneurs, with differentiated approaches for women entrepreneurs, with targeted training, business development support, and financial support, empowering them to increase their productivity and create sustainable businesses and enable job creation (Component 134). Second, it will help prepare youth for wage jobs by bridging the skills gap through market-aligned training, skills certification, well-designed internship models, by offering job intermediation, mentoring, and support for digital employment, and by ensuring improved access to employability support for young women, to broaden youth access to wage employment (Component 2). Lastly, it will strengthen the capacity of national institutions to deliver labor market programs and employment services that are responsive to labor market demands, effective and sustainable over the long term (Component 3). Together, these interventions aim to increase labor productivity and access to employment opportunities for targeted youth which will also enhance their capacity to adapt and cope with climate shocks. The project's comprehensive approach to address vulnerable youths’ access to employment opportunities and increased earnings is expected to generate substantial social and private benefits, as follows: (a) enhancing youth employability and entrepreneurship, reducing the risk of social unrest caused by high youth unemployment; (b) matching employers with suitable candidates through job placement services, reducing the cost of searching and the risk of a mismatch of vacancies and applicants; (c) improving the availability and quality of jobs for vulnerable youth, which in turn may help improve household welfare; (d) supporting a greater number of youths to gain productive employment, helping Angola to benefit from its demographic dividend; and (e) facilitating youth movements into non-traditional sectors of employment, contributing to reducing the gender gap in the labor market. 57. The project is expected to have net positive economic benefits and generate sustainable results. The IRR and NPV are calculated for each subcomponent and component and for the whole project, assuming a 10 percent discount with sensitivity analysis. The project yields an internal rate of return (IRR) of 29 percent, with a resulting net present value (NPV) of US$81.5 million. The benefits of each component are measured as the expected average increase in annual earnings resulting from program participation.81 Unquantified benefits include positive effects that strengthened training and employment services have on wages; improvements in welfare and increased productivity by equipping vulnerable youth with job-relevant and entrepreneurial skills and support; and enhanced social cohesion due to lower youth unemployment and targeting of vulnerable youth and women. The project focuses on capacity building for youth to promote access to employment and enhance productivity as well as on institutional strengthening for INEFOP. The activities to promote access to employment opportunities (internships and entrepreneurship grants/support for vulnerable youth) are also universally aligned as they will not consider firms handling only fossil fuels nor will they include universally non-aligned activities. While the national building code for Angola does not have energy performance standards, the two training centers are/will be fully electrified and grid connected, will not use fossil fuels for space and water heating nor include energy intensive equipment. As such, the construction works and rehabilitation activities for the training centers are considered to pose a low risk and the emissions impact is expected to be negligible. In addition, the mitigation of GHG-emissions of the construction and rehabilitation works, will be maximized through low carbon input and locally sourced materials, with several energy efficiency features also considered, such as prioritizing purchase of energy-efficient equipment including LED lights, backup (renewable) energy sources, and rainwater collection systems. In sum, the project is not expected to have a negative impact on Angola’s low GHG-emissions development pathways and as such it is considered aligned for mitigation. 63.
