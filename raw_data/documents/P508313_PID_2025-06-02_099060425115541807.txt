Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Cambodia has a young population, predominantly rural, and is marked by high female labor force participation but significant gender disparities in employment quality. About 60 percent of Cambodia’s population of over 16 million live in rural areas. Children (ages 0–14) and youth (ages 15–24) constitute 29 percent and 17 percent of the population, respectively. Internal migration is becoming more prevalent as people move in search of jobs. Women’s labor force participation is high compared with other countries. In 2022, 69.6 percent of women were in the labor force versus 82.1 percent of men. However, women are less likely to be in formal, better paid, and secure forms of employment. Limited access to affordable and quality childcare services in Cambodia significantly constrains women's ability to participate in formal employment, as traditional gender roles often assign primary caregiving responsibilities to mothers. 2. In the two decades preceding the pandemic, Cambodia experienced uninterrupted economic expansion. Cambodia was one of the fastest growing economies in the world between 1998 and 2019 with a sustained average real growth rate of 7.7 percent, driven largely by tourism, manufacturing exports, real estate, and construction. The country reached lower middle-income status in 2015 and is aspiring to attain upper middle-income status by 2030 and high- income status by 2050. Cambodia has achieved considerable improvements in socioeconomic indicators such as health Page 2 Official Use Only The World Bank Cambodia Basic Education Improvement Project (P508313) and education, but equitable access to basic public services remains a challenge. The poverty rate in the country declined from 33.8 percent in 2009 to 17.8 percent in 2019. 3. However, Cambodia’s current economic success has been built largely on the expansion of relatively low - technology, low wage/skill production in such industries as textiles, apparel, and basic electronics. Cambodia’s industrial sector remains weak and narrow as it heavily relies on exporting low-values products, placing Cambodia in low position in the Global Value Chains (GCVs).1 Moreover, its comparative advantage in these industries is being eroded as wages increase, making unit labor costs (as a share of value added) very high among comparators2. 4. To maintain growth prospects in the long term and remain competitive, Cambodia must diversify its exports and transform its labor-intensive industrial structure into a knowledge-intensive model to move to the next stages of participation in GVCs. The country’s Pentagonal Strategy-Phase I (PS-I) and Industrial Development Policy (IDP) (2015- 2025) specifically calls for creating a competitive economy through knowledge and innovation. The RGC has further identified priority sectors in its IDP, including automotive, electronic, and agro-industrial sectors. Its National Research Agenda 2025 also spells out key research areas related to these priority industries, including food production and processing, cloud-based services, electronic and mechanical spare parts, and energy supply. 5. Such diversification and transformation require Cambodia to ensure that its workforce has more and better education, starting with a focus on ensuring that everyone has foundational literacy and numeracy skills. However, _For Official Use Only_ Cambodia will not be able to expand its workforce of high-skilled workers unless it ensures that more children have basic numeracy skills and can read with understanding by the end of primary grades. 6. Recognizing the importance of human capital development, the Royal Government of Cambodia (RGC) has given it high priority in its PS-I. Specifically, the Strategy recognizes the central role of education in boosting economic mobility, raising incomes, and improving employability, and, thereby, driving poverty reduction and economic growth. Sectoral and Institutional Context 7. The education sector is overseen by the Ministry of Education, Youth and Sport (MoEYS), which is responsible for formulating policies, managing programs, and overseeing the delivery of education services. MoEYS works closely with sub-national administrations, which, under the country’s decentralization and deconcentration (D&D) reforms, have been delegated certain responsibilities for service delivery, including education, to provincial, district, and commune-level authorities. Specifically, with recent reforms, the management of early childhood and primary education teachers has been devolved to district and municipal administrations. Non-governmental actors, including private schools and NGOs, also play a significant role in education delivery, particularly in rural areas. These institutions are integrated into the education system, adhering to national curricula, standards, and examinations. Education outcomes are mixed: on the one hand, Cambodia has made remarkable strides in increasing access to education; between 2001 and 2023, primary, lower secondary, and upper secondary enrollment rates rose from 84 to 96 percent, 17 to 65 percent, and 8 to 44 percent, respectively. By contrast, recent assessments of students’ learning outcomes show that most students are not acquiring foundational literacy and numeracy skills. For instance, in a national assessment conducted in 2021, 81 percent of Grade 6 students were not meeting minimum proficiency levels in math and Khmer. The findings from the national assessment echo findings from the regional Southeast Asia Primary Learning Metric (SEA-PLM 2019)4 which showed that 89 percent of 5th graders in Cambodia were not meeting minimum proficiency levels in reading while 81 percent were not meeting the minimum proficiency level in math. 9. As a result of Cambodia’s participation in international assessments and the rich contextual information collected as part of these assessments, the underlying causes of the poor education outcomes are becoming clearer. Analysis of these assessments suggest that six principal causes explain the results: (i) children are not ready to learn; (ii) teachers are not ready to teach; (iii) children are being allowed to fall behind; (iv) the problem of students who are falling behind is being compounded by large classes; (v) shortages of key inputs (such as textbooks) and poor infrastructure is impeding learning; and (vi) the management of the sector has not been modernized for the efficient use of the allocated resources.5 _For Official Use Only_ 10. In its Education Sector Plan (2024-28), the MoEYS has outlined a plan to improve educational outcomes. The plan aims to ensure Cambodian children are enrolled in Early Childhood Education (ECE) programs and ready for primary school at age six. It also seeks to have all primary school-aged children complete primary school with good competency in core subjects and life skills, and for Cambodian youth to complete secondary education with the necessary knowledge, skills, discipline, ethics, and behavior for a better future. 11. To achieve these goals, the plan includes several strategic actions. First, the plan targets 76 percent of grade-one entrants to have preprimary school experience by 2028. It aims to increase ECE enrollment through the provision of more preprimary schools and well-trained ECE teachers, as well as community-based childcare centers and parental education. Second, it aims to scale up Early Grade Learning (EGL) in all primary schools, addressing gaps in early grade reading (EGR) and early grade math (EGM). Currently, there are significant gaps in EGR implementation for grade 3 and EGM implementation for grades 2 and 3. Third, it proposes interventions for students falling behind, such as additional teaching hours and learning clubs. Fourth, it plans to attract and retain education specialists through Continuous Professional Development (CPD) and Teacher Career Pathways (TCP), with an intention to produce 300 in-house education specialists by 2028. And fifth, it emphasizes the need for school construction and renovation to reduce overcrowded classrooms, especially in suburban, northern, and western regions. 12. Climate change and natural disasters pose significant challenges for Cambodia, particularly impacting the continuity of education and having cumulative and compound effects on human capital accumulation. For instance, the 2011 floods affected more than 1.7 million people, killed 250, and left 50,000 families homeless.6 This disaster highlighted the vulnerability of the country's educational infrastructure. Tropical cyclones, which have the potential to cause flooding and wind damage, affect Cambodia an average of six times a year between April and November. This frequent exposure to cyclones further exacerbates the risks to educational facilities. 69 percent of educational facilities are located in areas vulnerable to tropical depressions with wind speeds below 61 km/h, while 31 percent are in regions exposed to cyclones with wind speeds between 62 and 88 km/h. The escalating threat of extreme heat is also emerging as a major danger to both lives and livelihoods. In Phnom Penh, Cambodia, the urban heat island effect results in 23 to 25 heat wave days each year—a figure expected to more than double by 2050 in some districts of the city. These climate and disaster risks result in prolonged school closures, loss of instructional time, and reduced access to safe learning environments. The cumulative economic impact exacerbates dropout rates and undermines long-term educational outcomes, particularly for children in disaster-prone regions. Education projects must mitigate these risks and remain vulnerable to implementation disruptions, such as disruption to training transportation, unexpected school closures, or damage to project-supplied materials. As such, the project intends to directly address climate and disaster vulnerabilities to beneficiaries and project implementation. C. The project’s activities have been selected because of their strong evidence base for improving student learning outcomes, at scale and in a cost effective way.7 As will be discussed below, the ambition of the proposed project is to ensure that every primary school student in the country can achieve minimum proficiency in numeracy and literacy. As such, the project has been designed to be scalable in scope and cost, allowing it to potentially accommodate additional finance or follow-on projects and/or additional resources from development partners, should those resources materialize. 15. The proposed project will complement and build on activities already under implementation by other development partners, and an ongoing IDA project, the General Education Improvement Project (GEIP). Specifically, first, several development partners have supported the design and, in select provinces, supported implementing of a best-practice way of strengthening teaching, using an Early Grade Learning (EGL) package. Basic Education Improvemnet Project (BEIP) will build on these efforts, seeking to bring them to nationwide implementation, and institutionalize them. Second, the GEIP focuses on establishing robust school governance, strengthening school management, improving accountability, and enhancing community engagement. This governance-focused approach creates the foundational structure for the proposed project to effectively implement its teaching and learning interventions, including EGL and Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL), along with increased instructional hours and enhanced teaching materials. While GEIP improves school management capacity, student assessments, and infrastructure across 2,000 primary schools, BEIP will strengthen the teaching approach in classrooms and institutionalize the mentoring system for teachers, not only in GEIP targeted schools but in all schools (building on _For Official Use Only_ the foundation laid by other development partners). 17. Component 1: Improving Primary School Readiness (US$12.5 million). This component aims to enhance the primary school readiness of children and address the issues highlighted in the sectoral context. The component will implement two main strategies: (i) institutionalizing cost-effective models of full day public childcare services by developing approximately 60 childcare centers for 0-2 year-olds, leveraging existing public preschools, and (ii) improving access and quality of the public ECE system through expansion of formal ECE classes and teacher training for 3-5 year-olds. 18. Sub-Component 1.1: Developing sustainable early childcare service (US$10.0 million). The childcare centers will be developed using lessons learned from the recently completed CBCC project (P171063), prioritizing areas near garment or industrial factories with high demand for childcare services to support working mothers (noting that 80 percent of garment workers are female). The project will provide necessary facilities including childcare center buildings, additional rooms, playgrounds, kitchen equipment and/or WASH components, based on facility standards, climate-informed designs, and individual center needs. Healthy snacks and/or meals will also be provided through community support, with the scope and implementation determined by community decisions, to enhance children's nutrition. Comprehensive 15-day training based on the updated operational manual will be provided to MoEYS staff and childcare center staff, who will be mobilized from the community. The project aims to develop the capacity of MoEYS to institutionalize childcare training at the Pre-School Teacher Training Center (PSTTC) and ensure that supported childcare centers meet MoEYS standards to receive operational and staff cost support from the government. Additionally, this subcomponent will promote parental education and raise community awareness about the importance of childcare through targeted campaigns. 19. Sub-Component 1.2: Improving Access and Quality of early childhood education (US$2.5 million). This subcomponent aims to strengthen early childhood education for children aged 3-5 years through improved access and enhanced quality of ECE services. To expand access, the project will construct 60 formal ECE classrooms attached Page 6 Official Use Only The World Bank Cambodia Basic Education Improvement Project (P508313) to public primary schools that currently lack preschool classes but demonstrate high demand for early childhood education. Quality improvements will be achieved through a comprehensive capacity-building approach that includes: (i) provision of teaching and learning materials and/or equipment for ongoing 500 community-based preschools, (ii) implementation of an updated teacher training program for 560 in-service teachers through in- person modalities and over 2,000 teachers through virtual delivery, with both approaches integrating a play-center approach, and (iii) conduct a school readiness program pilot for remote districts without any preschools. The progression to Phase 2 is conditional on Phase 1 results demonstrating quality and feasibility in terms of: (a) technical and system development, (b) alignment with government regulations, and (c) sustainability. 28. The overall environmental and social risk classification is Substantial. The project will be implemented nationwide, including in the north-eastern provinces which includes areas of biodiversity and Physical Cultural _For Official Use Only_ Resource significance. The north-eastern provinces where interventions will take place are home to a number of indigenous peoples with substantial gaps and disparities in poverty and quality of education. The project is expected to complement and build on activities already under implementation by other development partners, and an ongoing IDA project, the General Education Improvement Project (GEIP: P174335) to improve educational outcomes for beneficiary school children and teachers. Eight of the Environmental and Social Standards (ESSs) are relevant (ESS1, ESS2, ESS3, ESS4, ESS5, ESS7, ESS8, and ESS10) based on environmental and social assessment carried out during project preparation and lesson from ongoing education projects. 31. The environmental risk rating is assessed to be Moderate. Component 1 activities include financing the renovation of existing school buildings, construction of additional classrooms and provision of digital tools to improve educational outcomes. The environmental risks and impacts from the project activities, especially from enhancement learning infrastructure, are expected to be site specific, temporary and reversible and readily mitigatable. Increased use of school facilities may lead to higher energy consumption and additional waste (water, solid) generation, Occupational Health and Safety hazards during construction, community risks from additional construction related traffic, and likely e-waste from the end-of-life use of digital tools under component 2. The construction of classrooms, however, will not be financed if the sites are in sensitive natural critical habitats, marine biodiversity or protected areas. The potential locations will be in mostly existing school compounds or state lands owned by MoEYS. Therefore, project activities are not expected to result in an adverse environmental impacts or biodiversity conservation or natural habitat areas. From previous education projects, including on-going GEIP and HEIP projects, there is no asbestos related material found in the existing schools campus. However, if it is found under this project, the asbestos management plan will be advised. 32. The project social risk rating is Substantial, considering lessons from previous education projects on institutional capacity to manage risks and impacts per the ESF requirements, and the magnitude and nationwide scale of BEIP activities. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 36. The project will follow the existing implementation arrangements of an ongoing GEIP (P174335). MoEYS will assume overall responsibility for coordination and implementation of the project including procurement, disbursement, FM, safeguards, and others. The Project’s activities will be directly implemented by technical departments and other units at the national and sub-national levels under MoEYS. At the highest level, the Project Steering Committee (PSC), established by MoEYS, will be responsible for the oversight of the Education Strategic Plan to streamline policy development, strategic planning, and implementation decision making processes. The PSC is led by MoEYS Minister and comprised of MoEYS leaders, including Secretaries of States and/or Under-Secretaries of State. At the implementation level, the Project Management Unit (PMU), chaired by Secretary of State and comprised of relevant Under-Secretaries of State, Secretariat General, Director Generals, Deputy Secretariat General, Deputy Director General, and Department Directors will provide support to the PSC, and provides day-to- day implementation oversight, monitoring financial management, procurement, project progress reporting, auditing, monitoring and evaluation and reviewing the operational plans.
