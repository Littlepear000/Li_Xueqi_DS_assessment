INTRODUCTION AND COUNTRY CONTEXT 1. The proposed Development Policy Finance (DPF) operation supports the program of the Government of Mexico (GoM) to promote women’s economic opportunities and sustainable productivity growth. The operation supports policy reforms that seek to increase women’s ownership of assets and their ability to create and expand businesses by facilitating their access to finance. Second, the reforms supported will facilitate women’s access to jobs and increase female labor force participation by improving the efficiency and safety of mobility that addresses the specific needs of women and by increasing social protection for domestic workers and the overall safety and security of women against gender-based violence (GBV). It also supports reforms in transport infrastructure to improve the efficiency and safety of mobility, reduce carbon emissions, and further improve women's access to jobs and markets. 2. Mexico is an upper-middle-income country and a member of the Organization for Economic Co- operation and Development (OECD), but it cannot realize its full development potential without addressing existing gender gaps. Despite substantial improvements in gender equality, from declining fertility and maternal mortality rates to rising life expectancy and closing gaps in primary and secondary education enrollment, significant gender disparities in economic opportunities remain. These include women’s low labor force participation, high employment informality, low-productivity self-employment, and limited access to productive inputs. Mexican women ages 15–24 are 16 percentage points more likely than young men to be out of employment, education, or training. Less than one in three graduates from science, technology, engineering, and mathematics (STEM) fields is a woman.1 Female labor force participation is only 45 percent versus an average of 51 percent in the Latin America and the Caribbean region and 52 percent in OECD countries. This gap is particularly large in states such as Chiapas, Veracruz, Tabasco, and Querétaro, where women’s labor force participation is less than 40 percent (32 percent in Chiapas). Mexico’s gender gap in labor market participation is associated with a potential loss of 25 percent of income per capita,2 while the economic cost of GBV, including in the workplace and transit to work, likely exceeds 2 percent of gross domestic product (GDP).3 Improving women’s access to economic opportunities is key to reducing inequalities in Mexico. 3. Institutional constraints hamper women's access to productive inputs and labor force participation, perpetuating gender gaps and undermining economic development. Care responsibilities are overwhelmingly carried out by women, who spend more than twice the time on unpaid domestic and care work than men.4 Lack of trust in childcare services is reported as the most frequent reason not to work, with only 8.7 percent of Mexican children ages 0 to 3 years enrolled in early childhood care and education (ECCE) services, compared with an average of 25.0 percent in the OECD. 4. Institutional and financing constraints also hamper the availability of efficient and safe low- emissions transport that can facilitate women’s access to economic opportunities, driving sustainable productivity growth. Mexico has undergone an intense urbanization process over the past three decades, increasing the urban population twofold and the urban footprint sevenfold. More than 80 percent of Mexico’s GDP and 77 percent of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions are generated within urban areas.5 Doubling a municipality’s density is associated with an increase in the productivity premium by 3 percent for manufacturing and 12 percent for services if quality infrastructure, solid institutions, and complementary policies to support the economic dynamism of entrepreneurs and firms are present. However, diseconomies of scale, congestion, and negative externalities prevail in the absence of these conditions.6 For example, firms have reduced access to workers with specific skills in cities with long commuting times, congestion, and high transport costs,7 leading to lower productivity. About 14 percent of Mexico’s workforce spends over two hours per day commuting to their jobs, reaching 20–23 percent in southern states such as Oaxaca, Chiapas, and Guerrero and 25 percent in Mexico City.8 5. Mexico’s urban sprawl and inadequate transport systems particularly affect women and the poor. Women have differential mobility needs and typically more limited transport options. For example, 53 percent of women in Mexico City depend on inefficient bus transport (colectivos), compared to 48 percent of men.9 Security is also a greater concern for women when making transport mode choices, with 5.1 percent of women having stopped using public transport in 2021 due to perceived insecurity10and increasingly preferring private transport to avoid sexual harassment.11 Inefficient urban transport also disproportionately penalizes the poor, who often live on city boundaries, far from employment centers, and depend more on public transport and non-motorized modes. Sustainably increasing Mexico’s productivity calls for expanding efficient, safe, low-emissions urban transport that incorporates a gender perspective. Mexico’s transport infrastructure and services are vulnerable to climate risk. Over 18 percent of Mexico’s transport assets are exposed to climate risk—primarily floods and landslides—and the expected annual damages are US$715 million in rehabilitation and repairs, while the disruption-related losses for firms are as high as US$8.6 billion per year.12 Infrastructure along Mexico’s coastline is vulnerable to tropical cyclones and hurricanes from both the Atlantic and the Pacific oceans.13 Overall, climate vulnerability is particularly high in the southern states, reinforcing regional economic disparities: in Chiapas, Oaxaca, and Guerrero, over 80 percent of primary roads are estimated to be exposed to flood risk and over 25 percent to landslide risk.14 There are 200 sites in Mexico at high risk of river (fluvial) flooding, mainly in Veracruz, Tabasco, San Luis Potosi, Chiapas, and Oaxaca. Moreover, extreme temperatures pose the danger of the road surface cracking and deforming on 99 percent of the country’s roads.15 As in other countries, climate risks also disproportionately affect vulnerable sub-groups within Mexico, including women, who tend to have lower asset ownership, access to information and finance, and control over natural resources, and, therefore, lower overall adaptive capacity.16 Women’s responses to climate disasters are typically more constrained due to social disadvantages,17 and women are more likely to suffer from climate-induced food shortages and malnutrition.18 Finally, in the aftermath of climate disasters, displaced women tend to face an increased risk of exploitation and trafficking, limited access to healthcare, and reduced access to formalized safety nets.19 Mexico’s National Climate Change Strategy recommends addressing climate vulnerability with investments in the adaptation of infrastructure, such as bridges at risk of flooding, and to consider the aspects of gender and socio-economic inequality in the design of all climate change policies. 7. Finally, the DPF follows the Maximizing Finance for Development approach, enabling private capital leverage in infrastructure, particularly urban public transport. 8. Many supported reforms address institutional constraints at the sector level, while some have important regional and global demonstration effects. In Mexico’s federal system, policies for gender or transport are often designed at the central (federal) level, while implementation rests with states and municipalities. For example, the regulation and management of public transport lie under the subnational jurisdiction, which is responsible for, among others, licensing, setting transit fares, and defining service requirements. The federal government supports transport development or gender policies through budget transfers to states, municipalities, and federal programs. This de facto devolution of the implementing role to subnational entities calls for (i) strengthening institutions at the central level, such as the Secretary for Agriculture, Land, and Urban Development (SEDATU) or the National Institute for Women (INMUJERES), to design and monitor overall sectoral strategies and policies; and (ii) improving coordination across institutions from different levels of government to help translate national standards and guidelines into State/local policies. Some reforms supported by this operation also have important global demonstration effects, especially in reducing GBVs and gender considerations in sustainable bonds. Closing gender gaps is also key for boosting productivity growth and helping Mexico reach its full development potential (Box 1). Part of Mexico’s growth underperformance comes from the gap in labor force participation (after accounting for household production), followed by gender gaps in entrepreneurship and self-employment. The WB 2021 report Participación Laboral de la Mujer en México alerts of barriers to women’s labor participation in certain economic activities in urban areas related to more limited transport options.26 Women often report preferring job proximity over job quality, taking up opportunities that are closer to home but more flexible or informal, and reducing the need for transport services that are scarce, expensive, and unsafe. 27 Other factors further discourage women from joining the labor market or force them toward jobs nearer home or unpaid work. 28 For example, it is still not forbidden in job interviews to ask about a woman’s family situation. On the supply side, individual characteristics and lack of access to productive inputs hinder female labor force participation. Only 35 percent of Mexican women owned at least one high-value asset in 2018 compared to 58 percent of men. Only 26.5 percent of women had access to formal credit. The main barrier identified for the low female labor force participation relates to unmet childcare needs, coupled with social norms and low career expectations for women. The Gender Assessment (2019) reports that Mexican women spend 9 to 12 more hours a week than men taking care of children and 17 to 26 more hours a week than men on household chores. On average, women in the OECD countries spend around 5 hours a day in unpaid work; Mexican women spend more than 6.5 hours. Market, cultural, and institutional features explain much of the observed gender earnings gap that individual characteristics, sectors, or job descriptions cannot explain. This also applies to the differences in performance between male and female-owned businesses unexplained by owner or firm characteristics. 2. MACROECONOMIC POLICY FRAMEWORK 2.1. RECENT ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENTS 9. Mexico has already recovered pre-pandemic GDP levels in 2023. After an 8.0 percent economic contraction in 2020, Mexico’s GDP grew 4.7 percent in 2021 and 3.1 percent in 2022. Domestic mobility restrictions were eliminated in April 2022, contributing to a rebound in retail, accommodation and food services, and transportation, helping aggregate services recover. Manufacturing has also bounced back with U.S. demand, particularly cars and electronics. In 2022, value-added and employment grew in most subsectors, except construction. Economic recovery is also present at the regional level: most states have grown except Campeche, with rising employment in most states, albeit with a significant contribution from informal employment. 10. Private investment has stagnated, partially offset by a recent jump in public infrastructure. Over the last five years, the average growth rate of total investment was 2.6 percent in 2000-2015, -1.1 percent in 2016-2019, and -17.7 percent in 2020 during the pandemic. Investment growth has rebounded to 10.5 percent in 2021 and an estimated 6.5 percent in 2022. In total, over the period, the contribution of investment to economic growth has thus been negligible. The Law of Mobility and Road Safety (the Law) sets a clear institutional framework for efficient transport management across the three levels of government, granting municipal governments the jurisdiction to formulate and implement relevant plans. The 2022 Financial Sector Assessment Program (FSAP) found that Mexico has a vibrant retail payments market in terms of instruments, and there is a great potential to further leverage SPEI (including CoDi) to advance financial inclusion. 54. Substance of the PA. The PA supports regulatory reforms to enhance the availability of digital payment services through clear rules for the indirect participation of financial institutions in SPEI while also enhancing the consumer experience for SPEI and CoDi services and ensuring a robust and modern payments system. The regulations establish rules for indirect participation in SPEI and set out the specific parameters under which direct participants will offer indirect participation services. These include limitations on the service to avoid potential disruptions to SPEI and rules to ensure competition between different types of participants. The new regulated indirect participation scheme is expected to have a positive impact on financial inclusion by enabling greater and more affordable46 access to SPEI for financial institutions and thus improving accessibility and affordability of SPEI-enabled digital payment services (for example, international remittances and CoDi payments) for their customers. The regulation also establishes separate processing modules for different types of SPEI transactions to guarantee the operational resiliency of the system and ensure that all payments are processed with high service standards. These measures are expected to improve the reliability of SPEI and therefore increase trust in digital payment services among consumers (helping address a key barrier to financial inclusion, particularly for women). Finally, the reform increases the transaction limit for CoDi transfers from MXN 8,000 to MXN 21,000, which is expected to make CoDi more attractive to a larger segment of merchants and users. In combination, the reforms are expected to improve the accessibility, affordability, and reliability of SPEI-enabled digital payment services and facilitate the deployment of more overlay services by SPEI participants, including services that address the financial needs of the vulnerable and financially excluded population segments, including women. Complementary demand-side interventions to improve the financial and digital capabilities of financially excluded populations—including women—are under way in line with Objective 4 of Mexico’s PNIF. 55. Expected results. Reforms that foster the growth of digital financial services in Mexico and reliability/trust among consumers are expected to help close the digital financial inclusion gender gap, given international evidence on the benefits of digital payment services for women. The results will be measured by the gender gap in the share of adults (ages 18 to 70) making digital merchant payments (baseline: 6.3 percentage points, 2021; target: 5.0 percentage points, December 2024), as measured via Mexico’s National Financial Inclusion Survey. PA1, which improves the guidelines to promote safety for women in sustainable modes of transport, will likely positively affect poverty and reduce gender gaps. An unsafe public transportation system restricts women’s freedom of movement, affecting health, family relationships, and quality of life. There is evidence that women, to feel safer, choose lower-quality colleges and longer travel time and spend more money commuting than men79. The Mexican economy has been characterized by its resiliency, openness to international trade and global value chains, strong institutions, stable economic steerage, and a solid fiscal position. This has allowed it to have robust growth after the pandemic, with a consolidated fiscal balance due to the restructuring of public spending, increased revenue driven by more audits to large taxpayers, low acquisition of public debt, and an adequate refinancing policy. This has allowed the application of a fiscal policy that is socially oriented and seeks to improve the quality of life, especially among low-income groups. This progress in the economic arena has not been without its hurdles, such as the high and persistent inflation recently affecting Mexico. To offset the expected inflation, Banxico has strongly increased the reference interest rate, setting it at record highs. This administration has also implemented fiscal policies to mitigate the impact of inflation on the population, such as the Package Against Inflation and Shortages (Pacic) and the Covenant on Openness Against Inflation and Shortages (Apecic). These programs include a reduction in tariffs for food imports, in addition to agreements with the private sector to contain the price hikes, as well as fiscal stimuli to moderate fuel prices. The costs of these programs were partly covered by extraordinary revenues accrued by high oil prices. Against this backdrop, and on behalf of the Mexican Government, the World Bank and the Government prepared a project based on public policy actions with the objective of promoting economic opportunities for women and sustainable growth of productivity. In particular, it sought to improve the social protection of vulnerable women in the labor market. One of the main elements of this reform was to bolster the institutional strength of the entities charged with implementing the Comprehensive Model of Primary Integral Actions for the Primary Prevention of Violence against Women (MIPPVM). This is expected to have a positive impact on the health of all Mexicans in the longer term. Increasing funding for sustainable transport will facilitate a sustainable transition in many ways, including by expanding the availability of funding for projects already planned and enabling green projects and projects with a high social impact that would otherwise not be implemented.
