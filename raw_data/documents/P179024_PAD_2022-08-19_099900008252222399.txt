Page 6 of 61 The World Bank Strengthening Social Assistance and Labor Market Programs Project (P179024) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Despite strong economic growth before COVID-19, the Kyrgyz Republic remains one of the poorest countries in the Europe and Central Asia region. With a gross national income per capita of US$1,220 in 2018, the country has experienced strong but volatile economic growth, averaging 4 percent per year since 2010 (ranging from 0.5 percent in 2010 to 10.9 percent in 2013). The country has a population of about 6.2 million (over 60 percent living in rural areas) of which 64 percent are within the working age of 15–64 years and less than 40 percent (2.4 million) are employed. Given the limited absorbing capacity of domestic labor markets, it is estimated that over 15 percent of the population is working abroad. Despite progress in reducing poverty, more than 1.2 million people in the country (20.1 percent in 2019) were living below the national poverty line before COVID-19. Internationally comparable poverty measures based on the international $3.20-a-day poverty line (using 2011 purchasing power parity conversions rates)—the measure most relevant for lower-middle-income countries—also show a significant decline. 2. Economic growth has been largely driven by gold extraction and workers’ remittances from abroad and has not translated proportionally into labor force growth. Growth before the COVID-19 pandemic resulted in only modest improvements in welfare among the bottom 40 percent coupled with falling incomes for the upper 60 percent. Furthermore, jobs have not been created in the formal sector, and most of the employment creation took place in the informal sector, estimated to be around 50 percent of gross domestic product (GDP) and 60 percent of the total employment. The poverty gap at international measures ($3.20 per capita) has also stalled since 2010. Economic growth and poverty reduction are below the country’s potential due to a business environment inconducive to private investment and job creation, weak public institutions and services, and constrained human capital that lacks essential skills required for a more productive and dynamic economy. 3. The COVID-19 crisis has undone the gains in poverty reduction achieved over the past few years, and a sizeable proportion of the population fell back into poverty. The crisis had adverse effects on labor incomes which have been the major contributors to poverty reduction before the pandemic. This, combined with the expected fall in the purchasing power of a substantial proportion of the population due to inflation and the lack of mitigating measures, left large segments of the population highly exposed to the economic consequences of the pandemic. Following the economic downturn due to the COVID-19 pandemic, the headline poverty rate increased by more than 5 percentage points in 2020, reversing the downward trend observed since 2013. As a result, an estimated 364,000 people fell back into poverty between 2019 and 2020. The food insecurity prevalence rate based on direct responses from people about their experiences of constrained access to food also showed a sharp rise in 2020. 4. After a brief rebound in 2021 from the pandemic, the Kyrgyz economy is now hit by another major shock—the decline in the Russian economy—through multiple channels. In the absence of mitigating measures, the large negative spillovers associated with this economic contraction are expected to reverse the progress made by the Kyrgyz economy in 2021 when annual GDP growth was 3.6 percent. The economy is now projected to contract by 2 percent in 2022, while average inflation is likely to exceed 15 percent, creating significant further pressure on fiscal and debt management as well as pushing more Page 7 of 61 The World Bank Strengthening Social Assistance and Labor Market Programs Project (P179024) people into poverty. The fiscal deficit is expected to widen to about 5 percent of GDP in 2022 as the Government increases spending to offset domestic spillovers from the decline in the Russian economy. Remittances, a major resource for the poor and vulnerable, have started to significantly decline. Recent data from the Listening to the Citizens of the Kyrgyz Republic show that many migrants delayed remittances due to the unfavorable exchange rates: during March-April 2022, the share of households receiving remittance fell by 16 percent, and the value of a typical transfer fell by 15 percent. In addition, the share of households with a member considering migration fell considerably from 13 percent before the crisis to 8 percent two months after. While massive returns have not been observed, they are expected to increase in case of a further deterioration of the Russian Federation economy. These effects are likely to be exacerbated by poor domestic labor market conditions including high rates of informality, self- employment (35 percent of total employment), and limited coverage of social insurance. Further, the decline in remittances (which were equivalent to 30 percent of GDP before the COVID-19 pandemic and mostly received by poor households) combined with higher inflation will erode households’ purchasing power. In addition, domestic business reliance on the supply of external raw materials will also result in lower output and income due to the current supply constraints. 5. The economic downturn is more severely affecting the poorest and vulnerable households, requiring the urgent support of the social protection system. The economic downturn in the Russian economy is severely affecting the poor and vulnerable groups and is expected to result in a significant increase and deepening of poverty. Households with labor migrants and receiving remittances are overrepresented among the poor and vulnerable: more than 20 percent of households from the poorest quintile receive some remittance income from international migrants, compared to 7 percent and 1 percent in the top quintiles. Like the drop in remittances, the increase in food prices is already disproportionately affecting the poor and vulnerable given the importance of food consumption in their consumption patterns. As a result of these effects, according to simulations using nationally representative household survey data, the poverty rate in the country is expected to jump to close to 40 percent compared to around 20 percent before the pandemic outbreak, in the absence of mitigating measures. In such a context, social protection systems have a crucial role to play both in providing emergency support to vulnerable households as well as to assist businesses and households during the period of economic recovery. 6. The Kyrgyz Republic ranks as the third most vulnerable country to climate change in the Europe and Central Asia region, based on the simplified index of vulnerability. The country is exposed to various types of natural hazards, with high risks of earthquakes, urban floods, landslides, and wildfire and medium risks of water scarcity and extreme heat. 1 Earthquakes with a magnitude of greater than 5 on the Richter scale (M ≥ 5) occur approximately once per month. Climate change projections show that the mean annual temperature will rise by 2.7°C and the mean annual precipitation will rise by 23.6 mm in 2050, expecting flood occurrences to increase. The projected temperature increase is expected to be higher during summer months, which supports a significant wildfire that would result in both life and property loss. A significant reduction in the country’s glaciers and snowfields is likely, with severe implications for the country’s water resources. As glaciers shrink, floods will occur with greater intensity in some areas, while water scarcity will become more acute in others. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 8. Social protection is an essential sphere of public policy in the Kyrgyz Republic, and in the past few years, the Government of the Kyrgyz Republic (GoK) has undertaken serious reforms in the sector. Social protection is among the key focus areas of the National Development Program of the Kyrgyz Republic until 2026, which was recently adopted. It outlines the main priorities in the sector, such as better targeting of social protection programs to the vulnerable and the poor, increased automatization of the social protection system to improve transparency and efficiency of social spending, digital skills development, and development of a comprehensive case management approach to social protection and specifically social services. Based on this development program, the GoK, with the support of development partners, is currently developing a Social Protection Sectoral Development Framework to be adopted in 2023. 9. Existing programs in the country span the three pillars of the social protection system—social assistance, labor markets, and social insurance—and the institutional setup is well placed to ensure effective service delivery. The priority groups targeted by existing social protection programs in the country include children and families in difficult life circumstances, people with disabilities, the elderly, and the unemployed. The main pillars of social protection include (a) social assistance programs and social care services, at the core of which is the Uy-bulogo komok 4 (or UBK) program, a monthly child grant for poor families; (b) labor market programs such as public works, vocational training, and unemployment assistance; and (c) social insurance programs including contributory pensions to workers, the self- employed, and farmers. The Ministry of Labor, Social Development, and Migration (MLSDM) oversees the implementation of both social assistance as well as labor market programs and is therefore well placed to ensure efficient service delivery and support graduation of social assistance beneficiaries into the labor market. The main social assistance program in the country—the UBK—had suffered over the years from chronic underfunding and as a result a significant exclusion of the poor. The current system of social assistance consists of several programs including, in addition to the UBK, a lump-sum birth grant, categorical monthly social benefits (targeting families with disabled members), energy subsidies, and compensations for vulnerable groups. The UBK is the largest social assistance program, providing monthly transfers to children living in poor families whose per capita income is below the Guaranteed Minimum Income (GMI) of KGS 1,000 per month per household member. Even though the program has expanded coverage over the last few years, and it is effective in reaching the poor and distributing most of the benefits among the bottom 40 percent of the population (Figure 1), its contribution to poverty reduction and the poverty gap has been low (1.2 percentage points and 0.3 percentage points, respectively). The main reasons preventing a higher contribution of the UBK to poverty reduction are related to insufficient coverage of the poorest, exclusion errors, and, until very recently, low benefit adequacy. Overall coverage remains low at 8.5 percent of the population (significantly less than the poverty rate) implying the exclusion of a significant part of the poor population. This is in part due to limited budgetary resources dedicated to the program and a very low-income eligibility threshold. These characteristics leave the poorest, including those supported by the UBK, highly exposed to future shocks and in a situation in which leaving poverty is difficult. This is exacerbated by the fact that households have already been severely affected by the COVID-19 pandemic without a social protection response, which makes them particularly vulnerable to the negative spillovers of the economic decline in the Russian economy. Labor Markets 12. The Kyrgyz Republic has a large working-age population but is faced with multiple challenges in labor markets, including high rates of unemployment among youth, informality, and vulnerabilities among workers who migrate overseas. The country’s population is young, with close to one-third of prime working age (29–59 years). However, official unemployment remains high and steadily close to 7.2 percent for the overall population and over 18 percent for the youth. Job creation, especially in the formal sector, is limited and has not been able to keep pace with the new entries of youth into the labor market, who are frequently unemployed. For example, only 6 percent of the 88,000 jobs created in the country in 2016 were in the formal sector. This results in high rates of informal employment, characterized by low incomes and limited social insurance. Given the slow, insufficient dynamism in domestic job creation, labor migration of youth is widespread, with 15–20 percent of the population migrating to Russia. While this generates large amounts of remittances, which as a proportion of GDP is among the highest in the world, it also leaves migrants and recipient households vulnerable to external shocks, as evidenced by the COVID-19 pandemic and now the Russian economic decline. 7 As a result, there is a critical need for policies aimed at promoting inclusive job creation to absorb the productive potential of the workforce. 13. The GoK has introduced multiple programs as well as the necessary legislative, regulatory, and institutional frameworks for the implementation of the active labor market programs (ALMPs), but there remain significant challenges in design and delivery. The MLSDM is responsible for overseeing programs aimed at activating the workforce. Local offices of the MLSDM are responsible for implementation at the district level, including registering job seekers, organizing public works, referring job seekers to professional training or retraining courses, and so on. The main ALMPs are public works and small-scale vocational training programs that aim to activate vulnerable groups such as the low- skilled, youth, and people with special needs to promote graduation out of social assistance. As in other Central Asia countries, participation of women in the labor market in the Kyrgyz Republic has been impeded not only by social norms and childcare responsibilities, but also by economic and labor market policies. More than half of men and more than a third of women in the region believe that when jobs are scarce, men should have more right to a job than women. 8 The role of women as caregivers—with 2.5 to 4 times more time spent doing non-paid work than men in Central Asia—limits their access to gainful employment and impacts the types of jobs they seek. 9 Gender disparities in agricultural employment are also associated with gender gaps in land ownership. There are also legal barriers for women’s access to the formal economy, including discriminatory provisions and a list of occupations from which women are banned. 10 The Kyrgyz Republic has lower scores than the average in the Europe and Central Asia region in terms of legal protection of women, 11 including laws related to pay gaps, childcare provision, and sexual harassment at work. Women have reduced networks 12 and challenges in accessing credit, 13 thereby limiting the establishment of women-owned businesses. Social Insurance 15. The pension system is the main pillar of social insurance in the Kyrgyz Republic. The system comprises a basic pension, a legacy pay-as-you-go pension, a notional defined contribution, and a funded defined contribution component. The Social Fund, which is the executive body of the system of the national social insurance and provision of pensions in the Kyrgyz Republic, manages the system. Those who do not qualify for an insurance pension receive a social pension. Overall, the system provides close to universal coverage for old age. Though about 90 percent of the old-age population is now receiving labor pensions, only about 30 percent of the working-age population is accruing pension rights at any given point of time, increasing the risk of poverty among the elderly in the future. At the same time, the Government is experimenting with mechanisms of expanding labor coverage, including recent unified collection of pension contributions with taxes under the Tax Administration. 16. In sum, the spillovers of the Russian economic decline are expected to further depress domestic job creation as well as limit employment opportunities in Russia in a context of limited capacity of public employment. A combination of factors including reduced demand and inflationary pressures is expected to result in job losses, a drop of earnings of individuals that remain employed, and further difficulties in finding a first employment among youth. In addition to the urgent increased support through social assistance to the poorest and the most vulnerable, there is also an urgent need to develop, strengthen, and scale up effective programs to provide support and assistance to workers most vulnerable to the negative impacts of the crisis and to strengthen public employment services, both as a crisis response and as a matter of capacity building to improve response to idiosyncratic and future systemic shocks. B. Project Components 21. The project will achieve its development objective on the basis of three components intended to (a) mitigate the immediate emergency caused by the Russian economic decline and affecting the purchasing power of households; (b) support poor households, the difficult-to-employ groups, and prospective and returning migrants with jobs and income generation opportunities; and (c) promote structural interventions to strengthen the social protection delivery systems to better support the target population and more efficiently and effectively cope with future crises affecting the income of poor and vulnerable households. This component supports the first part of the PDO by (a) providing emergency support and ensuring that the UBK social benefits are paid on time to the households whose incomes are particularly affected by the negative economic impacts caused by the economic crisis in Russia and (b) strengthening Page 14 of 61 The World Bank Strengthening Social Assistance and Labor Market Programs Project (P179024) the effectiveness of the UBK program to support the poor and vulnerable during the shock and assist them to be better prepared for future shocks. To achieve these goals, this component will use the country’s existing systems to deliver the increased benefit of the main antipoverty program for families with children (UBK). The project will also support the Government in improving the effectiveness of the UBK program by developing, piloting, and evaluating the improved eligibility criteria and beneficiary selection methodology for the UBK program to reduce exclusion and inclusion errors. Subcomponent 1.1: Enhanced Support to Extremely Poor Families (US$17.5 million) 23. This subcomponent aims to support the Government’s efforts to assist poor groups of population whose income has decreased because of the spillover effects of the economic crisis in Russia on the Kyrgyz economy. The decrease in remittances from the migrants in Russia as well as the general inflation levels call for the urgent increase of the social assistance benefits to compensate for lower purchasing power. The poorest and the most vulnerable households in the country are the most exposed to the negative impact of higher prices of food, fuel, and other basic goods as well as lower remittances caused by the deterioration of the Russian economy. To mitigate the impact of the shock among the poorest and the most vulnerable segments of the population, a Presidential resolution approved on May 14, 2022, increased the size of the main social benefits in the country effective June 1, 2022, including the UBK program. The monthly payment for families with children provided by the UBK was increased by 50 percent from KGS 810 to KGS 1,200 per child in eligible families, supporting about 360,000 children in 110,000 families. Therefore, this subcomponent will provide temporary emergency support to ensure that the Government can transfer the increased benefit to eligible households on time, including remote areas coefficients and corresponding bank transfer fees. Implementation at the subnational level (identification and selection of beneficiaries) will remain the responsibility of the MLSDM district (rayon) branches. Subcomponent 2.1: Supporting Graduation from Social Assistance through Economic Inclusion Opportunities (US$4 million) 29. The objective of this subcomponent is to facilitate the transition of UBK beneficiaries to IGAs with an emphasis on rural areas beneficiaries, through the implementation of a productive inclusion intervention in the Kyrgyz Republic called a ‘Social Contract’ program. To complement the targeting improvement of the UBK program and the increase in the amount of the UBK benefits, the proposed project will support the Government in developing and piloting a graduation strategy of the UBK program with the ultimate goal of assisting beneficiaries supported by social assistance toward sustainable income- generating opportunities. The strategy, based on combining the UBK payments with participation in the Social Contract intervention, is expected to ensure that eligible poor and vulnerable households are supported to leave assistance programs by offering them tailored technical support and sustainable and efficient economic inclusion options consistent with their needs and capacities. D. Results Chain 49. The proposed project is designed to address several challenges the GoK is facing. In the short term, the economic consequences of the economic downturn, caused by the Russian economic decline, will be felt most severely by the poor and vulnerable households. The main impacts will be in the form of higher prices of food, fuel, and other basic goods and lower remittances, both of which will affect mostly poor and vulnerable families’ purchasing power and household income. Growing food prices will result in a considerable proportion of the population falling deeper into poverty and requiring an urgent benefit- level adjustment. Therefore, in the short term, the project aims to support the benefit-level adjustment while in the medium term, it will help improve targeting of the program to ensure lower exclusion errors as well as develop a productive inclusion/graduation element to the program for the program beneficiaries. In the medium term, the project will support the GoK in strengthening ALMPs by improving the data and information/links with the labor market and by developing and piloting a youth employment program to help vulnerable youth find first jobs and get relevant professional experience. Finally, the social protection delivery system still has scope for improvement to increase the efficiency of services provided to the population, especially concerning the provision of the ALMP measures. 50. Medium-term measures include the implementation of activation programs targeted to the poor, vulnerable, and difficult-to-employ population. These interventions are expected to support the families and young workers to enter the labor market, exit social assistance, and provide jobs and income- generating opportunities. These interventions, along with the operational tools the project will strengthen, are to improve the existing instruments and equip the Government with new ones to help prepare for future crises that might affect the incomes of vulnerable people. E. Rationale for Bank Involvement and Role of Partners 51. The World Bank has collaborated with the GoK in the area of social protection through operational and analytical work. Over the last few years, the World Bank has collaborated with the GoK to improve the effectiveness of social protection interventions in the country in areas in which the World Bank has acquired clear comparative advantage and can provide tailored assistance based on relevant international best practices. Recently, such collaboration has been supported by two lending projects— the Kyrgyz Second Health and Social Protection Project (P126278) and the Electricity Sector Modernization and Sustainability Project (P177871)—and by the findings of two advisory services and analytics activities—Leveraging Modernization of Delivery Mechanisms to Strengthen Safety Nets (P172526) and Policy Options to Reduce the Vulnerability of Labor Migrants during COVID-19 and Beyond (175869), which directly fed into the design of this project. The World Bank is in a unique position to support the GoK in the next step of such collaboration. Given a holistic approach, the World Bank can combine the consolidation of a relevant and sustainable social protection system with the more immediate needs arising from the crisis—all while offering relevant expertise and international lessons acquired from various areas of social protection. 52. There is a need to implement rapid and tailored measures to help mitigate the negative impacts of the economic downturn to the incomes and employment of poor and vulnerable groups and labor migrants. Given the existing circumstances, the rationale of the World Bank supporting the Kyrgyz Republic is based on natural continuation of the collaborative work, so far achieved, to substantially improve the social protection system and ensuring that interventions and programs in place support those poor and vulnerable groups particularly affected by the crisis to maintain their purchasing power and Page 26 of 61 The World Bank Strengthening Social Assistance and Labor Market Programs Project (P179024) smooth consumption during the crisis. By addressing both areas, the World Bank is also in an advantageous position to support the Government with substantial value added to mitigate the impact of the crisis while continuing the process of strengthening the social protection delivery systems. 53. The World Bank aims to foster collaboration with other development partners to support the emergency and strengthen social protection delivery systems. The World Bank team is well positioned within the group of development partners supporting the GoK in the area of social protection to contribute to a harmonized approach to mitigating the crisis’ impact on households’ income. In this regard, the World Bank is closely coordinating the proposed project activities with other international organizations including key United Nations (UN) agencies (ILO, the United Nations Development Programme, the United Nations Children’s Fund [UNICEF], and WFP); bilateral donors (for example, the Swiss Agency for Development and Cooperation, the United Kingdom’s Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO), and the European Union); and other multilateral organizations (the Asian Development Bank [ADB]) to provide complementary assistance through different mechanisms to ensure well-coordinated government support. Lessons Learned and Reflected in the Project Design 54. Measures to alleviate the impact of shocks and increase social benefits permanently are urgently needed but should be complemented with interventions to support vulnerable groups beyond the immediate emergency because the economy’s decline might persist, and therefore, medium-term investments in human capital and social protection system are essential. The additional support to the most affected groups of the population, particularly those below the poverty line, is paramount for a successful intervention to help people manage the impact of declined purchasing power of low-income groups. As the Government rightly decided to increase the main antipoverty social benefit permanently, there is need to complement this measure with interventions to promote activation of the poor and vulnerable and a strategy to sustainably support them in exiting social assistance and (re)entering the labor market. The proposed project follows this approach by complementing the emergency measures with medium-term support to the beneficiaries of the UBK program for them to engage in IGAs, for vulnerable youth to increase employment chances, and long-term investments to improve the efficiency of the social assistance and labor market programs. 55. Emergency measures should focus on protecting the welfare of the poorest. Once the crisis has affected the incomes and welfare of the poor and vulnerable, the risk of further falling into poverty and adopting negative coping mechanisms to at least partially smooth consumption is high. As some preliminary data show, in the Kyrgyz Republic, the affected poor households are taking measures to mitigate impacts that may be costly in the long term, including borrowing money; depleting their savings; and cutting expenditures in key areas such as health, education, and nutrition. These measures may mitigate some of the impacts in the short term; however, it will be substantially more difficult and costly to help households that relied on such measures to return to the precrisis situation and eventually leave poverty. Essentially, it would be much less expensive and more effective to provide timely and sufficient support to such families to prevent them from falling into poverty in the first place. Therefore, the Page 27 of 61 The World Bank Strengthening Social Assistance and Labor Market Programs Project (P179024) proposed project is supporting measures that support low-income families to smooth consumption and minimize taking negative coping strategies. 56. Using existing country systems is the best option to efficiently transfer assistance in the short term. Delaying the implementation of emergency measures (such as the increase of the monthly benefit for poor families) may lead to a long-term negative impact in terms of poverty and vulnerability. A key element of the emergency support is the efficient and timely transfer of cash assistance to the most needy households and individuals. The availability of mechanisms to identify and select beneficiaries and reliable channels, procedures, and controls to disburse funds to recipient households is crucial for the intervention’s success. The Kyrgyz Republic has a system to select beneficiaries which has proven to be effective in reaching the poorest as well as mechanisms to transfer allowances in a safe manner. Therefore, the proposed project is addressing the risk of delaying timely support to low-income families by supporting the Government to start paying the additional monthly benefit even under budget constraints by including retroactive financing and using the UBK delivery systems to make the additional payments. 57. Medium-term reforms to improve long-term effectiveness and shock-responsiveness of the social protection system are needed. Even under conditions that require emergency support to low- income families, building solid operational blocks to better support the poor and vulnerable and promote self-sustainability and graduation from social assistance through effective activation measures is the best option to strengthen the capacity of social protection delivery system. The emergency period should also serve as a vehicle to introduce necessary adjustments to improve the efficiency of social protection delivery systems to be ready to effectively support the most vulnerable in future shocks and economic slowdowns. Following this approach, the proposed project will contribute to the Government’s efforts to be better equipped to mitigate and assist exposed populations to cope with future shocks affecting the incomes and welfare of the poor and vulnerable groups. The overall residual risk of the project after mitigation measures is rated Substantial. The proposed overall project risk rating is based on the Substantial residual political and governance, macroeconomic, technical design of the project, and fiduciary risks, after considering specific planned and actual mitigation measures discussed below. 86. Residual political and governance risk is rated Substantial. One of the main risks the project faces is related to political and governance issues, which is rated high before mitigation measures. The main reason for this rating is the diverse views from involved and relevant actors regarding the options the country should follow to support the poorest and the most vulnerable to address the risks of shocks. While an agreement between relevant counterparts was reached during the preparation of the project, the experience of the last few years shows that the Government’s perspectives and priorities may change rapidly, which eventually may jeopardize project implementation and the achievement of the PDO within the projected time frame. The main mitigation measure for this risk has been the active involvement of key counterparts during preparation. Particularly, the World Bank has proactively involved the MoF to participate in the preparation and ensure their agreement in all areas of the project scope. Further mitigation measures include ongoing policy dialogue and involvement of the MoF and the Presidential Administration Office, particularly during the period of ratification and also during project implementation. As a result of these mitigation measures, the residual risk is Substantial. 87. Residual macroeconomic risk is Substantial. The economic impact caused by the deterioration of the Russian economy has been significant, while the country is still recovering from the shock resulting from the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, the inherent macroeconomic risk is rated High. The Kyrgyz Republic’s economy is being particularly affected by higher prices of food and basic goods as well as the sharp decline in remittances (which may represent up to 30 percent of the GDP). Furthermore, as the crisis deepens, the Government may increasingly face a substantial macroeconomic deterioration and further impacts in terms of financing social protection and activation programs. Budget and fiscal constraints may lead the Government to shift priorities, jeopardizing the necessary budget allocation for the interventions supported by the project and therefore the achievement of the PDO within the proposed time frame. In this context, the project is designed to provide temporary funding to ensure that the poorest and the most vulnerable are timely supported with adequate benefits, while promoting a sound recovery through activation interventions. Furthermore, the disbursement modalities can also help mitigate the risk of delaying budgetary allocation, as the Government is already paying the increased transfers which will be reimbursed through both retroactive financing and recurrent disbursement conditions during the first stages of implementation. Furthermore, there is a broad consensus on the need to provide measures to actively support the population to be engaged in IGAs and thus reduce the fiscal pressure on the social assistance budget. Therefore, the residual risk is Substantial. 88. Residual sector strategies and policies risk is rated Moderate. In the recent past, the GoK has shown some level of volatility regarding sector strategies and priorities in social protection, which, if continued, may delay the implementation of the project and therefore the inherent risk is rated Substantial. Even though the MLSDM has shown a clear consistency regarding social protection priorities and the goal to modernize the social protection delivery systems in the country, the overall Government policy agenda, with particular emphasis on social sectors, is less steady. In the past, other key actors within Page 36 of 61 The World Bank Strengthening Social Assistance and Labor Market Programs Project (P179024) the Central Government structure have intervened and changed the direction of social protection policies and strategies. To mitigate this risk, the preparation of the project has been carefully consulted with key counterparts beyond the MLSDM to enhance consensus around the actions and investments supported by the operation. During the project implementation, the team will intensify the policy dialogue and ensure that all relevant actors including other international agencies are involved and well informed. As a result, the residual risk after the mitigation measures is rated Moderate. 89. Residual risk of technical design of the project remains Substantial. Component 2 of the project includes two activation interventions that, despite having promising results at the pilot stage, could bring up some risks when being scaled up nationwide; therefore, the inherent risk of technical design of the project is rated Substantial. This rating is based on the fact that both programs require a combination of different interventions to effectively support beneficiaries (for example, skills development with technical support and financial assistance in the case of the Social Contract and training, life skills, and coordination with public and private firms in the case of youth employment). Even though the ministry has tested both interventions, they are small pilots that can be relatively well controlled, while scaling them up will require additional design and implementation considerations, a careful process to select beneficiaries, additional complementary measures, and an increasing role of public agencies at the local level. Failure in any of these areas may cause delays in the implementation of the programs and the achievement of the PDO on time. To mitigate this risk, the Government and the World Bank will carefully define all necessary parameters of these programs and institutional and implementation arrangements before starting their implementation to ensure that beneficiaries have a higher probability of success and that the interventions are sustainable in the medium term. Even though mitigation measures will reduce the likelihood that this risk will jeopardize the achievement of the PDO within the projected time frame, the residual risk is still Substantial. 90. Residual fiduciary risk is rated Substantial. FM and procurement risks and mitigation measures are discussed in detail in annex 1. The lack of experienced FM staff assigned to the project as well as complex implementation arrangements and flow of funds might pose some implementation risks, which would be mitigated by having an action plan in place to ensure adequate project FM arrangements. Potential procurement risks identified are low procurement capacity, procurement delays, and low- quality contract management. To mitigate the project’s substantial fiduciary risk, (a) the World Bank team will provide targeted trainings in World Bank policies and procedures, as well as in contract management to the PIU’s procurement and FM specialists before implementation and as needed during the project’s lifetime to increase capacity, (b) frequent and ongoing technical and fiduciary oversight will be provided by the World Bank’s FM and procurement specialists based in Bishkek, (c) annual audit of the PFS will be conducted by independent auditors acceptable to the World Bank. Regular full team missions will occur twice a year, but just-in-time assistance from the World Bank’s fiduciary team is available to the PIU staff. Project Institutional and Implementation Arrangement 1. The implementing agency for the proposed project will be the MLSDM. It is the central agency responsible for social assistance and labor program policies as well as the migration interventions in the Kyrgyz Republic. The MLSDM at the central level has a Department of Employment and Labor and a Department of Social Assistance Payments. At the rayon level, the MLSDM branches have representatives of these departments that are charged with social protection program implementation, specifically in the case of this project’s UBK and employment programs implementation. Therefore, at the rayon level the employment department plays the role of the employment support center, while in Bishkek and Osh only, the employment support center is a stand-alone agency. Regional training centers exist in the regional capitals and are under the responsibility of the MLSDM as well. Professional trainings are organized either at these centers or through private training-providing companies through tenders. Direct implementation of social protection programs is carried out at the district and local levels by the regional MLSDM branches. Therefore, the implementation of the UBK, the Social Contract, and the youth employment, as well as other labor market programs, are under its domain. The MLSDM has implemented other World Bank- financed projects in the past and has been assessed by World Bank fiduciary specialists as having the capacity to manage the proposed project with the support of a PIU. 2. To support the implementation of the project, a PIU will be established in the MLSDM under the direct supervision of a deputy minister. The PIU will support the MLSDM at the central and subnational levels in implementing project activities and will be responsible for the fiduciary as well as social and environmental aspects of the project. The PIU will comprise at least a project coordinator and M&E, procurement, FM, and social and communication specialists responsible for the relevant activities under the three components of the project. 3. The implementation of the UBK benefits under subcomponent 1.1 will follow the existing arrangements within the MLSDM and relevant subnational authorities. The MLSDM will maintain the same parameters and procedures for the registration and payment of the additional benefit for existing and new beneficiaries of the UBK. Therefore, the registration and assessment of new beneficiaries will take place through the existing procedures; that is, the implementation of the program (identification and selection of beneficiaries) will remain under the responsibility of the MLSDM district (rayon) branches. Payments will continue under the responsibility of the post offices. Implementation of the benefits under subcomponent 1.2 will also be under the responsibility of the MLSDM, registration and assessment of new beneficiaries will take place through the newly developed procedures (subject to the SCSM approval by the World Bank), and the implementation of the program (identification and selection of beneficiaries) will remain under the responsibility of the MLSDM district (rayon) branches and will follow the existing procedures. Support to ensure that increased UBK benefits are timely transferred to beneficiaries will be carried out on a reimbursable basis using recurrent disbursement conditions. A simple methodology can be used to identify household and individual poverty. The 2020 Kyrgyz Integrated Household Survey (KIHS) microdata include 4,993 individual household observations for a wide array of variables. The 2020 KIHS is used to assess poverty levels in the country. The methodology to identify the share of households and individuals living under poverty includes the following: (a) Obtain per capita income values. Using the total income (toty) variable, which is presented at the household level, per capita values are obtained by dividing toty by household size (hsize). (b) Obtain per capita income values net of transfers. It is necessary to subtract the value of transfers from the per capita values. Transfers include scholarships (y3), alimony (y5), social insurance benefits (y41), monthly social benefits (y42), lump-sum benefits (y43), unified monthly benefit for poor households and individuals (y44), unemployment benefit (y45), and other benefits (y46). (c) Apply survey weights. Using the weights variable in the survey, apply the corresponding weights to the net per capita income values. The resulting values will better reflect the survey composition and the poor and vulnerable groups of the population (Figure 2.1). 24 24Figure 2.1 shows that higher incomes in the distribution are farther away from the 45-degree line. It follows that incomes net of transfers for each point in the income distribution are lower—and increasingly so—than the total incomes.
