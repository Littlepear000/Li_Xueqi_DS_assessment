Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Ghana has experienced steady economic growth averaging 3 percent annually in the last two decades. This led to a near doubling of GDP per capita putting Ghana in the top ten fastest-growing economies and lifting it to lower middle-income status (LMIS) in 2011. In recent years, however, Ghana's economy has been going through cycles of fiscal pressures and heightened vulnerability exacerbated by a weak fiscal stance and significant energy sector shortfalls. In 2022, following the COVID-19 pandemic, Ghana experienced a macroeconomic crisis an unsustainable fiscal stance, and public debt. Inflation rose to over 50 percent, with the balance of payments facing acute pressure, and GDP growth falling (from 5.4 percent in 2021 to 2.9 percent in 2023). Inflation continues to hover above 22 percent in June 2024 and the burden of the economic crisis (high inflation and low growth) has fallen on the vulnerable segments of the population. It is estimated to have pushed over 800,000 Ghanaians into poverty. 2. Restoring fiscal balance, debt sustainability, and long-term growth are imperative priorities for the government under the IMF three-year extended credit facility (ECF) program. The program which emphasizes frontloaded fiscal consolidation and tight monetary and exchange rate policies and structural reforms of tax policy, revenue administration, and financial management, will help control inflation and rebuild fiscal and external buffers. Under the program steps are being taken to address the weaknesses of the cocoa and energy sectors. In addition, the three-year First Resilient Recovery Development Policy Financing (DPF) (P180718) approved by the World Bank in FY24 for an amount of US$900 million, has set Ghana on the path to recovery. The DPF includes major reforms for strengthening macroeconomic stability, and for protecting critical social sector financing, including the education sector. 3. Ghana’s human capital development outcomes are below its peer income grouping, but above the average for Sub - Saharan Africa. With a Human Capital Index (HCI) score of 45 percent, Ghana is not efficiently mobilizing and using its human capital potential for economic development. A child born in Ghana today will reach less than half her productive potential as an adult compared to if she enjoyed complete education and full health. Ghana’s relatively low HCI is largely driven by its low learning outcomes measured by harmonized test scores (HTS) and limits the effectiveness of the budget allocations to the sector. In 2023, the percentage of GDP to the education sector was 3.5 percent compared to the UN benchmark of 4-6 percent. Sectoral and Institutional Context 4. Ghana's education system spans from basic to tertiary levels, including non-formal education, with a strong commitment to early childhood development as outlined in the Early Childhood Care and Development (ECCD) Policy. The Ministry of Education steers the sector with its Education Sector Plan, and the Ghana Education Service and Ghana Tertiary Page 3 The World Bank Ghana Accountability for Learning Outcomes Project (P165557) – Additional Financing Request 3 Education Commission oversee pre-tertiary and tertiary education, respectively. Technical and vocational education is managed by the Ghana TVET service and the Council for Technical and Vocational Education and Training. The NTC is mandated to license and register teachers, regulate institutions offering teacher education, oversee continuing professional development for teachers and ensure compliance with the National Teachers’ Standards. The NaSIA is responsible for providing independent external evaluation of quality and standards in both public and private schools, while NaCCA has overall responsibility for curriculum development and assessment. Other agencies under the MoE that will support the proposed AF are the Center for Distance Learning and Open Schooling (CENDLOS), the Complementary Education Agency (CEA), the National Service Scheme (NSS) and the National Education Leadership Institute (NELI). 5. Ghana has made significant progress on improving access to education in the decade from 2000 to 2022. Gross enrollment rate (GER) has improved significantly at the kindergarten (KG) and primary levels from 60 percent to 122 percent and from 83 percent to 107 percent, respectively. The government's flagship Free Secondary Education Policy rolled out in 2017, is associated with a 28 percent increase in the number of students enrolled at higher secondary level between 2017 and 2023. Gross enrolment increased from 55.9 percent in 2017 to 60.7 percent in 2023. Through the IDA financed Ghana Accountability for Learning Outcomes Project (GALOP) the MoE has implemented a wide variety of reforms at scale. These achievements include, implementing differentiated learning approach, supporting teachers with a coaching and mentoring program, and providing grants into over 10,000 of the lowest performing schools specifically to improve learning. The GALOP has also supported the development of an accountability for learning framework and dashboard which provides decision makers with relevant data for accountable decision-making, and the conduct of two rounds of the National Standardized Test (NST) for all P2 and P4 learners in public basic education schools across the country. 6. Despite improvements in access over the past two decades many learners fall below proficiency levels across most regions, and assessment of Socio-emotional learning is limited. Only about half of assessed learners in P4 meet the minimum competency benchmarks (51.3 percent for English and 49 percent for mathematics) according to the National Standardized Test (2022). The NST results for P4 reveals significant regional disparities in learners’ English language proficiency ranging from 21 percent of learners in the Ahafo region with advanced proficiency, to 97 percent of learners in Volta region with below basic proficiency. For both English and Mathematics, all 16 regions had less than 25 percent of P4 learners registering less than 25 percent with advanced proficiency. For mathematics, the Upper West had 88 percent of their P4 learners below basic proficiency compared to 36 percent in the Northern region. There is limited focus on measurement of socio-emotional learning. 7. There are various factors contributing to low learning outcomes in the country. These include challenges in teacher deployment, particularly in rural northern districts, and a decline in education expenditure for basic education. Gender parity has been achieved at lower and secondary levels, but regional disparities call for additional efforts. Social inclusion issues, such as enrolling children from poor backgrounds and with disabilities, remain, as does the impact of child labor on education. Finally, strengthening education management and accountability is essential to address issues like teacher absenteeism and to improve decision-making processes. 8. The Government’s response to the education sector challenges has been guided by the government’s strategic framework for education as outlined in its Education Strategic Plan (2018 to 2030) and the medium-term national development plan on “Coordinated Program of Economic and Social Development Policies (CPESDP) 2017–2024�?. Education is a major national priority as stated in the medium-term development plan and Education Strategic Plan. The Government’s commitment to addressing Ghana’s access to quality early childhood education and the learning crises is visible through the implementation of several reforms including the expansion of free basic education to include the kindergarten, the set-up of the NTC to strengthen teachers’ capacity in a systematic approach. The National Schools Inspectorate Authority Page 4 The World Bank Ghana Accountability for Learning Outcomes Project (P165557) – Additional Financing Request 3 (NaSIA) was set-up to strengthen independent periodic external evaluation of quality and standards in both public and private schools among others. Further, the launch of the GALOP is aimed at addressing challenges of learning poverty challenges at the school and community levels. The overarching goal of the ESP 2018–2030 is to improve the quality of education for all. The ESP lays out core education priorities of improved equitable access to and participation in inclusive education at all levels, improved quality of teaching and learning and STEM at all levels and sustainable and efficient management, financing, and accountability of education service delivery. Project Scope 9. The proposed second Additional Financing (AF) will scale up successful interventions of the parent GALOP project, and introduce new activities to maximize its development impact considering the extent of challenges in foundational learning. The proposed second AF and restructuring are in response to a request from the Government of Ghana to: (a) revise GALOP PBCs and PDO indicators; (b) extend the closing dates for both the parent GALOP, and the GPRBA funded Education Outcomes Fund (EOF), to December 31, 2026; (c) scale up support to the education sector to finance effective activities aligned with the Ghana’s Partnership COMPACT objective of improving foundational learning for all; and (d) introduce a childcare intervention in project communities to promote early stimulation for foundational learning and to empower girls and women. The proposed AF will revise two existing PDO level indicators: PDO 3a Increased percentage of targeted KG with PTTR below 50:1 to reflect the revised baseline and target, and PDO 2 students benefiting from direct project interventions to enhance learning, to align with the new corporate score card indicator. One new PDO indicator to assess learning outcomes to reflect the increased ambition of the project, in terms of increasing the quality of learning, will also be added. The restructuring would revise four PBCs to reallocate undisbursed funds linked to unmet PBCs (PBCs 1.3, 2.2, 3.2 and 3.3) and to revise the baseline and target related to PBC 5. It is proposed that the GPRBA financing closing date will be extended by one year (from December 31, 2025, to December 31, 2026) to provide sufficient time to assess learning gains and retention of the GEOP intervention, which was initially delayed. It is also proposed that the closing date of the parent GALOP will be extended by two years (from December 31, 2024, to December 31, 2026) to align the two closing dates. 10. An additional US$ 7.4 million grant from the Early Learning Partnership (ELP) Multi donor trust fund (MDTF) will be processed to align with the proposed GALOP AF and will support the scale-up of interventions and the introduction of new activities aimed at improving foundational learning in Ghana. Aligned with Ghana’s Partnership Compact vision of ensuring foundational learning for all, the proposed AF will have a particular focus on girls, children involved in child labor and learners with disabilities. The AF will support the training of teachers on gender sensitive pedagogies and support the GBV prevention and mitigation efforts through district level incentives. Complementary ongoing virtual trainings would also be incorporated to deepen the teacher’s understanding of concepts. The teaching pedagogies will be further deepened under the proposed program with stronger focus on implementation fidelity, digitized monitoring of individual learners by teachers in the classroom, effective supervision and coaching by SISOs for improved teaching practices in primary classrooms which will be measured by the NaSIA. 14. Sub-component 2.1: Learning grants to support activities focused on learning The AF will support the provision of inflation-adjusted enhanced learning grants for 5,500 AF schools. These grants will incentivize schools to tackle learning challenges in their schools by introducing funding conditions. Schools will receive initial advances of learning grants comprising a base grant and per capita (depending on the number of students enrolled) which will be safeguard against inflation. During the first year, there will be no pre-requisites for schools to receive learning grants which will fund activities agreed with SMCs under their School Performance Improvement Plans (SPIPs). There will however be the financial management requirements of a functioning bank account and approved signatories for the funds. In subsequent years however, beneficiary schools will be required to demonstrate the following actions have been met in the preceding year: (i) regular DL learner assessments (literacy and numeracy performance of learners) have been undertaken and reported on the DL+ platform; (ii) submitted mSRC data for three (3) terms; (iii) publicly displayed the latest NST assessment results in the school (e.g. on school notice board); (iv) ensured good record-keeping on enrollment and attendance with records; (v) prepared SPIP in collaboration with SMCs; (vi) followed-up on learners who are at risk of dropping out of school and/or involved in child labor and/or at risk of being in a child marriage; (vii) reported on the GRM and following up on any GBV issues, and (viii) school is compliant with agreed climate smart guidelines. The climate smart guidelines will be identified in the PIM and would encourage schools to engage in environmentally friendly-waste disposal, establish environment clubs in schools, and promote tree-planting and create awareness about the effects of deforestation. 23. Sub-component 3.4: Support to Communities of Excellence (CoEx) to develop localized solutions for improved learning outcomes. To strengthen accountability of education officials to the communities, the government has approved a Communities of Excellence strategy to strengthen accountability of education delivery at the community level. CoEx will be selected from the 100 beneficiary districts with a high concentration of GALOP and AF schools (signifying low learning levels) and with a high incidence of child labor, OOSC (based on population census data 2021), with special consideration for communities in cocoa growing areas. It is expected that each district will have a minimum of 20 CoEx to implement transformational activities for improved foundational learning. CoEx will receive financing through their districts with an overall aim of ‘building a network of highly literate communities across Ghana’. The government approved its communities of excellence strategy in 2023 with 2 key objectives (i) At least 90 percent of P4 pupils within the Communities of Excellence Program are reading and writing at grade-level proficiency (ii) 80 percent of the communities are actively participating in education service delivery across all schools. Key activities under the communities of excellence will include the following: (i) stakeholder engagement to identify learning bottlenecks in primary schools in the community; (ii) develop a targeted and prioritized strategy for addressing identified challenges; (iii) support from district officials in building capacity of teachers to effectively deliver on teaching, and community leaders (to demand accountability from schools and implement community strategy; (iv) monitoring of implementation of community plans through quarterly monitoring. The activities to be supported within the communities of excellence will be fully aligned to the national communities of excellence strategy. Some CoEx supported under the AF will promote women participation in areas where there is low women labor force participation by ensuring access to quality childcare. 37.
