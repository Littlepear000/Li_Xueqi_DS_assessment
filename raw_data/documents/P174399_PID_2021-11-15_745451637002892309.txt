Introduction and Context Country Context 1. The Bhutanese economy exhibited consistent growth and gains in poverty reduction prior to the COVID- 19 pandemic. Much of the growth has been driven by public sector-led hydropower development and the sale of electricity to neighboring India. From 2007 through 2017, Bhutan reduced poverty by two-thirds, from 36 percent to 12 percent, based on the $3.20/day poverty line, albeit the pace has slowed down in recent years. Hydropower projects have helped the government create fiscal space for investing in human and physical capital, allowing Bhutan to significantly improve service delivery, education, and health. 2. However, dependence on hydropower and prevalence of subsistence employment in agriculture makes Bhutan’s economy highly vulnerable to macroeconomic volatility and climate shocks, and Bhutan’s private sector is yet to create competitive jobs in order to create a stable macroeconomic environment. Most of Bhutan’s productive infrastructure (hydropower plants, roads, airports), fertile agricultural land, and over 70 percent of the settlements are located along the main drainage basins, which puts them at high risk of flooding. With climate change, the frequency and intensity of extreme events is expected to increase. As a result, large parts of the population are vulnerable to natural shocks. Furthermore, a pandemic like the COVID-19 have significant impact on population as they lack transversal skills and the coping mechanisms to accommodate and adjust to crises. 3. The Royal Government of Bhutan (RGoB) has made commendable efforts to control the COVID-19 pandemic and protect its population. Implementation of a National Preparedness and Response Plan for Outbreak of Novel Coronavirus is being successfully followed, including efforts to vaccinate the entire eligible adult population within one week. This has been complimented by efforts to secure food stocks, boost domestic production and distribution of food, keep trade flows open, and provide direct cash transfers to affected populations. 4. Nevertheless, Bhutan’s economy has been significantly affected by COVID -19. Lockdowns and dependence of the Bhutanese economy on trade, tourism and remittances, created an economic contraction impacting the welfare of citizens. Activity in the service sector declined as tourist arrivals dried up, while industrial activities were also severely disrupted due to shortages in critical inputs (including foreign labor), and depressed external demand, especially from India. On the demand side, consumption and investment declined due to domestic containment measures and disruptions in public sector infrastructure projects. Services sector workers in urban areas, including many that directly or indirectly depend on tourism, have experienced job or earning losses. High food inflation has also likely eroded the real incomes of many rural poor. This is expected to lead to a 1.8 percentage point increase in poverty (measured at the $3.20 poverty rate) by the end of 2021. 5. COVID-19 losses in household welfare can further exacerbate other pre-existing inequities of opportunities. Household poverty is significantly correlated with lower educational attainment and poor access to health services. COVID-19 economic impact can exacerbate pre-existing inequities if actions to generate and diversify income of population are not in place. Without adequate safety nets, families may engage in unhealthy coping strategies such as keeping their children from school or reducing their nutritional intake, thereby decreasing chances for intergenerational income mobility. Bhutan has made significant progress over the past years on human capital, and yet the World Bank’s Human Capital Index 2020 shows that a child born today in Bhutan will be only 48 percent as productive in adulthood as she could have been with a complete education and better health care. In other words, GDP per worker could be as much as 2 times higher if the quality and quantity of human capital were improved for all. Bhutan’s score is lower than the average for the South Asia region and lower middle-income countries, due to relatively poor adult survival rate (80 percent) and learning-adjusted years of schooling (6.3 years). 7. Bhutan also continues to face challenges of unequal access to quality foundational human capital services and underutilization of acquired human capital (employment). Overall in 2019 ECCD enrollment of age 3 -5 was only 24% resulting into a missed opportunity to maximize human capital outcomes through early on interventions. Earlier Bhutan Living Standards Survey from 2017 reveals that 22% have attained primary or ECCD or daycare in urban areas as compared to only 13% in rural areas. Similarly, secondary school completion rates are 7 percentage points lower in rural areas than in urban. Furthermore, despite investment in education and skills majority of employment is in subsistence agriculture and gender disparities in employment prevail with close to 50% of employed females in 2019 being family workers in comparison to only around 20% for male workers (Bhutan Labor Force Survey 2019). 8. Despite its relatively low number of coronavirus (COVID-19) cases, Bhutan is facing both a disruption in provision of human capital services and a decline in household welfare and thus ability to invest in human capital . For example, the Government quickly established a variety of distance learning programs to be offered in a very short notice to minimize learning loss. However, limited connectivity in most of the country resulted in the government relying on mostly low technology and physical learning materials. The combined effect of COVID-19 on education, health, and household welfare could lead to an increase in poverty, stunting and food insecurity, and affect social development indicators such as educational achievement and productivity, threatening gains from previous years. To mitigate against such detrimental impacts, the RGoB launched a package of responses to the COVID-19 crisis through a process of rapid innovation that laid the operational foundations for development of a more resilient system. Two programs are especially noteworthy. 9. First, the RGoB launched the Economic Stimulus Plan (ESP) which includes fast-tracking implementation of employment creation opportunities for vulnerable workers. The ESP provides immediate income support, employment retention and reskilling, and redeployment programs (creating a bridge between response and recovery). Ministry of Labor and Human Resource (MOLHR) is a key implementing agency for this plan. 10. Second, the RGoB started to develop the New Educational Roadmap 2021-2030 (NER) to pave the way for enhancing quality and resilience of the education sector. The NER is implemented by the Ministry of Education (MoE) and identifies the expansion and quality improvement of early childhood care and development (ECCD) and the digitization of education services as critical challenges to improve human capital and build a more resilience education system. RGoB’s national plans currently envisions to expand ECE access to 50% of 3 -5 year old children by 2024 on priority basis in rural communities and other areas where there is an identified socio-economically disadvantaged population. On the digitization front, MoE has started to roll out ICT literacy curriculum in the formal education system to support children’s digital citizenship skills and competencies. Moreover, MoE plans for an effective implementation of the New Normal Curriculum (NNC) from 2021 academic session. The proposed project would support the Government of Bhutan’s national goals of improved human capital outcomes for economic productivity and growth. It would do this through links with priority areas for enhancement of the quality of public service delivery and good governance as well as with the country’s vision of creating a “just, harmonious and sustainable society through enhanced decentralization�? as presented in the country 12th Five Year Plan 2018-2023 (12th FYP). The 12th FYP also puts greater emphasis on intergovernmental coordination, consolidation, and collaboration to improve cross-sectoral programming, targeting, and the leveraging of scarce resources, as well as promotes the consolidation of actions around core flagship programs to ensure that critical issues such as youth unemployment and spatial disparities are addressed more coherently. 12. The proposed project is in line with CPF FY2021–24 (Report No. 154927-BT) and will support two of the strategic focus areas—human capital and resilience—and focuses on both the supply and the demand sides of job creation. The project will promote investments in human capital and skills that are needed to diversify and sustain Bhutan’s sources of growth and increase the productivity of the current and future workforce. Moreover, the project can also contribute to environmental resilience through support to livelihoods of low-income populations dependent on agriculture (who are highly vulnerable to natural disasters and climate change). Rationale for Bank Engagement and Choice of Financing Instrument 13. The proposed PforR operation builds on the World Bank’s ongoing engagements in the human development sector. Engagement to date has included analytical support and technical assistance aimed to: (a) improve quantity, quality and access to jobs through skilling, apprenticeship and entrepreneurship support programs; (b) strengthen service delivery and address skill mismatch through enhancing labor market information system; (c) improving access and supply of ECCD; (d) improve quality of primary, secondary and higher education through policy revisions and regulatory reforms. In addition, it will complement several operations: COVID-19 Emergency Response and Health System Preparedness project (P173787); Development Policy Financing Credit with Catastrophic Deferred Drawdown Option (CAT DDO) (P173008); COVID-19 Crisis Response Development Policy Credit (P175758), and the Additional Finance for Food Security and Agriculture Productivity (P175717). The World Bank is particularly well positioned to consolidate recommendations emerging from the diagnostics and support RGoB to tackle development challenges that require a coordinated, cross-sectoral response. 14. The PforR instrument is appropriate for the proposed operation as the Government has a well- articulated program for which the Bank’s financing can be leveraged to produce larger systemic changes. Bhutan also has sound institutions and procedures, thus the PforR instrument is an effective instrument to shift the focus from policy and program design to implementation and achievement of results. This choice of the instrument is also aligned with the recent CPF that points out that a PforR would allow more effective support for building the institutional capacity needed for policy and program implementation. In response to COVID-19, the instrument will support the Economic Stimulus Plan that includes fast-tracking implementation of the 12th FYP, with an emphasis on revitalizing the rural economy and creating employment opportunities. D. Program Description PforR Program Boundary 15. The proposed operation supports the implementation of two government programs with the goal to accelerate economic recovery and improve human capital outcomes: (i) 12th FYP; (ii) ESP; (ii) National Skills Development Program; and (ii) the New Educational Roadmap (NER). National Skills Development Plan (NSDP) that was recently approved by the Cabinet outlines provisions for the National Skill Training (NST) program that aims to support employment recovery through skilling (including on the job training) and self-employment. The RGoB also developed the NER, which includes priority areas such as the expansion of access as well as enhancement of quality of ECCD and digitization of educational service delivery. The NER aims to address challenges around quality, equity and sustainability of ECCD given low coverage of programs and challenges in delivery in remote areas. Education disruptions due to repeated waves of COVID-19 lockdowns highlighted the importance of digitization for more resilient service delivery. The digitization of education requires the effective implementation of the New Normal Curriculum (NNC) starting with the 2021 school year. The NNC builds on lessons learned during the pandemic and shifts approach from knowledge centered learning to competency-based learning through experiential learning and real time engagement with digital technology. 16. The boundaries of the Program supported by the PforR are defined by including only those expenditures that are directly related to the successful implementation of NST, ECCD expansion and curriculum digitization. Specific components from the policy lines in the Government’s program that are directly linked to the objective of implementing the two sublines are included in the definition of the Government program for this PforR. 17.
