Introduction and Context Country Context 1. While Tunisia’s economy is resilient and among the most diversified in Africa1, the country is navigating a delicate economic situation with persistently high levels of unemployment (16% in Q2 2024), particularly among youth (40%) and women (21%)2. Despite notable export gains in tourism, mechanical, and electrical sectors3, driven by high- complexity products, overall economic growth in 2023-2024 has been hindered by severe drought, tight financing conditions, and the pace of reform implementation, leaving the economy below pre-COVID levels compared to other MENA region countries. Compounding these challenges, Tunisia has increasingly relied on recurrent public expenditures to meet immediate needs, resulting in widening fiscal and current account deficits — though these are expected to improve in 2024. This has also contributed to a rising debt burden, which reached 79.8% of GDP in 20234. As of spring 2024, the budget remains under pressure with limited growth further straining public finances.5 Structural labor market issues, such as a preference for public sector jobs, skills mismatches, and low private-sector productivity, have compounded unemployment challenges. 1 African Center for Economic Transformation. In the coming decades, Tunisia, as other countries in the MENA region, will increasingly face the impact of climate change, digitalization and population aging. While these megatrends are not new, they will accelerate significantly within the century. To withstand the potential threats that arise from them and capitalize on their associated opportunities, the Tunisian economy requires a structural transformation including through changes in the nation’s academic and scientific research sectors. (a) Tunisia is highly vulnerable to climate change, 6 with frequent severe weather, rising sea levels, and ecological disruptions threatening key economic sectors like agriculture and tourism. Water shortages and temperature changes could drastically reduce crop yields (e.g., olives by 69%, wheat by 35%) by 2050, while coastal tourism faces risks from rising sea levels. Without urgent action, these challenges could shrink Tunisia's GDP by 3.4% by 2030. However, proactive measures, such as decarbonizing the electricity sector, could boost GDP by 9%, reduce poverty by 12%, and cut carbon emissions by 80% by 2030. 7 Climate change also affects education, with extreme weather disrupting learning environments, increasing school closures, and causing dropouts due to economic shocks and food insecurity. 8 Building climate resilience, particularly through diversified livelihoods and education, is critical for Tunisia’s sustainable development. (b) The rise of digital technologies is transforming Tunisia's economy, jobs, and society, with the government prioritizing innovation and digital transformation through strategies like Tunisia Digital 2020, Digital Strategy 2025, and the National AI Strategy9. Leveraging 80% internet penetration10, Tunisia has invested in initiatives such as the Higher Institute of Digital Engineering and Elghazala Technopark to advance digital skills and foster collaboration among academia, industry, and startups. Fiber optic connectivity supports nationwide digital infrastructure, enabling AI-driven solutions in education (e.g., dropout prevention), agriculture (e.g., optimized irrigation), and tourism (e.g., enhanced booking platforms). These efforts aim to position Tunisia as a leader in technology-driven economic growth and job creation (c) Tunisia is experiencing significant demographic shifts due to longer life expectancies and declining birth rates, with the highest share of individuals aged 65 and older in the MENA region. 11 The population, which grew from less than 4 million in 1950 to nearly 12 million in 2020, is projected to peak at 14 million by 2050-2065 before declining to 13 million by 2100. The elderly population is expected to triple by 2050, reaching 22% of the total and nearly 31% by 2100.12 This rapid aging trend highlights the urgent need for a strategic plan to enhance elderly care services and train multidisciplinary healthcare teams. The country has accomplished several critical successes in higher education over the past decade. Major achievements include: i) high female representation, both among students and faculty; ii) significant strides in higher education quality, as reflected in improved international rankings, accreditation16 and ISO-certifications17; iii) graduate tracer studies implemented, albeit not harmonized and underutilized; iv) a strong commitment to equity, at the regional level through opening of universities in interior regions and among students from diverse socio-economic backgrounds through a widespread access to scholarships and student services; v) enhanced outreach to the private sector through co- constructed programs, internships, applied learning with private sector and PhD student mobility with industry, among others; vi) high knowledge output, ranking among Africa’s top tree countries in research outputs and fifth in terms of H- index; vii) strong institutional leadership and a culture of accountability quality and evaluation introduced through the Quality-support programs (PAQ) developed under the Tertiary Education and Employability Project (TEEP) / PromESsE project supported by the World Bank; and viii) the introduction of the Public establishment of scientific and technological nature status (Etablissement à Caractère Scientifique et Technologique (EPST)) to increase institutions’ autonomy in managing their financial resources. 5. Nonetheless, remaining challenges need to be addressed for higher education to play its role in the country’s transition to a green and knowledge economy. The several existing systematic challenges include: i) low employability of graduates18, partly due to the lack of flexibility for HEIs to quickly adapt their programs in collaboration with industry, thus not contributing to a high skilled labor force and creating dissatisfaction among unemployed graduates; ii) a high share of repeaters, with almost one out of four (22 percent) students repeating at least one grade; iii) inequity across students from different regions, genders, and socio-economic background and between coastal and interior universities19; iv) research and innovation without a significant impact on the economy; and v) insufficient autonomy and accountability for results of public universities, hindering greater efficiencies and performance improvements. 6. To effectively navigate the three global megatrends of climate change, digitalization, and population aging, Tunisia must continue to invest in green skills, digital competencies, and health education. In particular: (i) Tunisia must invest in green skills to support its green economic transition, as outlined in the 2023-2025 Development Plan. While about half of surveyed higher education and TVET institutions offer green skills programs, these are often broad and under-equipped for critical areas like water resource management, sea- level rise, and renewable energy. The focus on higher-level skills overlooks the urgent need for medium-level vocational skills, such as technicians, in the renewable energy and energy efficiency sectors. Addressing this gap is vital to meet labor market demands, with the energy strategy forecasting significant job creation—70,000 by 2035 and up to 400,000 by 2050. Component 1 aims to boost tertiary education institutions to deliver quality and relevant programs and better student services, via a competitive funding mechanism, in close partnership with industry. Subcomponent 1.1. ($26.5 million) will finance HEIs through a series of competitive grants (PAQ 2.0) that will focus on enhancing employability, entrepreneurship and innovation. These PAQs will support innovative proposals of priority degree programs in Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM), health sciences and medical education, climate resilient and low-carbon economy, investment promotion, and other relevant programs focusing on enhancing employability in arts and humanities degrees. For each of these priority areas, the proposals selected will finance the following activities: a) Expansion of existing programs and development of new degree programs in fields of study in high labor market demand or in high priority areas; b) Quality review and updating of existing degree programs, in terms of practical curriculum content, and through the introduction of innovative pedagogical and digital teaching approaches; c) Strengthening innovation and industry partnerships; d) Strengthening of the Career Certification and Competency Centers (4Cs) for the promotion of students’ employability skills (including digital, non-cognitive/soft, entrepreneurship, and language skills), and the provision of internships, training, practical lab sessions, and career guidance; e) Enhancement of employability and facilitation of labor force entry of female university graduates. 9. Subcomponent 1.2 ($27 million) will primarily focus on upgrading and greening university facilities and campuses to provide a better teaching and learning environment to students and support the provision of student support services. 10. Subcomponent 1.3 ($15 million) will support training, education and applied research for Climate Adaptation, Mitigation and Resilience. This will include: (a) Subcomponent 1.3.a ($10 million) will support the development of a Renewable Energy Innovation Center including the establishment of a network of renewable energy training partners; and (b) Subcomponent 1.3.b (US$5 million) will support training, education and applied research for innovative solutions fostering a high-quality workforce to address Tunisia’s climate vulnerability, including water scarcity and rising sea levels. Component 2 Strengthen Higher Education and Scientific Research system, Governance and Digital capacities ($31.5 million) 11. Component 2 aims to strengthen the higher education and scientific research system, governance, and digital capacities – thus complementing Component 1 and laying the foundation to its success. More specifically, the project will support HEIs to enhance their governance and project management capabilities, support the governance of the system at large, and the digital transformation of the system (at the central, university, ISET, and other higher education levels). 12. Subcomponent 2.1 ($16.5 million) will use competitive funding to support the governance and management of HEIs through : a) Support the development and implementation of universities’ strategic plans; b) Improved management through higher financial autonomy of universities via the operationalization of the EPST status; c) Strengthened management capacity of central administrations of universities, to ensure universities and higher education institutions are professionally run and have capacity to lead project management. 13. The project involves activities such as small-scale civil works, rehabilitation of existing buildings, and the development of green campuses, all confined within existing educational institutions. While avoiding large-scale construction, these activities may still result in temporary, site-specific environmental risks, including noise, dust, and waste generation during the construction phase. The MOHESR will lead the project ‘s implementation, coordinating project activities through the PMU, ensuring that the necessary legislation is in place to efficiently implement these activities, and providing the necessary support, through the PMU, to help develop and implement project activities. 18. A Project Steering Committee (PSC) will provide high-level project oversight and strategic guidance. The minister of HESR will chair the PSC consisting of representatives of the MOHESR, Presidency of the Government, Ministry of Economy and Planification, Ministry of Finance, other relevant ministries, representatives from the private sector and members of the PMU. The PSC will (a) provide project oversight and strategic guidance for project implementation and ensure coordination with other existing or proposed overlapping activities and programs; (b) oversee project implementation and approve annual action plans and budgets; (c) review and approve project progress reports; and (d) assess project achievements. 19. A PAQ Steering Committee will oversee the grant competition process, initiating each round of the grant competition process with a call for proposals according to the strategic priorities communicated by MOHES; reviewing proposals and ensuring transparency; ensuring the efficient implementation of PAQ sub-projects; and monitoring PAQ subprojects by collecting performance indicators and producing regular implementation progress reports. 20. MOHESR PMU. The project will be implemented by a well-established PMU under the supervision of a coordinator and in close collaboration with the relevant departments of the MOHESR, the World Bank and all other stakeholders. The PMU will ensure the day-to-day supervision of the project execution; liaise with the general directorates coordinating Page 8 The World Bank Strengthening Tertiary Education for Employability Innovation and Resilience (P506551) the implementation of the tertiary education sector strategy, with universities and implementing entities24 and with all other relevant stakeholders (in academics, policy, research, and with the private sector25) and professional staff to ensure successful implementation of the project. Higher Education Institutions (HEIs)26 Universities, student affair offices and research centers will be responsible for the implementation of their competitive grants (PAQs). Each PAQ project will be led by a Project Manager (PM) who is a recognized academic/researcher in the PAQ project's main discipline, supported by senior lecturers from the relevant departments. In addition, the institution's administrative staff will contribute to the implementation of the project. Partners, including international academics, are often represented on an advisory committee. Where relevant and appropriate, HEI will set up Project Management Offices (PMOs) to support the implementation of large PAQs. Human and financial resources will be mobilized to ensure the effective implementation of PAQ sub-projects, staff training and the recruitment of external experts as required.
