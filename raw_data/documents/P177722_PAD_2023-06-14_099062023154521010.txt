Page 6 of 64 The World Bank Tajikistan Financial and Private Sector Development Project (P177722) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. Favorable external conditions propelled Tajikistan’s economic growth and poverty reduction, but GDP per capita remains low, job creation continues to be weak, and productivity improvements are small. Real GDP growth averaged about nine percent in 1998 - 2008 before easing to an average of seven percent in 2010 -2019. Income per capita grew from US$ 162 in 2000 to above US$ 859 in 2020, while the poverty rate reduced from more than 80 percent to an estimated 26 percent. Despite that, per capita GDP remains the lowest among the countries of the former Soviet Union. While the working-age population increased by three percent annually between 2007-2017, net jobs rose by only 1.1 percent annually. Growth was mainly driven by the non-tradeable service and agricultural sectors that operate at low productivity levels. The growth contribution from the industrial sector has been rising, but the sector is dominated by state-owned enterprises (SOEs) with the lowest productivity. Promoting private sector development, particularly in the high value-added sectors, will be critical for productivity gains and job creation. 2. The COVID-19 pandemic harmed Tajikistan’s economy, but it rebounded in 2021 due to the exports of precious materials and remittance-driven domestic demand. The pandemic illustrated how the reliance on predominantly unstable, low-quality, and low-wage jobs in the informal sector exposes livelihoods to external shocks and raises risks related to fragility, conflict, and violence (FCV). In May 2020, there was a nearly 40 percent increase in the number of respondents to the World Bank Listening to Tajikistan survey who reported that “no household member had worked during the preceding week.” The growth rate slowed to 4.5 percent in 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic but rebounded to 8.5 percent in the first half of 2021. This can be attributed to the increased export of precious materials and improved domestic demand, partly stemming from increased remittances. Foreign direct investment (FDI) also showed a strong recovery in the first half of 2021, rising by 64.6 percent or US$38.6 million (compared to the first half of 2020) for a total of US$95.5 million, after a 47 percent decrease in 2020 (compared to 2019). The FDI increase was mainly driven by foreign investments in the mining sector. Nevertheless, the country’s dependence on mining leaves the economy vulnerable to metal price fluctuations. 3. In 2022, strong GDP performance, with the Tajik economy growing by 8.0 percent, contrasted with earlier expectations shaped by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and the effect from the post-COVID rebound of 2021. Though Tajikistan’s direct exposure to Ukraine is low, Russia accounts for more than 80 percent of the total inflow of remittances, or about 30 percent of GDP (on average), and is the second largest source of FDI inflows. In parallel, imports from Russia amount to about 30 percent of total imports. However, the prognosed adverse spillover effects did not materialize or were mitigated by offsetting positive impacts, as Tajikistan saw strong financial inflows, mostly from China, that fueled household income growth and economy-wide liquidity. The economy also benefited from Russia’s strong labor demand and appreciation of the Russian ruble – the main earning and transfer currency of labor migrants, resulting in substantial increases in remittances. An additional contribution to GDP growth came from externally co-financed development projects which drove the growth of public investments in sectors such as energy, education, and transport, and private investments which were primarily concentrated in the mining industry. These gains resulted in GDP growth of 8 percent which exceeded earlier growth estimates. 4. However, Tajikistan’s growth prospects are not as strong as its recent performance record. The outlook for 2023 and the medium term is largely affected by uncertainty in the regional geopolitical environment, tightening global financial conditions, and continued necessity to accelerate structural reforms. Private sector-led diversification in the export market (focusing on regional integration, high-value addition, and knowledge spillovers) can increase growth and economic resilience. Out of Tajikistan’s entire US$1,174 million export portfolio in 2019, 65 percent were commodities, including minerals (24 percent), stone (17 percent), metals (14 percent), and cotton (11 percent). Raw extractive sectors typically do not create well-paid jobs and are at the lower end of global value chains. While 70 percent of the workforce is in agriculture, the sector accounts for only 22 percent of GDP (due to low productivity). It pays three times lower wages than the non-agriculture sector. SOEs dominate the economic space and drain resources from the state budget. The preferential market position of SOEs prevents the efficient allocation of resources and inhibits further private-sector development. In 2018, Tajikistan’s new business registration stood at 0.2 per 1000 people aged 14 to 65 compared with 2 for Kazakhstan and 1.9 for Moldova. Annex 2 provides more detail on Tajikistan’s private sector. 6. Weak financial intermediation remains a key obstacle to efficient capital reallocation, private sector-led growth, and productive SME development. Deposits as a share of GDP were at 9.37 percent in 2020,1 much lower than in comparator countries such as the Kyrgyz Republic (26.9 percent) and Moldova (37.7 percent). Such a low deposit mobilization constrains financial intermediation. Credit to the private sector stood at 13 percent of GDP in Tajikistan by the end of 2020, significantly lower than the average of 42 percent for lower-middle-income countries and 57 percent for countries in the Europe and Central Asia (ECA) region. Annex 2 provides more detail on Tajikistan’s financial sector. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context Deposit Mobilization 7. A lack of confidence in the banking sector contributes to low deposit mobilization. Based on available Global Findex Data, only 47 percent of adults have an account with a formal financial institution, and just 11.3 percent have formal savings.2 Cash held outside the financial system is significant. Tajik retail depositors hold only TJS 10323 (approx. US $83) in bank accounts on average, or about 10 percent of the country’s per capita income - a low volume, especially given that households lack alternative means of saving other than bank deposits. In general, a quarter of the population does not have accounts at local banks due to a lack of trust in financial institutions. Notably, recent restrictions in accessing funds at Tojiksodirobonk (TSB) and Agroinvestbonk (AIB) further reduced confidence in the banking system. 8. A credible deposit insurance scheme can support deposit mobilization, but the Tajik scheme has operational and funding weaknesses, and the complementary bank resolution framework needs to improve implementation gaps. Annex 3 provides a comprehensive review of the deposit insurance scheme and other complementary segments of the financial safety net. The proposed project will address the identified gaps in Individual Deposit Insurance Fund (IDIF)’s capacity to perform its mandate.4 IDIF reforms include increasing its recovery ratios in future payouts and incorporating lessons learned from the TSB and AIB payouts - namely, the need for further automation to hasten the payout process and for raising public awareness on the deposit insurance scheme. Furthermore, the liquidations of TSB and AIB in 2021 enabled IDIF to initiate the payout of insured depositors. Still, they depleted its reserves to around 100 million Tajikistan Somoni, far below the target level of seven percent of individual deposits or 350 million Tajikistani Somoni. It would take over ten years for IDIF to reach this target based on collecting new premiums from banks (assuming no further payouts). Banks allocate capital to the private sector inefficiently, and MSMEs face high collateral requirements. Banks dominate the financial sector and have a long history of weak performance due to poor lending practices. Historically, some banks have been subjected to extensive directed lending at subsidized rates for priority projects, politically connected borrowers, and loss-making SOEs. These practices have discouraged adequate risk assessment and made it costly for private firms to access bank credit. In 2019, only 18 percent of private firms had a bank loan or line of credit, compared with an average of 37.6 percent in ECA. Among MSMEs that wanted to apply for credit in 2019 but have yet to, most cited high rates and high collateral as the main reasons. The level of collateral needed to obtain a loan stood at 125 percent. Consequently, only 2.5 percent of private firms reported using banks to finance investment, compared with 13.2 percent in the ECA region. The low credit intermediation has made it difficult for private firms to finance working capital and expand their activities or undertake new investments using more efficient external sources. 10. Female-headed MSMEs’ access to finance and female participation in MSMEs could improve. Fewer than one- fifth of female-headed SMEs have a bank loan or line of credit to finance investments or working capital shortages. Although 95 percent of female-led firms have an account at a financial institution, only 17.7 percent have a bank loan or line of credit. Approximately 96 percent of businesses with a female as the top manager used internal resources to finance investments. Overall, female participation in employment, top management, and firm ownership is amongst the lowest in the Caucasus and Central Asia regions. Addressing this gap could stimulate productivity and private sector expansion, and lead to a decline in unemployment (with the young female unemployment rate at 12 percent in 2019). 11. Partial credit guarantees supporting MSME access to finance could be expanded, including for female-headed and female-owned firms. Such risk-sharing facilities can support MSMEs facing collateral constraints in normal times and higher risk aversion by banks during periods of economics crisis. There are active partial credit guarantee schemes in Tajikistan while they have demonstrated high demand, they had limited results due to constrained financial resources and some design issues. The Ministry of Finance (MoF) has expressed interest in scaling up the use of guarantees, and the NBT has requested technical assistance in upgrading the regulatory framework for credit guarantees. Nevertheless, private sector activity is hampered overall by a challenging business environment6 and limited MSME market linkages to large local and multinational corporations operating in Tajikistan or the region. The Government of Tajikistan is taking steps in the right direction: the State Export Agency was created in 2019 for this purpose, and there is a State Export Development Program for 2021-2025 which focuses on developing export, strengthening export-oriented and import-substituting production, and increasing the competitiveness of domestic products in the domestic and foreign markets. Similarly, a 2014 Law on State Entrepreneurship Support sets the framework and envisions budget allocation for entrepreneurship support programs. However, there is a need for a robust set of practical instruments implementing and complementing government-backed interventions for MSME capacity building that could unleash Tajikistan’s local comparative advantages. 13. Firm capabilities—notably in the non-agriculture private sector—need to improve for MSMEs to better connect to and move up the local, regional, and global value chains. In 2019, only 2.2 percent of firms in Tajikistan had an internationally recognized quality certification, compared with 22 percent in the ECA region. Firms in Tajikistan are much less likely to develop new products/services (only 19 percent introduced a new product/service in 2019, compared with 29 percent in the ECA region),7 new process innovations (only 10 percent introduced new process innovations in 2019, compared with 20 percent in the ECA region), or use digital means to do business (only 28 percent have own website, compared with 50 percent in the ECA region). 14. Supplier and Export Development programs can benefit MSMEs specializing in the lower-value-added industries and suffering from limited market linkages in value chains. Only 1.6 percent of small firms and 4.6 percent of medium- sized firms exported directly in 2019, compared with 14 percent for small firms and 25 percent for medium-sized firms in the ECA region. Firms that connect to the international market are typically the large ones, with 11.9 percent of them exporting directly in 2019 and primarily specializing in producing and exporting raw materials and commodities. Targeted government support for MSMEs has successfully created market linkages with large companies and helped MSMEs move up the private sector value chains in other Central Asian and ECA countries, especially in tradeable sectors. II. PROJECT DESCRIPTION A. Project Development Objective PDO Statement 17. The Project Development Objective is to strengthen deposit insurance, promote bank lending to MSMEs, and boost MSME integration in value chains. PDO Level Indicators 18. The PDO indicators are: (i) Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF): Size of the deposit insurance fund as a share of total individual deposits in the system, including deposits in state-owned Banks, including the returns obtained from investing such amounts and any payouts made during project implementation. (iii) Supplier and Export Development Program (SEDP): Number of MSMEs that become accredited suppliers to large companies under the SEDP. 19. (iii) The percentage of surveyed beneficiaries who stated that they increased awareness about deposit insurance. (iv) The number of Participating Financial Institutions (PFIs) under the PRSF. (v) The cumulative volume of MSME credits guaranteed under the PRSF. (vi) The volume of guarantees called under the PRSF (for monitoring purposes, no target). (vii) The number of MSMEs benefitting from the SEDP’s capacity building activities. (viii) The number of certificates received by the MSMEs under the project. The World Bank has implemented several successful interventions with deposit insurance systems through technical assistance and recapitalizations, such as under Component 1. For example, the Serbia Deposit Insurance Strengthening Project (P146248) was the first World Bank project to (re)capitalize a deposit insurance fund. The Mozambique Financial Inclusion and Stability Project (P166107) demonstrated that the full support of a broader set of stakeholders – namely the Central Bank and the MoF – is an essential element for the enhancement of a deposit insurance agency. A key lesson learned from all deposit insurance engagements is that preparing and conducting a successful payout is challenging, requiring adequate human and technical resources and strict coordination between the deposit insurer and the supervision and resolution authority. 33. The PRSF design under Component 2 builds on the World Bank’s Principles for Public Credit Guarantee Schemes for SMEs14 —a set of good practices to guide the design, execution, and evaluation of public credit guarantee schemes around the world, developed by the World Bank in conjunction with international associations of guarantee schemes and lenders in 2015—and on a World Bank guidance note of 2020 on adopting the principles in the time of COVID-19. For example, it builds on principle 11, which suggests that guarantees by credit guarantee schemes should be partial, providing the right incentives for borrowers and lenders. Specifically, to avoid a moral hazard on the part of both lenders and borrowers, credit risk must be shared appropriately among the scheme, lenders, and borrowers. The PRSF will be partial and undisclosed to the beneficiaries, building on an open model and engaging existing GFs with a successful track record. 34. Natural disasters and climate change threaten Tajikistan’s economic and social develo pment. The country’s geological, climatologic, and topographic features make it vulnerable to many natural hazards, including earthquakes, floods, drought, landslides and mudslides, heatwaves and wildfires, and avalanches. From 1992 to 2016, natural disasters affected seven million people in Tajikistan—over 80 percent of the total population—and caused economic losses worth US$1.8 billion. In response to these risks, the Government of Tajikistan is gradually moving from disaster response to risk mitigation. It has taken steps to include disaster risk mitigation in development planning, including adopting the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction in 2015. 53. Tajikistan is a mountainous country with diverse topography and aridity; high temperatures and significant inter-annual variability of almost all climatic elements are predominant characteristics of Tajikistan’s climate . Annual mean temperatures vary from 17°C in the south to -6°C in the lower Pamirs. Maximum temperatures are observed in July and minimum in January. Due to climate change, the mean annual temperatures are projected to be warmer by approximately 2°C by 2050. The periods of December-February and June-August are likely to be the warmest periods. Exposure of crops and livestock to climate variability will likely increase horticulture and livestock production waste. As its primary objective, a functioning deposit insurer promotes confidence in deposit-taking institutions and fosters a country's financial stability by avoiding contagious bank runs. As such, the deposit insurer is a crucial component of a country’s financial safety net. In addition, by protecting the savings of households and, in most countries, small and medium enterprises, deposit insurers also contribute to social welfare and maintaining consumption during economic crises. 2. To achieve this objective, the deposit insurer deploys a formal process of promptly reimbursing depositors after a bank fails, rather than forcing them to wait in the liquidation procedure. By assuring depositors that their funds will be promptly available even if the bank fails, the deposit insurer reduces the incentive for depositors to run on their banks in case of a crisis (or rumors thereof). 3. By lessening the disruption to the financial system in case of a bank failure, an explicit deposit insurance system allows the supervisory authorities to close troubled institutions promptly. Closing a financial institution may raise concerns among the population about the impact on the remainder of the financial system. Rumors of a broader crisis can quickly spread, further undermining the economic situation of impacted institutions. This often leads to uncertainty on the part of the supervisory authority, which in turn may lead to supervisory forbearance. With a functioning payout mechanism for depositors capable of dealing with those impacts, the supervision authority is in a better position to take prompt and decisive action when a bank fails. 4. A well-designed deposit insurance system protects taxpayers’ resources. Properly functioning formal or explicit deposit insurance systems shift the insurance cost to the banking industry and away from the government, which is the funding source when implicit or assumed schemes are in place. Without an explicit deposit insurer, a country may extend such implicit protection to depositors on an ad hoc basis, either through forbearance (not closing troubled banks) or by injecting capital or otherwise restructuring a troubled bank so that no depositor loses money through a payout. 5. The deposit insurer must have access to adequate funding to accomplish its mandate. Funding includes adequate reserves, accumulated ex-ante to allow the deposit insurer to fund a payout26, but also enough for it to hire and train the staff to be prepared to make a prompt payout of insured deposits once a triggering event for a payout takes place. 6. From a public confidence perspective, adequate reserve levels are necessary to assure the public that the deposit insurer can compensate deposits during crises. Funding adequacy and availability are critical to IDIF’s ability to fulfill the mandate to reimburse insured depositors promptly and thus contribute to financial stability. According to IADI Core Principle 9, an effective deposit insurance system requires deposit insurers to have readily available funds and all funding mechanisms necessary to ensure prompt reimbursement of depositors’ claims, including assured liquidity funding arrangements. This requires accumulated reserves resulting from regular contributions by member institutions. It also requires emergency arrangements, such as the right to charge extraordinary contributions, to contract loans from third parties, and, most importantly, the existence of a backup funding mechanism that is certain and readily available in times of need, usually through some form of government loan or guarantee. 26 Deposit insurers can be either funded “ex-ante” or “ex-post”. Ex-ante funds collect contributions from the industry regularly to form reserves that can then be used to pay depositors. Ex-post deposit insurers do not collect reserves – once a bank fails, they request the industry to pay at once the amount required. Because ex-post funds are pro-cyclical (i.e., they place a burden on banks in the middle of a crisis), they have been largely discontinued and replaced by ex-ante arrangements. Component 2 will finance the Partial Risk-Sharing Facility (PRSF). The PRSF aims to incentivize lending by Participating Financial Intermediaries (PFIs) to viable businesses through the provision of a partial credit guarantee under an individual and portfolio model. Individual guarantees will target larger loan facilities with the loan size determined during the implementation stage. Under individual guarantees, the PFI assesses each loan request based on its underwriting criteria. If the PFI decides that the project to be financed is viable, producing positive cash flow but lacking adequate collateral, it submits a guarantee request to the PRSF that evaluates it against a set of criteria and approves or rejects the request. A portfolio guarantee is a suitable instrument for the COVID-19 response and loans below a predefined limit. Under the portfolio approach, the guarantee will be applied to all credits meeting predefined eligibility criteria. This offers: (i) systemic coverage, as the guarantee will be provided to a large portfolio of eligible credits; and (ii) reduced processing time, as the Guarantee Funds (GFs) do not review applications at the individual credit level and accept all credit meeting predefined eligibility criteria. Importantly, because the PFIs keep part of the credit risk, it is in their interest to carefully screen credit applications and select viable borrowers. 2. Implementation structure. The MOF will establish the PRSF account at a commercial bank(s) with sound financial performance, solid history of meeting with prudential norms, and allowing for the quick/instant transfer of funds to accounts of other financial institutions. The MOF will allocate IDA funding as ‘capital’ to the account and will be invested. This account will be owned (as a trustee) by the MOF. The PRSF will be set up through agreements signed between the MOF and the GFs (GF Agent Agreements). Each GF Agent Agreement will assign the GF as the manager of certain designated windows of the PRSF (see more details about the windows below; the alternative of KPIs will also be explored). The GFs will be responsible for developing the detailed operational procedures (the PRSF Procedural Manual). The GFs will also be responsible for the day-to-day management of the designated PRSF windows, including signing agreements with PFIs, registering new guarantees, process claim payouts, track recovery proceeds, and manage investment for allocated funds. The GFs will extend this risk sharing to PFIs which meet a set of predetermined criteria for the designated windows. 3. Financial parameters: The final decision regarding commissions and other financial parameters of the PRSF, such as “stop loss measures” designed to protect the PRSF from large losses experienced by a few PFIs, will be set in the PRSF Procedural Manual to be developed by the GFs. The financial parameters include, inter alia, the following: • Administrative fees to the GFs: The MOF will negotiate and pay fees to participating GFs. Exact fee details and rationale will be further described in the PRSF Procedural Manual. • Commission fee: The PFIs will pay a commission fee to benefit from PRSF coverage, which could be different for each window. The fees are collected by the GFs and kept in the PRSF account to cover operational expenses including GFs’ management fees. More details will be specified in the PRSF Procedural Manual. • Guarantee coverage: Guarantees are expected to cover around 50 percent for each credit. The coverage could be further increased for more risky segments such as startup financing to incentivize lending by financial institutions. • Stop loss: There are potentially two stop-loss mechanisms. First, once a certain level of claim ratio (defined as the volume of claims divided by the amount of credit guaranteed) is reached, the PFI will not be able to enter Page 52 of 64 The World Bank Tajikistan Financial and Private Sector Development Project (P177722) further credits in the guarantee. Second, at a higher level of claim or NPL ratio, the window will stop guaranteeing new loans and paying against claims until the portfolio recovers. • Maximum guarantee amount allocated for each PFI: A maximum amount may be set for the guarantee for each PFI based on loan projections under each window or on a first come first served basis, above which no new credits will be entered into the guarantee portfolio. The PFI may be charged a commitment fee for the allocated guarantee amount if the guarantee amount is committed. The GF could change the threshold periodically across the PFIs based on uptake. • Fund investment: Fund investment will be managed by the GFs. Profits from invested funds will ensure sustainability and potential expansion of PRSF. • Credit registration process: The PFIs analyze each credit request and decide whether or not to extend the credit. For portfolio guarantees, GFs do not perform any further credit checks and the registration of credit is automatic. For individual guarantees, the GF will assess the credit on a loan by loan basis. • Claim settlement process: If a credit guaranteed under one of the windows defaults, the PFI will submit a claim (after attempts to resolve or restructure have been exhausted) to the GF that will process the payment swiftly. Defaults will be defined in the PRSF Procedural Manual and will be aligned with the definition provided by the National Bank of Tajikistan. Within 30 calendar days, the GF will review and settle the claim. Quick payment is an important benefit for PFIs as they receive cash immediately without having to wait for a lengthy settlement process. The PFI will remain in charge of the recovery, and the resulting proceeds will be shared proportionally with the PRSF based on the guarantee coverage. The annual technical and financial audit will assess whether or not the PFIs have been conducting recoveries and sharing the proceeds with the PRSF. • Mobilizing additional financing for the PRSF: The PRSF will be set up in a flexible way to allow participation from other donors and development partners in its capitalization. Tajikistan has a young population with limited domestic job opportunities, resulting in large out-migration. Tajikistan has a population of 9.57 million and GDP per capita of US$859 in 2020. With over a third of population currently under 14 years old, Tajikistan’s labor force is set to rise over the long term. Development of non-agriculture formal private sector is a promising way for Tajikistan to create more and better jobs. To date, Tajikistan is still a mostly agriculture economy. Connecting to the international market and moving up the value chains in tradable private sector will help Tajikistan to create more and better jobs. International evidence suggests that connecting to international market will help firms to upgrade their technology and move up the value chain38. Tapping into the large international markets also enables firms particularly in tradable labor-intensive manufacturing industries to quickly expand production and create large number of jobs.39 Nevertheless, connectivity to international market is low in Tajikistan - only 3 percent of firms directly exported in 2019, comparing with the average of 17 percent in ECA. For the firms that do export, Tajikistan mainly specializes in producing and exporting raw materials and commodities. By developing and moving up the private sector value chains especially in tradable sectors, Tajikistan could take advantage of its demographic dividend to create more jobs and accelerate economic growth and poverty reduction. 15. As an example, the detailed OP 10 benchmarked the operations and performance of both CGFT/ TCSP and Zamonat against the Principles for Public Credit Guarantee Schemes for SMEs developed by the World Bank. CGFT/ TCSP was found within the acceptable standards and principles but Zamonat required significant technical assistance to qualify it to meet these guidelines 6. The envisioned entity to be created at the end of the transitional period is likely to be an independent legal entity, not-for-profit institution, in the form of a company or a foundation and is expected to be financially and operationally independent and to apply the principles related to the operation framework. It is also envisioned that other development agencies or donors may be interested to provide further funding for specific initiatives carried out by the PRSF. An approach under consideration in this project is to use excess funds generated from the deposit insurance initiative to further capitalize of the PRSF. This is in line with international best practices and the Government of Tajikistan’s expectation and vision based on the several discussions held during field and virtual missions held to date. 7. The PRSF model has been proposed to all parties concerned and further discussions will take place to work out the details. The Team will also ensure that other relevant parties such as the National Bank of Tajikistan would be informed in order to ensure that the new entity to be created is in full compliance with all regulatory requirements.
