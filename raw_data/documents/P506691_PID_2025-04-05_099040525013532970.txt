Introduction and Context Country Context 1. Bangladesh faces several significant challenges that successive governments have been striving to address. These include sustaining growth-generating employment, reforming the financial sector, improving the business environment, and enhancing governance. The student-led uprising during the summer of 2024 resulted in political transition that has put transparency and accountability high on the reform agenda. The current Government intends to address these pressing issues through substantial governance reforms with a sense of urgency. Bangladesh aspires to develop a public sector that is open and responsive, including a citizen-centric system that fully leverages technology to ensure that all enjoy equal rights and opportunities. 2. Bangladesh experienced rapid economic and social progress in recent decades, reaching lower-middle-income status in 2015, but economic growth has slowed since FY22. Slower growth has been accompanied by a reduction in the pace of poverty reduction and worsening vulnerabilities in the financial sector. Stable macroeconomic conditions underpinned an average annual real gross domestic product (GDP) growth of 6.3 percent between 2010 and 2024. Over the same period, poverty and extreme poverty declined by 20.6 and 5.7 percentage points to 29.0 percent and 6.1 percent, Page 2 The World Bank Strengthening Institutions for Transparency and Accountability (SITA) (P506691) respectively.1 However, real GDP growth declined from 7.1 percent in FY22 to 4.2 percent in FY24 and is projected to decline further to 3.3 percent in FY25. The pace of poverty reduction has also slowed, as has the pace of job creation, particularly in urban areas and in the formal sector (Bangladesh Development Update, April 2024). Despite overall poverty reduction, rising urban inequality led to a three-point increase in Bangladesh’s income Gini index at the national level. Between 2016 and 2022, urban income inequality rose from 0.464 to 0.545, while rural inequality declined slightly from 0.480 to 0.474. 3. To achieve faster growth and transition to an upper-middle-income country (UMIC), Bangladesh needs to implement multisectoral structural reforms. These include targeted interventions to build a conducive and competitive business environment, diversify exports, deepen the financial sector, and create more productive and efficient urban areas (Bangladesh Country Economic Memorandum 2022). These reforms also need to be accompanied by better labor market outcomes. Bangladesh needs to improve its human capital and create an enabling environment for more and better jobs for the large number of youths entering the job market every year.2 Such reforms will strengthen international competitiveness as Bangladesh prepares to graduate from least-developed country (LDC) status. LDC graduation will reduce concessional financing and preferential market access for its exports. Bangladesh has a low risk of overall and external debt distress according to the June 2024 joint International Monetary Fund (IMF)-World Bank Debt Sustainability Assessment (DSA). The Joint DSA is expected to be updated by June 2025. Bangladesh is currently not subject to debt limits conditionality under the Sustainable Development Finance Policy (SDFP). 4. The Global Climate Risk Index ranks Bangladesh as the world’s seventh most affected country over 2000–2019 faced with a high level of vulnerability to climate and disaster hazard risks often characterized by floods, tropical cyclones, storm surges, extreme heat stress, and prolonged drought. The Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR) identified that since 1971, more than US$10 billion was invested by the Government of Bangladesh (GOB) on disaster risk management; yet, frequent and overlapping climate-induced disasters and extremities slowed poverty reduction and economic growth. Although significant priorities were set by the GOB (through prioritizing disaster preparedness by strengthening coordination), support to improving governance by integrating climate and environmental externalities (for example, emission reduction, green tax reforms), resilience building and mitigation embedded investment policy decisions across government portfolios is still limited. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. Bangladesh’s public sector capacity has weakened over the past decades. According to the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI), the GOB’s effectiveness and accountability have deteriorated in the past two decades compared to peers such as Bhutan and Viet Nam (figure 1). It indicates weaknesses in the quality of public services and the civil service, as well as the ability to formulate and implement policies, which are critical for reaching UMIC status. Despite significant progress in improving its National Statistical System (NSS) over the past two decades, Bangladesh still faces critical challenges in leveraging data for policy making. These include gaps in transparency, weak administrative data quality, poor coordination around data sharing, and slow digitization of systems and processes. The NSS—which includes the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) and other data-producing administrative agencies—faces challenges related to infrastructure, institutions, and human resources, as well as a crisis of data reliability, compounded by restricted accessibility. As a result, it struggles to meet the growing demand from citizens and data users for timely and transparent access to high-quality data. Key challenges include the following: (a) institutional barriers limiting timely sharing of core official statistics and administrative data; (b) coordination issues within BBS, across administrative agencies and data users, compounded by a lack of digital public infrastructure; (c) limited financial and technical resources to improve administrative data quality, including the absence of focal point officers from BBS in other government agencies; (d) inadequate physical infrastructure and human capital, in particular shortage of skilled resources in data production, processing, management, and access, which hampers data digitization within BBS; and (e) insufficient technical skills to leverage emerging technologies such as data science and big data. 7. Bangladesh’s revenue mobilization is extremely low and inadequate for its development financing needs. As documented in the forthcoming Bangladesh Public Finance Review, the tax-to-GDP ratio stagnated between 7 and 8 percent of GDP in FY12–FY24, reaching 7.4 percent in FY24, one of the lowest in the world and significantly below its peers, including the average of other South Asian countries (19.4 percent average from 2017 to 2022), structural peers (16.1 percent), and aspirational peers (20.5 percent). The main challenges include (a) a heavy reliance on trade-related taxes; (b) a value added tax (VAT) system characterized by numerous exemptions and truncated rates that reduce collection without being pro-poor; (c) low tax compliance; and (d) significant exemptions (6.9 percent of GDP in FY22), deductions, rebates, and tax holidays including lack of environmental cost recovery with user fees. The challenges have been identified in several policy documents, including in the annual government budgets. However, revenue generation has continued to remain low because of the political economy challenges in reaching a consensus on concrete policy reforms. Given Bangladesh's acute vulnerabilities and the escalating frequency of adverse climate events, the persistently low level of revenue mobilization not only constrains public spending but also severely undermines the country’s ability to mitigate and adapt to climate risks, further exacerbating long-term economic and environmental instability. There are significant inefficiencies in public investment management (PIM).3 PIM efficiency is hampered by lack of budget discipline, long implementation delays and cost overruns (minimum cost overruns average about 30 percent and delays about 3 years, and poor quality of service delivery (116 out of 137 countries in the overall quality investment of the Global Competitive Index). In many investment projects, key elements such as objectives, inputs, outputs, and log frames are not adequately documented. This lack of clarity leads to challenges in implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. Projects are taken without proper feasibility studies. Projects initiated without proper feasibility or stakeholder consultations often face significant obstacles during implementation. Given the level of spending per capita on infrastructure, Bangladesh could have better quality of infrastructure and logistics, including for transport, roads, ports, air transport, and electricity. Bangladesh needs to strengthen public investment project management, by adopting effective management practices at the planning, allocation, implementation, maintenance, monitoring, and evaluation phases, which will improve infrastructure service delivery. Full digitalization of the development planning process, which is currently mostly paper based, is required. Also, there is a crucial need to establish robust monitoring and evaluation (M&E) frameworks and IT systems at the Project Implementation Unit (PIU) level, as well as at the Implementation Monitoring and Evaluation Division (IMED), and have these systems interconnected. Reducing climate and disaster vulnerabilities will necessitate a substantial increase in green and climate-resilient investments, funded by both the public and private sectors. Applying budgetary discipline to prioritizing investment projects will be critical given the large number of existing public investment projects and limited fiscal resources. 9. Strengthening public procurement will be central to improving public investment efficiency and effectiveness and service delivery. The GOB launched the e-Government Procurement (e-GP) System in 2011, starting with three processes: Annual Procurement Plan (APP), Bidding and Contract Award, and Contract Management. Though the e-GP is widely adopted in the country, its usage has stagnated at 67 to 70 percent for the last couple of years as the e-GP is not legally mandated and procurement of large works and multilateral development bank (MDB) procurement procedures are not available in the e-GP. More generally, the public procurement system still needs improvement, including contract management (about 70 percent of civil works contracts are not completed on time); lack of incentives for sustainable public procurement (SPP) that aims to achieve value for money on the life cycle of goods and services procured and embed climate risk management; delays in bid evaluation and approval of contracts; and inadequate integration of the procurement system with the budget and accounting system. The consolidation of procurement responsibilities and authorities under the Bangladesh Public Procurement Authority (BPPA) needs to be fully operationalized and complemented with critical reforms. 10. Women in Bangladesh face significant barriers to participation in public procurement, with only 7.2 percent4 of businesses being women-owned, mostly SMEs, and just 1.7 percent5 of formal firms being women-owned. In addition to the challenges they face to starting and growing a business, women encounter further barriers to participating in public procurement, commencing with registration. The e-GP registration process is complex, involving fee payments, document notarizations, and profile verification. Women, in particular, face difficulties due to lower awareness of the requirements and limited capacity to acquire trade licenses, secure necessary funds, and navigate digital systems. As a result, only 3.6 percent of registered bidders are women (with 4,2056 registered women bidders out of 116,119 as of September 2024). Despite initiatives by government organizations, capacity building for women-owned businesses to become successful tenderers is lacking. Currently, a firm is only eligible to receive training after it has registered with e-GP. The GOB is emphasizing the strengthening of oversight and accountability of public sector institutions for improved service delivery to its citizens. The Government has developed significant digital public financial management (PFM) capabilities, including automation of critical PFM processes. However, achievements remain fragmented, and strengthening oversight and accountability of public sector institutions will be critical to ensure better service delivery. The latest PEFA performance of 2021 and 2016 reveals that external scrutiny and audit continue to remain a weak spot in the PFM system. There is a significant backlog of audit reports, and the follow-up system is not always applied, resulting in a grade of D+ (basic) for external audit. The prolonged delay of audit undermines the accountability of public spending, mainly because of the delay in preparation of Financial Statements, consolidation of Financial Statements of Self Accounting Entities, Special Accounts, Railway, Bangladesh Forest Department, and so on. Lack of professionalization, inadequate interface with other digital system on PFM and its periphery, remain key impediments for the timely preparation of Financial Statements and audit report. The Supreme Audit Institution (SAI) is an important actor in a country’s accountability chain. Its external oversight role is established by the Constitution. Bangladesh’s SAI, the Office of the Comptroller and Auditor General (OCAG), needs to be enhanced for greater efficiency and effectiveness through the technological transformation of audit systems across public institutions and support for specialized audit capacity. The oversight and accountability systems of strategically important ministries and departments need to be strengthened for growth-sustaining and socially impactful public investment programs. Their institutional capacity could also be developed by building specialized audit capacity in identified areas, including the climate finance program, the environment, procurement, emergency operations, and large infrastructure projects. Accordingly, following PEFA 2021, the Ministry of Finance approved a comprehensive PFM Action Plan for 2024–28 published in March 2023, which prioritizes six key reform actions and implementation roadmap to strengthen the institutional capacity of OCAG and improve the systems and procedures in use so that external audits are gradually carried out as per international standards, with timely publishing of the audit report. 12. The proposed project is fully aligned with the Country Partnership Framework (CPF)7 for FY2023–27, currently under implementation. Building on the country’s development progress, the CPF seeks to help Bangladesh transform itself into a more competitive, resilient, inclusive, and greener economy as it aspires to become a UMIC. The proposed project will support Objective 3: ‘Improved effectiveness of public institutions to deliver better services to citizens and businesses.’ The CPF also proposes leveraging digital tools to enhance transparency and accountability in Bangladesh, and the proposed project will directly contribute to these efforts. D. Project Description 14. The project will provide technical and financial support to five critical agencies to implement and institutionalize urgent transparency and accountability reforms undertaken by the Interim Government. The agencies are BBS; the National Board of Revenue (NBR); the Planning Division (PD) under the Ministry of Planning; the Bangladesh Public Procurement Authority (BPPA), and the Office of the Comptroller and Auditor General (OCAG). The Project will have three key components with a clear line of sight to the policy actions supported through the Strengthening Governance and Institutional Resilience (SGIR) DPC. Component 1: Statistical Capacity Enhancement and Modernization (SCEM) for Evidence-based Policy and Public Investments 15. This Component complements the transparency reforms supported by the SGIR DPC through the adoption of digital data systems for statistical capacity enhancement and modernization. It will strengthen the capacity of the BBS to support evidence-based policymaking and improved service delivery through investments in: (i) modernizing physical and statistical infrastructure, including data management and information and communication technologies (ICT) systems, to inform core macroeconomic, fiscal, and social protection policy functions; (ii) improving the transparency and timeliness of data production to ensure its availability for public and private sectors, civil society, and citizens; and (iii) building public sector capacity to utilize statistical, administrative and big data to support citizen-centric service delivery. Component 2: Domestic Revenue Mobilization for Sustainable Public Sector Financing 16. The Component aims to modernize tax administration in Bangladesh through reforms in business processes, automate tax collection, and strengthen analytical and policy analysis capacity. It will strengthen capacity of NBR’s Research and Statistics Wing in data collection, management, tax analysis, revenue forecasting, tax gap and tax expenditure analysis, and identification of compliance risks. It will support the design and implementation of VAT and tax expenditure rationalization and tax compliance management. This Component, to be implemented by NBR, will also strengthen tax administration, focused on digitalization of tax administration and interoperability of the various tax IT systems. It will support: (i) Income tax administration, including end-to-end automation of simplified business processes;(ii) VAT administration, including the development of a SAP competency center within the NBR’s IT Wing, and the completion of the development of the Integrated VAT Administration System (IVAS) IT system and the integration of the IVAS with the SAP and Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) Software; (iii) Interoperability among the NBR IT systems (Automated System for Customs Data (ASYCUDA) World, IVAS and income tax) and with the iBAS++ (Integrated Budgeting and Accounting System), and other key data systems and (iv) Taxpayer outreach and services, including the establishment of an automated Taxpayer Call Center. The subcomponent will also support the tax administration workforce capacity by developing and implementing a capacity development and training plan. In particular, it will strengthen internal information technology (IT) capacity, including SAP competency and certification programs. Component 3: Expenditure Efficiency and Transparency for Accountable and Improved Service Delivery 17. This Component is divided into three subcomponents designed to address three aspects of expenditure efficiency: public investment preparation and implementation (to be implemented by the Planning Division of the Ministry of Planning); procurement modernization to improve service delivery (to be implemented by BPPA); and strengthening accountability institutions for improved oversight of public expenditure (to be implemented by OCAG). The Component will strengthen institutional capacity for efficient and transparent project planning, preparation, and selection. First, it will support (i) the development and implementation of a project selection framework and citizens’ feedback mechanisms to streamline, rationalize, and improve public scrutiny of investment projects, including pipeline and stock; (ii) training of Page 7 The World Bank Strengthening Institutions for Transparency and Accountability (SITA) (P506691) staff in the Planning Commission and across ministries, departments, and agencies (MAD) in various planning, appraisal, and forecast tools. The Component will improve procurement practices, project implementation, e-GP scope and coverage and implementation of SPP. This support consists of three interrelated areas (i) E-GP enhancement, (ii) Public Procurement Professionalization, and (iii) Public Procurement practices and SPP. The Component will also support Strengthening Accountability Institutions for Improved Oversight of Public Expenditures, in particular (i) Building IT infrastructure and audit digitalization; (ii) Enhancing transparency, accountability and efficiency of public institutions and high spending sectors, as well as infrastructure investments; (iii) Improving the timeliness of the Annual Audit, Government Response, Disclosure, Dissemination and Communication Strategy; and (iv) Strengthening the center of excellence “Financial Management Academy�? The overall environmental and social risks associated with the SITA project are rated as “Moderate�? due to the type, scale, and nature of the project activities. The project locations are not finalized at this appraisal stage. However, the project is not expected to involve any significant or irreversible environmental or social risks. The modernization of physical infrastructure is limited to internal refurbishments and furnishings without any major construction related activities. Internal refurbishments and instalment of new furnishings are likely to generate waste, noise and dust which can be mitigated using known engineering and housekeeping measures. Environmental risks primarily relate to the management and disposal of electronic waste (e-waste) generated during the replacement of outdated IT equipment, such as computers, servers, and networking devices, across various government agencies. These risks can be mitigated through the development and implementation of an E-Waste Management and Disposal Plan (EWMDP), which will follow Hazardous Waste (E-Waste) Management Rules 2021 and Good International Industrial Practices (GIIP). E-waste disposal will be further improved through training of staff and proper recordkeeping of equipment purchased, reused and auctioned. Social risks are primarily related to the potential exclusion of individuals and businesses with low IT literacy, particularly small and medium enterprises (SMEs), and people with disabilities who may face challenges in accessing and using the newly implemented digital systems. To mitigate these risks, the project will focus on developing inclusive system designs, user-friendly interfaces, and capacity-building programs to ensure that all stakeholders can benefit from the project. Minor refurbishment and furnishings may require small scale labor. They will be trained on codes of conduct (COC) with sexual exploitation and abuse/sexual harassment (SEA/SH) requirements and occupational health and safety (OHS) guidelines. These requirements will be included in the bidding documents. Additionally, stakeholder consultations will be Page 8 The World Bank Strengthening Institutions for Transparency and Accountability (SITA) (P506691) held to incorporate feedback from diverse groups and address any concerns related to accessibility and social inclusion. Grievance redress mechanisms (GRM) will be established for labor and for overall project. The project does not involve land acquisition, physical displacement, or activities that would impact Indigenous Peoples or small ethnic groups. As a result, the social risks are considered low, given that the project activities are limited to IT upgrades, capacity building, and digitalization efforts within existing government facilities. Gender considerations will be integrated into the project design to promote positive gender outcomes. The project will aim to collect sex-disaggregated data, encourage the participation of women through targeted training. Furthermore, project will support the participation of women-owned firms in public procurement to complement the recently approved Sustainable Procurement Policy which aims to make public procurement more accessible to such firms. The SEA/SH risk rating using the Education tool is rated to be "Low". Given that large amounts of personal data will be collected and organized there is also a risk associated with data privacy. The project will ensure that data privacy is an important feature in data handling and storing in new systems. 20. The implementing agencies will develop the necessary instruments including the Stakeholder Engagement Plan (SEP), Labor Management Procedures (LMP) embedded in relevant Environmental and Social Code of Practice (ESCOP) and the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP) to address potential risks and impacts effectively. The E-WMDP shall be prepared, disclosed, consulted, and adopted before carrying out the relevant Project activities. Through these measures, the project will ensure compliance with the Environmental and Social Framework (ESF) and promote positive environmental and social outcomes throughout its lifecycle. E. Implementation Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 21. To streamline and simplify the implementation of the SITA Project, an agile oversight and coordination institutional arrangement will be put in place. The ERD Secretary will provide strategic guidance, oversight and coordination between the five Implementing Agencies (IAs) involved through an appointed Focal Point. The Focal Point will be part of the Steering Committees of the five project components carried by BBS, NBR, Planning Division, BBPA and the OCAG. Project procurement will be implemented by the 5 agencies independent of each other. Accordingly, each agency will be registered in STEP and will have direct access to submit the procurement plan and review requests to the Bank, whereas ERD as an overall coordinator will be registered in STEP under all the five implementing agencies so that it will receive alert emails and have access to STEP, however, will not be assigned a role to submit requests.
