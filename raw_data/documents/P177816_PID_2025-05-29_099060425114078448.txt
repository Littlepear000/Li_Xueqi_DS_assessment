Introduction and Context 1. This Project Information Document describes the additional financing (AF) for two country-level projects currently being implemented under Phase 3 of the Food Systems Resilience Program for Eastern and Southern Africa (FSRP Phase 3, P177816). The AF includes grants of US$50 million from the Crisis Response Window Plus and US$14.6 million in national International Development Association (IDA) resources to the Malawi Food Systems Resilience Project (Malawi FSRP, also known as AGCOM 2.0); and of US$30 million in national IDA resources to the Somalia Food Systems Resilience Project (Somalia FSRP). The central purpose of the AF is to restore, in full, the resources both projects reallocated to emergency operations undertaken upon activation of their Contingency Emergency Response Component (CERC). A total of US$50 million was reallocated to the CERC by the Malawi FSRP, and US$28.28 million was reallocated by the Somalia FSRP. The AF will thereby allow the projects’ original components to recover the resources originally budgeted to them. In addition, it will provide the two projects, specifically the Malawi FSRP, additional resources beyond those originally budgeted. For reasons detailed below, the Malawi FSRP’s non-CERC components will gain US$34.6 million, and the Somalia FSRP’s US$1.72 million. The AF will increase the Malawi FSRP’s overall budget by 20 percent, bringing it to US$390.6 million, and it will increase the Somalia FSRP’s budget by 20 percent, bringing it to US$180 million, CERC included. The paper provides the context and rationale for the proposed AF and describes the modifications needed to effectively utilize the additional resources. 2. The parent Food Systems Resilience Program for Eastern and Southern Africa (P178566) (the MPA, or the Program) was approved by the World Bank Board of Directors on June 21, 2022, with an overall envelope of US$2.3 billion equivalent, including US$788.1 million for Phase 1. Phase 1 provides financing to Ethiopia (US$600 million), Madagascar (US$158.1 million), the Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) (US$25 million), and the Centre for Coordination of Agricultural Research and Development for Southern Africa (CCARDESA) (US$5 million). The program development objective (PDO) of the MPA is to increase the resilience of food systems and preparedness for food insecurity in Project areas. 3. On May 30, 2023, the Board approved the second and third phases of the MPA together with an increase in available IDA financing raising the total International Development Association (IDA) envelope to US$2.75 billion. Phase 2 (P179818) provides US$300 million in national and regional IDA financing to Tanzania. Phase 3 (P177816) provides US$603 million in national and regional IDA financing to Kenya (US$150 million), the Union of Comoros (US$40 million), Somalia (US$150 million), Malawi (US$250 million), and the African Union (US$13 million). Additionally, Phase 3 included a Global Page 4 The World Bank Food Systems Resilience Program for Eastern and Southern Africa (Phase 3) FSRP (P177816) – Additional Financing Request 2 Agriculture and Food Security Program (GAFSP) grant of US$15 million for the Malawi FSRP and a Global Program for the Blue Economy (GPBE) grant of US$3 million for the Comoros FSRP. Several MPA projects have since received AF. In June 2024, the Madagascar FSRP received US$70 million in AF to support its Contingency Emergency Response Component (CERC) operation, comprising US$50 million from the Crisis Response Window Early Response Financing (CRW ERF) and US$20 million from the GAFSP. In September 2024, the Tanzania FSRP received an AF of US$8.3 million. 4. The Malawi FSRP recently received AF. On February 20, 2025, the Board approved US$61 million in grants for the project comprising US$45 million from the Malawi Second Agricultural Commercialization and Resilience Enhancement Project Multi-Donor Trust Fund (AGCOM 2.0 MDTF1), and US$16 million from the Food Systems 2030 Umbrella Program (FS2030). This AF aims to expand the project’s reach across all four technical components, support a new activity under Component 4 (focused on potential reforms to the national agricultural input subsidy program), and retroactively finance emergency activities implemented under the CERC between November 2023 and August 2024. A. Context 5. Both Malawi and Somalia have experienced significant deteriorations in food security since the approval of FSRP Phase 3. • In Malawi, the food security crisis has worsened since 2023 due to a succession of climate and market shocks. The destructive passage of Tropical Cyclone Freddy in early 2023, the 44 percent devaluation of the Kwacha against the dollar in November 2023, and fuel and electricity price inflation triggered steep increases in the prices of maize and other staple foods. 2 As a result, the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) projected that 3 million people, or about 15 percent of Malawi’s population of 20 million, would face crisis-level food insecurity (IPC Phase 3) in 2023. Conditions continued to deteriorate and between October 2023 and March 2024, the number of people facing IPC Phase 3 or worse was projected to rise to 4.4 million people (22 percent of the population). As the El-Niño phenomenon persisted, droughts devastated an estimated 44 percent of the national crop area in 2024;3 and as of March 2024, year-on-year food price inflation reached nearly 40 percent. Between October 2024 and March 2025, 5.7 million people, or around 28 percent of the population, were expected to face crisis-level or worse food insecurity (IPC Phase 3+). Of those, some 416,000 people were expected to face acute, emergency-level food insecurity (IPC Phase 4). • In Somalia, extreme rainfall associated with El-Niño during the 2023 dyer season (the second rainy season, October– December) caused widespread damage. The flood destroyed irrigation infrastructure, crops, and pastures, and resulted in large-scale livestock deaths, leading to estimated damages and losses of US$57 million.4 Approximately 68,000 households in southern and central Somalia were directly affected. The resulting disruptions to household incomes and food supply were expected to push nearly 305,000 additional people into food stress or crisis-level food insecurity (IPC2+), including nearly 122,000 people into acute levels of food insecurity (IPC 3). 6. Both the Malawi FSRP and Somalia FSRP have activated their CERC to address emerging crisis situations. • Malawi has activated its CERC twice: first in November 2023, reallocating US$20 million in project funds, and again in November 2024, reallocating an additional US$30 million. The sectoral and institutional context of the two projects is described in the original FSRP Phase 3 project appraisal document (PAD). Agriculture is central to the two countries’ economies, employing 80 percent of Malawi’s population and 70 percent of Somalia’s. However, the sector remains mostly small-scale, rainfed, weakly diversified, unmechanized, data- and-evidence-poor, underserviced, low-input, and only semi-commercialized. It is also associated with extreme poverty, acute gender-based disparities, and vulnerability to climate change. Both Malawi and Somalia are among the most climate- vulnerable countries in the world according to the ND-GAIN index.5 Malawi’s 2021 Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) recognizes the risks weighing on the agrifood sector and its resources (especially water) and calls for investments in resilient value chains, climate-smart agriculture (CSA), the land tenure system, water catchment management, irrigation capacity and efficiency, the climate-proofing of infrastructure, knowledge and information systems, and risk management.6 Somalia, in recent years, has faced the compounding effects of record-breaking, multi-season drought, and a vicious cycle of land and resource degradation, on its agrifood systems and population. In a context of already weakened productive infrastructure and public services, limited human and institutional capacity for innovation, and female disenfranchisement, worsening climate shocks have essentially depleted the country’s coping capacity. In this context, building climate -resilient infrastructure is a top sectoral and national priority. B. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 9. The AF, like its parent projects, fully aligns with World Bank global, regional, and country priorities. The AF is highly relevant and directly linked to the World Bank’s mission to end extreme poverty and boost shared prosperity on a livable planet; and supports the World Bank’s priorities for Eastern and Southern Africa, which include strengthening climate resilience and food security. The AF also aligns with the Malawi Country Partnership Framework 2021 –2025 (CPF, Report No. 154505-MW, April 2, 2021) discussed by the World Bank Board of Directors in May 2021, especially focus area 2, which aims to foster human capital, job creation, sustainable livelihoods, and food security, and focus area 3, which aims to enhance resilience to shocks. Promoting agricultural commercialization and climate resilience are both central to this engagement. Similarly, the Somalia FSRP aligns with the Country Partnership Framework 2024–2028 (CPF, Report-187254- SO), discussed by the Board in February 2024, especially core High Level Outcome-3, which aims to build greater resilience to climatic and other Shocks. The AF also strongly responds to the CPF’s focus on improving Food and Nutrition Security through regional and national projects, enhancing pastoralists’ access to finance and food security systems. Project Description The primary purpose of this AF is to retroactively finance the emergency operations undertaken by both projects which were initially funded by diverting funds from planned project activities. While both projects used their CERC to respond to crises, they differ slightly in how these operations were financed. All three CERC operations were financed—at least initially—by reallocating resources from existing project components. • The Malawi FSRP’s first CERC operation (US$20 million) was initially financed by reallocating funds from other project components. It was later retroactively financed through the first AF, allowing the originally allocated components to recover their funds and proceed as planned. Once approved, the current, (second) AF will assume that retroactive financing role thereby freeing up US$20 million from the first AF to expand the project’s outreach as outlined below. • The second CERC under the Malawi FSRP’s (US$30 million), was similarly financed through the reallocation of project funds. However, unlike the first CERC operation, it was not retroactively financed. The current AF will provide that financing, enabling the recovery of funds diverted to this second emergency response. • In the Somalia FSRP, majority of the current AF will retroactively finance the CERC operation (US$28.28 million). The remaining US$1.72 million will be used to strengthen the government’s institutional capacity to coordinate and implement emergency food security responses. 15. Two sources of financing will be mobilized for this AF. • For the Malawi FSRP, the AF will consist of a US$50 million equivalent grant from the IDA Crisis Response Window Plus (CRW+); and a US$14.6 million equivalent grant from the country’s performance-based allocation (PBA) system under IDA. This funding is part of a broader regional emergency response package designed to support recovery from El Niño-related droughts and floods and to strengthen resilience to future climate shocks. The CRW+ grant was mobilized in late June 2024, in response to the Government of Malawi’s April 2024 appeal entitled “The National El- Niño Induced Prolonged Dry Spells and Floods Response Appeal�?. • For the Somalia FSRP, the AF will consist of a US$30 million equivalent IDA grant from the country’s PBA. 16. Component 5: Contingent Emergency Response Component (US$28.28 million equivalent IDA). The CERC was activated at the government’s request, resulting in an increase in its allocation increase from US$0 to US$28.28 million. The CERC operation was carried out from February 2024 to March 2025. To facilitate re-cropping in the flood-affected areas, inputs and small tools were provided to about 39,000 households to take advantage of the remaining flood waters before they receded completely. It also trained 6,000 farmers in climate-smart agriculture practices, deployed 20 tractors and 120 water pumps and provided technical assistance related to irrigation. It also assessed water points – in collaboration with the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations (FAO). Additionally, the operation engaged 334,455 people through community outreach activities, which focused on beneficiary selection and raising awareness about time-sensitive livestock treatment and vaccinations. More than 4.5 million animals received vital medical treatments, including antibiotics (Oxytetracycline, Punster, Tylosin), dewormers (Albendazole, Ivermectin), and supportive multivitamins, to enhance their overall health. In addition, 1.9 million small livestock (sheep and goats) were vaccinated against the Peste des Petits Ruminants, while 1.49 million were vaccinated against contagious caprine pleuropneumonia. 29. With technical assistance from FAO for infrastructure assessments, design, and monitoring, the CERC operation aims to rehabilitate damaged irrigation and flood control infrastructure to safeguard agricultural land from future flooding. Additionally, it will focus on rehabilitating feeder roads and market facilities. The government’s 2024 Contingency Emergency Response Action Plan (CERAP) has played a pivotal role in shaping the design of ongoing Somalia FSRP CERC operation. The institutional framework for its implementation is aligned with FSCP, which seeks to enhance coordination and ensure the effective implementation of emergency response activities in Somalia. 30. The AF project activities are the same in typology as identified under the parent project. The AF will bring about positive benefits such as increasing rural employment opportunities and improving rural livelihoods through improved agricultural productivity. Its positive environmental outcomes include resilient and sustainable water supply for productive food system and water and soil moisture conservation and soil health investments; however, AF activities have potential to lead to land acquisition and possibly result in loss of livelihoods, however, physical displacement is not anticipated. Support to Agricultural infrastructure investments, irrigation schemes, water points, market infrastructure, and small feeder roads and bridges pose E&S risks and impacts associated with project activities have been identified as the following: risks associated with land acquisition, poor labor conditions and child labor, OHS, waste generation, hazardous material management, noise and vibration, wastewater discharges and air quality as well as community H&S including transmission of communicable diseases, such as HIV/AIDS, COVID 19, etc. , sexual exploitation and abuse (SEA) and sexual harassment (SH) and other forms of gender-based violence (GBV). Other potential social risks could be related to: (i) operational concerns due to remoteness and insecurity, including monitoring and supervising social and environmental risks including grievance management; (ii) security issues in some of the project countries that may require the use of security services such as Somalia; and (iii) weak implementation capacity especially at grassroots level with limited functional structure and trained manpower. Other risks and impacts include those associated with the operational phase of these improvements/new construction/rehabilitation as well as with the subprojects of the matching grant program: beneficiary selection, child and forced labor, poor labor conditions, occupational health and safety, water and energy consumption, hygiene and food requirements, land, waste and water management, the use of pesticides, increase in water-borne diseases, manure and veterinary waste management. In addition, climate change impacts such as flooding and droughts are risks to be considered in the assessments and risk management plans.
