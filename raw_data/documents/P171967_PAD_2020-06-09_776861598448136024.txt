Page 7 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Regional Context 1. The Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA) region includes some of the fastest growing economies in Africa. It stretches from Tunisia to Eswatini including 21 countries and 557 million people uniting for “economic prosperity through regional integration”. The Regional Economic Community was initially established in 1981 as a Preferential Trade Area and transformed in 1994 with its focus being the formation of a large economic and trading unit capable of overcoming some of the barriers that are faced by individual states. Several COMESA countries, notably Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), Djibouti, Ethiopia, Kenya, and Rwanda were among the fastest growing countries in the world with economic growth between 5 and 10 percent during the 2016 and 2017 period. 2. These gains are threatened by the Coronavirus Disease (COVID-19) pandemic. In the short-term, economic growth in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) is expected to decline from 2.4 percent in 2019 to -2.1 to -5.1 percent in 2020, the first recession in the region in 25 years. Additional scenarios in Eastern and Southern Africa show a potential decline in Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth between 4.5 percent (optimistic scenario) and around 7 percent (pessimistic scenario) compared with a no-COVID scenario (Africa Pulse April 2020). The pandemic is affecting countries with strong value chain participation (e.g. Ethiopia, Kenya, Tanzania), and reducing foreign financing flows in the form of lower foreign direct investment, foreign aid, remittances, tourism revenues, as well as capital flight. The direct impact of COVID-19 on economic activity from a wider spread of the virus in the region and disruptions caused by containment and mitigation measures imposed by governments are severely affecting economic activity. The COVID-19 crisis is also contributing to increased food insecurity as currencies are weakening and prices of staple foods are rising in many parts of Africa. The COMESA region includes many countries affected by Fragility, Conflict and Violence (FCV) in the Horn of Africa and in its Southern part. 3. The pandemic will increase fiscal deficits in a context of heightened debt vulnerabilities. Dwindling government revenues are causing a fiscal crunch. The problem is compounded by the larger and riskier debt positions and an increase in external borrowing costs. Before the pandemic, public debt vulnerabilities were a growing concern. Nearly half of COMESA member countries were in debt distress or in high risk of debt distress. The total debt outstanding grew on aggregate from 31.6 to 63.6 percent of GDP between 2012 and 2019, as a result of large public infrastructure spending and shift from concessional debt toward non-Paris Club bilateral lenders and commercial creditors. 4. Regional coordination and sowing the seeds for future resilience will enhance the policy response (Africa Pulse 2020). Regional coordination would mitigate the economic fallout, and pre-COVID priorities in the COMESA region included increasing intraregional trade (from 7 percent of total COMESA trade in 2017), building regional markets in energy and digital inclusion. Building long-term economic resilience involves policies supporting sustainable investment in resilient infrastructure in energy, transport, water and urban development. The need for supporting the provision of long-term funding to private infrastructure to support the economic recovery, is therefore higher than ever of account of the COVID-19 crisis. B. Sectoral and Institutional Context 5. COMESA countries suffer from ailing infrastructure. As at 2014, the region was estimated to have a US$60 billion funding gap for infrastructure i.e. US$28.4 billion for railways, airports, ports, roads and border posts, US$31 billion for Page 8 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) electricity and US$630 million for information and communication technology (ICT). Deficits like these have a clear impact on competitiveness, growth and poverty reduction. Narrowing SSA’s infrastructure gap could potentially have a large impact on growth and poverty reduction by increasing GDP per capita for an estimated 1.7 percentage points per year. 6. The largest infrastructure deficit is to be found in the power sector. The total installed capacity for electric power in the 21 COMESA countries is about 90,800 megawatts compared to Brazil with over 150,000 megawatts (but one third of COMESA population). Effective generation of electricity in COMESA is less than the installed capacity by 20-30 percent due to drought, lack of maintenance or rehabilitation, and general system losses of electricity in transmission and distributions that are higher than the international average of 12 percent. It is estimated that such energy deficit causes about 2 percent loss of GDP growth in most COMESA countries. More than 640 million Africans – over 60 percent of the region’s population – have no access to energy, and more than half of these are in COMESA countries. 7. Heavy borrowing for infrastructure had risen sovereign debt levels pre-COVID 19. SSA’s debt to GDP ratio rose sharply – the median ratio increased from 35 percent in 2010 to over 50 percent in 2018 and public expenditure for infrastructure has been a key contributor. Ethiopia’s government gross debt rose from 39.6 percent of GDP in 2010 to 61 percent in 2018 under borrowing for infrastructure (e.g. hydropower, railways, industrial zones). Uganda’s public debt climbed by 22 percent in the fiscal year 2017/18 with borrowings to develop hydropower and transport projects. Kenya borrowed in total US$4.7 billion to finance the Standard Gauge Railway that is 8 percent of its public debt stock. The moratorium on IDA countries’ debt repayments from official bilateral creditors announced in April 2020 will provide much needed relief to fight the COVID-19 pandemic. But structural issues remain on sovereign debt sustainability, and on how to finance infrastructure needs. 8. Given the high and rising debt to GDP levels and the large gap that still exists in infrastructure provision, it is imperative to develop alternate sources of financing, enhance capacity to structure projects and improve the enabling environment. Private investment into Africa’s infrastructure remains low, averaging only US$6 billion/year. This level is limited by the lack of bankable projects for the private sector. Banks could do more, since the ratio of private sector credit to GDP in SSA is only 17 percent as of 2018 (47 percent in South Asia and 54 percent in Latin America and Caribbean), however they are constrained by international rules on assets and liabilities matching. Therefore, new long-term finance sources are needed to help bridge the infrastructure finance gap. 9. Attracting private finance in infrastructure requires a comprehensive approach. This approach (Figure 1) includes five pillars: (i) an enabling environment to mobilize private capital; (ii) sound public debt management; (iii) the origination of bankable projects; (iv) long-term finance; and (v) risk mitigation instruments. This integrated approach has been successfully piloted in Kenya, but it took several years, and results are only starting to emerge. Kenya had a track record with Public-Private Partnerships (PPP) in the energy sector, which helped expand them to other sectors. Initiated between 2013 - 2015, seven PPP projects prepared under the Infrastructure Finance and PPP operation (P121019) are now in active procurement. Kenyan pension funds have been trained in project finance for the past two years under the Financial Sector Strengthening Initiative (FIRST Kenya Infrastructure Finance - P163792) and fifteen leading pension funds representing US$2 billion in assets have formed a consortium to look for investment opportunities. World Bank provided technical assistance (TA) to the Government of Kenya to develop a robust framework for assessing and managing fiscal commitment and contingent liabilities (FCCL) in PPPs. Issues in sovereign debt management are being addressed through the Development Policy Operations (DPO) series and ongoing TA. The imperative for action on supporting the provision of infrastructure finance has expanded in light of the COVID-19 economic fallout. The pandemic is expected to dry up long-term finance. As economic activity dips, financial inflows to banks decrease, interbank markets tighten, risk aversion rises, and commercial banks ration their available liquidity to short-term lending. Thus, it is expected that long-term finance from commercial lenders, which was already scarce, will significantly dry up in the coming months, and will particularly affect the infrastructure finance market. An initiative that supports the long-term finance market for infrastructure, and provide comfort to commercial lenders, is therefore needed even more urgently. Infrastructure investments can be a driver to quicker economic recovery, both in terms of sustaining investment activity and creating jobs in the near term, but also in terms of building an essential foundation for longer term recovery and growth. The World Bank’s Africa Region “Framework for Operational Response to the COVID-19 Pandemic and Global Crisis” notes the need to invest in faster recovery with structural reforms to spur new private investment, and investment in resilience such as climate smart infrastructure. 11. The proposed operation seeks to address the long-term finance gap through a regional approach focused on private financing of infrastructure, working with country engagements on the other pillars. It also serves as a response to the COVID-19 pandemic through contributing to economic recovery efforts. The operation seeks to build financial and technical capacity in a regional institution to provide a larger and sustainable source of infrastructure finance, while also working on improving the enabling environment. Mobilizing private investment into infrastructure is time and resource intensive, complex, requires multiple and coordinated projects, and a conscious effort to build specialist institutions and capacity on infrastructure. As a result, it cannot be easily replicated in every COMESA country. Hence, the objective is to leverage the Eastern and Southern African Trade and Development Bank (TDB), and the COMESA Secretariat, to initiate some of these changes through a regional approach. Not every country can develop their own infrastructure development bank and PPP institutions (Box 1). In the spirit of promoting private sector investment, the Project will not fund any sovereign borrowing for infrastructure or other activities. Page 10 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) Box 1: Why a regional approach to develop infrastructure finance? This operation proposes a regional approach to expand long-term finance to selected infrastructure sectors through TDB and COMESA Secretariat. Going regional presents the following benefits: (a) Economies of scale: TDB’s 22 member states have a total population of 643 million with a combined GDP of US$855 billion (World Bank 2017). It is more effective for IDA to provide infrastructure finance through TDB in all these countries rather than going through each country individually. This has economies of scale and allows financing impactful sub-projects that would be too small for IDA to support as standalone. (b) No impact on government’s finances: 10 out of 22 member countries of TDB are in debt distress or high risk of debt distress. Since IDA will lend directly to TDB, it will not have any impact on the debt position of TDB’s members. This will allow infrastructure investments to be realized in these countries. (c) Cross-border impact: About one-third of SSA countries are landlocked and crucially dependent on their neighbors for access to global markets. TDB extends development capital and services to advance regional growth and integration in Eastern and Southern Africa. As of June 2019, TDB had facilitated approx. US$390 million of intra-sub-regional trade in its Project and Infrastructure portfolio. The facility will also be an opportunity to incentivize cross-border projects. (d) Leverage private finance and develop local capital markets: IDA funds will leverage the private funds that TDB is mobilizing in the capital markets and through its intermediation activity. TDB is a regular bond issuer in foreign currency and has issued in local currency in Uganda, Kenya and Tanzania. TDB generally lends to private infrastructure projects alongside commercial banks and requires sponsor’s equity. (e) Specialization: Infrastructure finance requires specialist expertise. It is more efficient and effective for IDA to strengthen the capacity of TDB and COMESA secretariat, institutions already involved in infrastructure finance, than to build such capacity in each country individually. (f) Demonstration effect: While the number of sub-projects funded by the facility will be limited, they will be sought to be selected based on their demonstration effects such as: strengthening the PPP framework in a country; PPP/private investment in a new sector; mobilization of local currency; mobilization of a new category of investors such as institutional investors. 12. Several COMESA countries are making efforts to mobilize private investment into infrastructure. (i) Ethiopia: At the inception stage, Ethiopia approved a PPP Law in January 2018 with World Bank support through two TA projects in the past three years i.e. Support for Instituting a PPP Framework (P157623) and PPP Capacity Building (P168405). As part of the Ethiopia Macroeconomic, Fiscal and Debt Dialogue (P162271), the World Bank is providing capacity building in the assessment, management, and monitoring of fiscal risks and contingent liabilities with a focus on Infrastructure PPPs and State-owned enterprise related PPPs. (ii) Uganda: The PPP Act was approved in 2015 and an operational PPP Unit is in place. Uganda has a significant portfolio of Independent Power Producers in the Energy sector. In the roads sector, a number of expressway PPP projects are under consideration, the most advanced being the Kampala – Jinja Expressway supported by International Finance Corporation (IFC) advisory. World Bank TA, Mobilizing Long-Term Financing for Infrastructure (P162655), strengthened the institutional framework for PPPs and sought to identify potential demonstration transactions for institutional investor engagement. (iii) Tanzania: The Tanzania PPP Support Program (P149535) is helping the country to re-establish its PPP program, which is anchored on a PPP Policy of 2009 and a PPP Act of 2010. The program provides advisory services to identify potential projects, with an emphasis on municipal PPPs. (iv) Malawi: The advisory services Mobilizing Long-Term Finance into Infrastructure (P169383) provides advice to the Government of Malawi on mobilizing long-term private finance for infrastructure. The World Page 11 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) Bank Group (WBG) is supporting the Mpatamanga hydro dam. (v) Mozambique: A PPP framework that regulates the issuance of guarantees and on-lending exists in the country and is largely being enforced. The World Bank team is also working with the Ministry of Finance in developing a credit risk management framework. The WBG is supporting the Temane gas Independent Power Producer. 13. While country situations vary, the main bottlenecks to infrastructure finance are generally: (i) the lack of enabling legal and regulatory frameworks for private sector participation in infrastructure finance. Some of the existing laws in the region have been seen as lacking flexibility for wider private sector engagement modalities beyond conventional PPPs, cumbersome with numerous layers of review and approval steps; (ii) limited institutional, technical and financial capacity of the public sector to screen and generate a bankable pipeline of PPP projects, including proper identification and management of fiscal commitments and contingent liabilities; and (iii) protracted public procurement processes and delays in government decision making within key decision points of the PPP lifecycle that further exacerbate an already long gestation period of infrastructure development. 14. TDB is a commercially driven development bank. Between 2016 and 2019, the asset size has grown from US$4.2 to US$6.7 billion and the loan book from US$3.4 to nearly US$5 billion. Non-performing loans have decreased from 2.85 to 2.3 percent of total loans. TDB has a strong capital base with a capital adequacy ratio of 36.1 percent as of December 2019, and a callable capital of US$1.5 billion partially guaranteed by the Lloyds market. TDB achieved investment grade status in June 2018 with Baa3 and BBB- from Moody’s and Global Credit Ratings, respectively, which is higher than most countries in the region. This rating reflects TDB’s sound risk management policies which have helped to improve its asset quality; credit enhancement undertakings with investment grade rated insurers; and the diversity of its funding/support base including equity from shareholders and debt (including debt capital market). Please refer to Annex 2 for a detailed analysis of TDB. 15. TDB is refocusing its intervention towards project and infrastructure finance. While its business has traditionally been dominated by lending to sovereigns, TDB’s corporate plan (2018-2022) underscores project and infrastructure finance as one strategic pillar i.e. “… the Bank will seek to support infrastructure in Member States with regional effects, notably in the areas of power, transport and ICT including initiatives that advance the East African Power Pool and Southern African Power pool formations, among others.” The project and infrastructure finance portfolio grew from 27 percent of the total loan portfolio in 2016 to 37 percent in June 2019. TDB has recruited six senior infrastructure finance experts in the past two years and expanded its business origination capacity through the regional coverage banking teams and is keen to expand further in this strategic direction. At present, TDB has 28 infrastructure projects in its portfolio in the sectors of energy, water and sanitation, transport and logistics, and telecommunications; this includes corporate finance transactions as well as PPPs. 16. Working with TDB is one way to mobilize private finance into infrastructure in COMESA countries, using a regional specialized entity. There are no other similar institutions to scale-up infrastructure finance in the COMESA geographic area, and this operation creates a platform for other partners to come in. TDB’s current pipeline on Project and Infrastructure Finance shows that on average, one dollar of TDB’s financing would leverage 5.7 dollars of private capital and debt. TDB has been working on infrastructure and PPP alongside 20 development finance institutions (DFIs) like African Development Bank (AfDB), European Investment Bank (EIB), Commonwealth Development Corporation, United States EXIM Bank. Past projects with high development impact include the Lake Turkana Wind Power in Kenya and the Coral South Floating Liquified Natural Gas in Mozambique (“infrastructure deal of the year” 2019 in African Banking Awards). TDB has co-financed three transactions with the IFC. Page 12 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) 17. COMESA Secretariat’s mandate includes the expansion of cross-border infrastructure. COMESA is a free trade area with a strategy to promote regional integration, but intra-regional trade remains structurally low. Trade is not only about goods, but covers also movement of services, capital and labor, which are constrained by the low level of infrastructure development. As a result, COMESA has recognized infrastructure development as a priority, and its Secretariat is working on a conducive enabling environment via: (i) the development of model policies and regulations and (ii) the development of priority regional physical infrastructure – for Transport, ICT, and Energy; and (iii) the development of legal and institutional frameworks. C. Relevance to Higher Level Objectives 18. The proposed Project is aligned with the WBG’s Africa Regional Integration and Cooperation Assistance Strategy (2018) which aims to (i) generate economic dynamism along regional economic corridors and to (ii) develop functioning regional markets in identified priority sectors. These two strategic priorities include leveraging private sector for financing infrastructure, harmonizing policies to promote trade and supporting priority regional energy generation and transmission links. Attracting private investment and strengthening regional financial institutions are key actions to achieving the strategy. 19. The proposed Project complements the Horn of Africa Initiative (HoAI) supported by the AfDB, European Union (EU) and WBG across all its pillars: (I) Regional Infrastructure Networks (Transport, Energy, Digital) development; (II) Trade and Economic Integration; (III) Building Resilience; (IV) Strengthening Human Capital. The HoAI supports the establishment of a conducive investment climate to attract private investments for the syndication of financing. Under Pillar I, the development of critical regional infrastructure will enhance regional power trade among the countries belonging to the East Africa Power Pool (EAPP), of which the majority of COMESA are members. Critical road and telecom corridors will further support regional integration with shared benefits stemming from economies of scale. 20. Infrastructure development has a positive impact on poverty reduction and shared prosperity. The main channels are through (i) growth and (ii) the provision of services to improve people’s lives (Box 2). Narrowing SSA’s infrastructure quantity and quality gap could potentially have a large impact on growth. For instance, growth in GDP per capita in SSA region would increase by an estimated 1.7 percentage points per year if it were to close the gap with the regional median of each infrastructure indicator (Africa Pulse 2017). By avoiding increases in countries’ debt and mobilizing private capital for infrastructure the operation will support the conditions for broad-based recovery in line with the corporate WBG response on COVID-19.1 21. The Project is aligned with the soft prioritization filters of IDA 18 SUF. It supports the IDA 18 themes of Jobs and Economic Transformation (JET) and Governance and Institutions. A framework has been developed to prioritize projects that create jobs, have positive cross-border impacts (trade and investment), and deploy new technologies (Annex 4). Regarding institution-building, the Project aims to build the capacity of TDB to become a regional specialized institution for infrastructure finance. Through TDB, the Project will mobilize private finance into infrastructure (target of US$975 million in addition to the IDA SUF credit). It will aim to build a regional market for off-grid energy with an enabling regulatory environment. Page 13 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) Box 2: Infrastructure investment and poverty reduction There are two main channels through which infrastructure can reduce poverty - through (i) growth and (ii) the provision of services to improve people’s lives. Sustainable infrastructure investment is crucial for medium- and long-term economic growth, and the adequate provision of infrastructure-related services is essential to improve people’s lives. The quality of infrastructure provided matters as much as providing increased access to it. Evidence about the impact of infrastructure on inequality is mixed. Some studies found positive welfare impacts with pro-poor interventions (Bangladesh, Bolivia, Argentina), while others found that richer households benefitted most (Nicaragua, Bangladesh). Expanding access to, and the quality of, economic and social infrastructure services (e.g. transport, energy, water, sanitation, telecommunications, health, and education) can improve productivity in various sectors of the economy and have a positive impact on people’s quality of life. A study conducted in India on the health gains from piped water showed that for children under age five, the prevalence and duration of diarrhea was lower in households with piped water, compared to households without piped water. Electricity improves households’ quality of life by supplying power for appliances that make life more comfortable, healthy, and safe; transport infrastructure reduces travel time, and improves comfort and safety; and digital development, notably broadband, can improve economic opportunities of the poor while helping achieve key development outcomes such as health coverage and women’s empowerment. SSA has some inherent characteristics that may enhance the potential role of infrastructure for its economic development— notably, the large number of landlocked countries, which are home to a major proportion of the region’s total population (about 40 percent), and the remoteness of most of the region’s economies from global market centers. SSA’s geographic disadvantages result in high transport costs that hinder intra- and inter-regional trade. Limited openness to trade is the main factor behind the stylized fact that, ceteris paribus, landlocked countries tend to grow slower than others. Narrowing SSA’s infrastructure quantity and quality gap could potentially have a large impact on growth. For instance, growth in GDP per capita for the region would increase by an estimated 1.7 percentage points per year if it were to close the gap with the regional median of each infrastructure indicator (in telecommunications, energy, transport, and water and sanitation). Eliminating the quantity gap would deliver 1.2 percentage points higher growth per capita per year; catching up in quality would bring about 0.5 percentage point higher growth per year. The growth effects of the quantity of infrastructure vary by sector, with the largest growth benefits obtained by narrowing the gap in electric power–generating capacity, at 0.7 percentage point higher per year. For quality, improving road quality provides the largest benefits. Gender tag. The empirical literature suggests that inadequate access to clean, affordable, and reliable sources of energy can broaden gender disparities in terms of health, income generation, and access to information. For instance, the high reliance of households in SSA on wood fuels and charcoal for cooking contributes to poor health outcomes among women and girls (World Bank, 2013). The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that about 2 million people die prematurely every year from exposure to smoke from traditional cookstoves and open fires; this is about twice the number of people who die every year from malaria. Similarly, electrification projects can save women time Page 23 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) of 1 to 4 hours daily in cooking, fuel collection and food processing. Having more time gives women opportunity to carry out income-generating activities (Dutta, 2005). Consequently, better access to electricity opens new, and often distinct, opportunities for women and men to generate income while improving health and potentially poverty outcomes. The RIFF includes the following activities to reduce the gender gaps in access to electricity:  Under Component 1, the RIFF will prioritize sub-projects incorporating gender considerations into project design such as female employment and gender-sensitive infrastructure.  Under Component 2, TDB will include an assessment of potential debt recipients’ gender strategy as part of its screening and diligence process, giving preference to those businesses that demonstrate strong gender engagement in their marketing and monitoring efforts, or demonstrate a clear strategy for developing this. This is meant to identify borrower firms who are committed to reducing the gender gap in energy access.  TDB will also require them to track and report gender-related metrics (such as sales to female-headed households), which will contribute to addressing the data gaps on gender issues in energy access;  Business development services in the TA to help SHS companies’ expansion into underserved markets will include specific support on gender aspects as part of successful market entry strategies. This is intended to support the business case for advancing energy access for women at the household and firm levels.  To measure the reduction in gaps, the results framework includes two indicators: (i) women provided with new or improved electricity service (750,000 or 50 percent of all beneficiaries); (ii) female-headed households provided with access to electricity through SHS (75,000 or 20 percent of beneficiary households). IV. PROJECT APPRAISAL SUMMARY A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis 55. Financial Analysis. The financial analysis for subprojects under the RIFF Project will be based on TDBs existing appraisal framework and credit policy which is guided by: (a) technical cost estimation (capital, maintenance, operating); (b) demand and revenue projections; and (c) financial sustainability. 56. Each sub-project appraised by TDB comes with a feasibility study with a financial model evaluating Internal Rate of Return (IRR), Return on Investment, and the Debt Service Coverage Ratio through project lifecycle costs (Capex and Opex) and revenues generated. All sub-projects were found to yield attractive financial returns, and hence their implementation should be of material interest to both banks and the sponsors. Based on the financial analysis of individual sub-projects, the overall pipeline of potential RIFF projects will be aggregated to produce an overall infrastructure sector Financial Internal Rate of Return estimate. Page 24 of 75 The World Bank Regional Infrastructure Finance Facility (RIFF) Project (P171967) 57. Economic Analysis. A framework approach has been developed for selected sub-projects under Component 1 which takes into consideration the monetary capital and operation costs as well as the project benefits (economic but also environmental) to compute a Net Present Value (NPV). The economic appraisal undergoes sensitivity analyses to further test project viability. This economic appraisal framework will only be applied for large energy projects (e.g. over US$30 million) as the model is well-tested. The framework approach consists of two methodologies:  Cost-benefit analysis: Which monetizes all major benefits and all costs associated with a sub-project so that they can be directly compared with each other as well as to reasonable alternatives to the proposed intervention; TDB and the World Bank employed the cost-benefit analysis framework to assess the impact of an energy project currently in the pipeline. The methodology, analysis and results are presented in Annex 5 and show an Economic Internal Rate of Return of 48.2 percent.  Cost-effectiveness analysis: In cases where cost-benefit analysis is not feasible due to data or technical constraints, TDB would evaluate eligible projects by comparing how the cost of the desired project outputs under the project compare to other options for providing similar results. Through this methodology, TDB would not attempt monetizing all anticipated benefits deriving from the sub-project or the alternatives considered. The applicability would be constrained by the need to make comparisons across alternative approaches that deliver roughly similar bundles of outcomes and benefits, for instance comparing costs and expected outcomes of a given sub-project in the timeline against comparable sub-projects which have been completed through similar service delivery methods. Political and governance (substantial): Eastern and Southern Africa includes fragile zones (Horn of Africa, Great Lakes). IDA has not worked with TDB before, but TDB is receiving financing from various DFIs which provide comfort on the governance. 67. Macroeconomic (substantial): TDB operates in low-income countries with weak credit ratings. Fiscal risk in its countries of operation is high. The impact of the COVID-19 on TDB’s member countries is expected to be significant. The Project will focus on private sector infrastructure projects where commercial viability is well established and will make long-term finance available for infrastructure at a time when long-term commercial funding sources are expected to dry up. 68. Sector strategies and policies (substantial): Infrastructure in SSA is expected to be adversely affected by the COVID-19, with the dry-up of external financial flows to sustain projects and heightened fiscal and debt vulnerabilities. As a result, the delivery of infrastructure investment is expected to be delayed. This would affect particularly Component 1. On the other hand, it is expected that infrastructure investment will play a key role to support economic recovery. 69. Technical design of the Project (substantial): Historically, a lack of bankable infrastructure pipeline has led to early stage low level drawdowns, overall low utilization and disappointing results in several similar facilities in the sector, at country level, for both development and commercial banks. Lack of an immediate pipeline, especially with TDB’s limited experience in the off-grid (solar) sector, is an area that requires close review. The pipeline provided by TDB shows a strong deal generation, and it is expected that synergies will be built between this operation and IDA country programs in a post-COVID recovery phase (for instance TDB is looking at PPP projects whose feasibility studies were prepared under the IDA Infrastructure Finance and Public Private Partnership (IFPPP) operation). The Financing Agreement includes a list of financial covenants to ensure that TDB maintain an appropriate level of capitalization, asset quality and insurance of callable capital. 70. As a result, TDB has limited experience of implementing sub-projects involving physical and/or economic involuntary resettlement or those with impacts on Indigenous Peoples. The project will use an integrated approach to develop a regional off-grid energy market with: (a) financing for stand-alone solar system businesses to be implemented by TDB; and (b) enabling environment and capacity building activities to be implemented by COMESA Secretariat. 2. About 267 million people or 53 million households in COMESA IDA countries have not been connected to any electricity source. On average, the cost of connecting a new consumer through grid is about US$2,000, and this cost increases significantly for geographically isolated consumers. Electricity utilities do not consider grid expansion as a financially and economically viable investment to electrify consumers in many rural and dispersed geographic locations at the current electricity tariff levels. Most power utilities are also facing financing constraints to even maintain the existing grid network. Private sector participation in grid electricity distribution business has therefore been mainly limited within urban and in high concentrated load areas in few countries. When the cost of grid extension becomes prohibitive, mini-grids or standalone off-grid solar systems become a more cost-effective solution. Mini-grids are more likely to be viable in areas of high population density or energy-intensive economic activity, while standalone solutions are more cost effective in more sparsely populated, or poorer areas. 3. Access to finance for stand-alone solar system companies is a major challenge. Commercial lenders are reluctant to lend to SMEs in general, and the solar equipment and service companies are relatively new and hence less attractive. The sector is nascent and evolving rapidly, resulting in a perception of high risk due to lack of sufficient operating track record. Many of the companies cannot meet lenders’ collateral requirements. Local currency debt is even more challenging, as domestic interest rates are too high for solar companies in many countries in the region. Many off-grid solar companies rely substantially on donor financing, impact investors. Access to finance all along the supply chain requires engagement from the private sector for developing and expanding the stand-alone solar systems market. Distributors need working capital to hold adequate product stocks. Companies that provide PAYGo platforms, offering consumers the option to pay in installments over time, have made off-grid solar products affordable to large new swaths of the population, but require long-term financing to help bridge the gap between paying for stocks and collecting revenues from consumers. 4. The proposed project will provide debt financing to SHS SMEs operating in COMESA region and TDB member countries. Investor interest so far has heavily focused on the largest companies and the most established off-grid markets. In addition, commercial investors’ return expectations have put pressure on SHS companies to prioritize more affluent customers over expanding their reach to underserved markets. Loans made to off-grid solar companies under RIFF component will therefore use the comparatively low cost of capital under SUF to encourage the larger off-grid businesses to accelerate expansion to new markets or target local businesses in underserved countries that might otherwise struggle to access regional capital markets The proposed project will provide this support to the private sector in the form of working capital loans or long- term loans through TDB – an experienced and responsible FI. As a result, the COMESA objective of creating a regional market and free movement of goods and people has been far from realized in the off-grid sector. Unfavorable business environments, trade policies, and customs and tax regimes deter entry to these markets. Most countries are subsidizing grid electricity tariffs while taxing stand-alone solar system products; likewise, most countries have no approved technical standard for solar PV technologies developing their own standards, which fragments the market further. Having regional standards on QA framework for solar products could protect consumers from low-quality products and attract more companies in the region. Other challenges have been lengthy and costly customs clearance procedures, lack of consumer protection, and lack of a regional licensing or standards framework to regulate the products and models that are being imported and disseminated in the market. In this context, the project seeks to create a regional market in the COMESA economic block to benefit the region by overcoming the country-specific barriers to off-grid investment. 14. A regional approach offers many benefits. RIFF is designed as a regional program to help seed the regional market, create the necessary supporting ecosystem and enabling environment, and attract solar companies and investors into the region. This will create interests and momentum in the countries to put in place the right policy and enabling infrastructure to take full advantage of the regional developments. Riding on the interests from the industry and enticing them with the bigger regional market, RIFF aims to create the necessary ‘pull’ to get the companies and investors interested in the region. For instance, agreement on quality standards for solar products among the COMESA countries can benefit the private sector solar PV product manufacturers as they get access to a large contiguous market, which will result in targeted research and development and manufacturing and, hence, significant cost reduction. Only a regional program can promote the concept of regional standards and support the countries to adopt similar standards to help create a common market. Regional agreement on important trade issues such as taxes and duties would facilitate easy movements of products across countries making them more accessible and affordable to consumers. This will increase trade in between the countries, creating jobs and incentive structures to support a sustainable market. 15. This Annex puts forward a draft methodological framework for the selection and prioritization of sub- projects under the RIFF project. The RIFF will prioritize projects with significant development impact and high private sector mobilization potential and will have stringent E&S eligibility criteria to avoid E&S risks that cannot be easily mitigated. Proposed Methodology for the RIFF 2. Drawing on Darwin et. al. (2016) 10, the agreed approach consists of a multiple-criteria decision-making framework to assist in the selection, prioritization and sequencing of sub-project proposals that would be funded by the facility during the Project implementation phase. It is expected that the application of the framework provides decision support to TDB management so it can commit capital to technically feasible, as well as profitable, ventures. 3. The methodology is sequenced in two phases: an eligibility phase based on two mandatory criteria: i) bankability/ fiscal exposure and private sector mobilization potential and ii) E&S risk compliance. A second “sub-project prioritization” phase applies a multi-criteria decision support tool for eligible projects identified in phase I, considering project specificities and expected outcomes along the following four dimensions: i) Project benefits and contribution to corporate commitments ii) Financial criteria iii) Strategic alignment iv) Technical consideration 4. The framework utilizes existing and accessible data available from both project specific and national/regional sources to systematically compare projects, whilst promoting the building of analytical capacity and data for more extensive economic analysis and technical deliberation within TDB. 5. Employing this method allows (i) to structure the decision space when capacity and information is limited but nevertheless enough for systematic comparison; (ii) to encourage better appraisal by fostering discussion of key decision factors for which project data should be improved or gathered in the future. This approach does not deliver a definitive list of projects for selection or replaces widely used methodologies in project appraisal, particularly Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA), but rather complements them. 6. This framework allows for two critical policy choices: i) the selection of criteria by which alternatives will be assessed, and ii) the weighting of criteria. The selection of criteria is essential for assessing each project’s risk levels, costs, and value addition, as well as expected performance with respect to prioritized development goals for the sector, country, or region. Criteria weighting is also a policy choice. The economic analysis of eligible RIFF interventions will be undertaken at the appraisal stage of the sub-project. A framework approach has been developed for selected subprojects under Component 1 which takes into consideration the monetary capital and operation costs as well as the project benefits (economic but also environmental) to compute a NPV. The economic appraisal undergoes sensitivity analyses to further test project viability. This economic appraisal framework will only be applied for large energy projects (e.g. over US$30 million) as the model is well-tested. The framework approach essentially consists of two methodologies:  Cost-benefit analysis: Which monetizes all major benefits and all costs associated with a subproject so that they can be directly compared with each other as well as to reasonable alternatives to the proposed intervention; and  Cost-effectiveness analysis: In cases where cost-benefit analysis is not feasible due to data or technical constraints, TDB would evaluate eligible projects by comparing how the cost of the desired project outputs under the project compare to other options for providing similar results. Through this methodology, TDB would not attempt monetizing all anticipated benefits deriving from the sub project or the alternatives considered. The applicability would be constrained by the need to make comparisons across alternative approaches that deliver roughly similar bundles of outcomes and benefits, for instance comparing costs and expected outcomes of a given subproject in the timeline against comparable subprojects which have been completed through similar service delivery methods. Box 6.1: Case of FDN, Colombia FDN is a financial corporation with a government-DFI-private sector shareholding that specializes in infrastructure project finance and structuring, as well as a provider of project support which includes guarantees and subordinated financing. Its inception was a reorganizing of an energy financing agency, so that it could widen its mandate to cover other sectors but also be more catalytic in terms of mobilization of private sector financing. This initiative mainly stemmed from an ambitious US$20 billion new road PPP program in a history of Colombia which had an erratic track record in PPPs. When the Government of Colombia created the FDN it took on the task of securing the participation of other actors as shareholders so as to strengthen FDN financially—and thus enable it to support the country’s infrastructure—but also to enhance its technical capacity and autonomy to provide finance, consulting, and project structure products and services. To this end, the game changer was in 2014 when the IFC (US$70 million) and Corporacion Andina de Fomento (CAF) (US$50 million) multilaterals first capitalized the corporation to a total sum of US$20 million. Later the Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation (SMBC), a Japanese bank that is a world expert in financing large projects, was brought on board. Currently, the IFC holds 15.7 percent of the FDN’s shares, the CAF and the SMBC hold 8.18 percent each, and the Government of Colombia is the majority shareholder with 67.5 percent. FDN's capitalization helped transform it into a key entity to provide financing, support the mobilization of resources, and promote infrastructure development. The stakeholders have also transferred their high standards of corporate governance to the FDN, which guarantees that the decisions it makes, whether in terms of finance or consulting and structuring, are technically grounded. As a result, any project that enjoys FDN backing must be commercially and financially viable and receive a positive vote from at least one of the representatives of the multilaterals on the board. This factor is not only important to the infrastructure sector’s development and sustainability but also contributes to boosting the confidence of market stakeholders. The IFC, CAF, and SMBC’s participation strengthens its technical capacity to provide finance and structuring solutions focused on developing infrastructure in Colombia. These entities contribute know how and represent a national and international seal of approval. Without a doubt they provide important backing for the FDN’s objectives. It is estimated that FDN expects to have closed financing for 17 projects, worth US$8.4billion, of the 30 planned under the US$20 billion 4G Program within 4 years from the program first began. Rating Agencies view of FDN: “Having multilateral lending organizations, such as IFC, CAF, and SMBC, as shareholders, has helped raise FDN's governance standards. Improvements include the reduction of government officials on the board and the appointment of an independent director as board chairman. Protocols and procedures to carry out E&S risk management functions: TDB ESMS includes clear process flow and procedures that are linked to credit and investment cycles, which conforms to the international best practice. TDB’s E&S procedures also include adequate management programs. From the review of their ESMS, TDB seems to have clear steps for E&S screening of transactions which includes initial assessment of a transaction against the exclusion list and categorization. 4. The E&S team members sit on various credit committees to ensure E&S factors are considered at transaction onboarding and that risks are appropriately categorized and weighted (also best practice). 5. E&S Staffing: TDB’s E&S unit is mapped to the Compliance and Risk Management Department which was created in 2012 to enhance TDB’s E&S risk assessment and to ensure the E&S risk assessment and management is fully integrated into the bank-wide Enterprise Management System. The unit is composed of an ESMS Specialist (E&S expert), ESMS Coordinator, and ESMS Manager. In addition, TDB’s E&S policy emphasizes the requirement of engaging external E&S experts during due diligence and monitoring phases of category 1 subprojects.
