Page 12 of 124 The World Bank Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (P175594) I. STRATEGIC CONTEXT A. Country Context 1. The COVID-19 pandemic translated into rising poverty in Côte d’Ivoire, one of the fastest growing economies in the West African Economic and Monetary Union (WAEMU) before 2020. With Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth of 6.9 percent in 2019 (3.5 percent in per capita terms), Côte d’Ivoire continued to be one of the fastest growing economies in Sub-Saharan Africa, benefitting from strong domestic demand and investments. With the onset of COVID-19 in March 2020, GDP growth slowed down to 2.0 percent. Economic growth is expected to gradually recover to pre-crisis levels to reach 6.5 percent by 2023.1 The global pandemic has led to significant income losses for more than 70 percent of Ivorian households across all economic sectors and income levels (with a higher share of female-headed households reporting losses), worsening the living conditions of already vulnerable households. National poverty increased during the pandemic, reversing the positive trends observed between 2015 and 2019. 2 Poverty is estimated to have reached 41.5 percent in October 2020 (up from 39.4 percent in 2018).3 While GDP growth has picked up again in 2021 (6.7 percent or 3.5 percent in per capita terms), influation has reached a 10-year high of 4.2 percent, with increasing food prices affecting the poorest disproportionally.4 2. Côte d’Ivoire’s fight against poverty slowed over the last few years and hides stark inequalities. Poverty has sharply declined from 55.4 percent in 2011 to 39.5 percent in 2018, equivalent to 10 million poor people of which 4.4 million are extreme poor. However, poverty reduction was not equally distributed across the country. Abidjan experienced the sharpest reduction in poverty reaching 10.2 percent in 2018/19 - a 20 percentage point (ppt) decrease from 2011, while rural areas only experienced a 13.5 ppt decrease in that same period.5 Poverty reduction efforts have been uneven, and poverty is mainly a rural phenomenon. More than 68 percent of poor people live in rural areas in Côte d’Ivoire. Poverty reduction was also not equally shared across rural regions with living conditions significantly declining in cash crop production areas since 2015. The southern and eastern regions, which are mostly reliant on cash crops such as cocoa, rubber and palm oil have experienced an increase in the poverty incidence. This was the result of many factors among which the decrease in the farm gate price of these crops. In addition, these areas do not have significant staple or food crops to compensate income losses from cash crops. Moreover, while inequality as a whole is decreasing in the country, the gap between the poor and the rich is still very large with the 20 top percentile’s consumption being on average six times greater than that of the bottom 20 percentile. 3. Côte d’Ivoire’s economic development did not translate into progress on human capital or gender equality. Furthermore, Côte d’Ivoire has one of the highest gender inequality rates in the world: in 2019, it was ranked 162 out of 189 countries on the United Nations Gender Inequality Index. In term of human capital and access to services, women are at a significant disadvantage. Women in Côte d’Ivoire continue to face inequalities at school, in accessing health care and in the labor market. They are overrepresented in non-agricultural self-employment as well as informal employment, and underrepresented in wage jobs, leaving them without access to social protection programs and thereby making them more vulnerable. When they are employed, generally self-employed, they end up working longer hours but are less productive than men. This lower productivity stems from lower skills levels and institutional obstacles, such as barriers to access financing. While overall bank and/or saving accounts are not widespread in Côte d’Ivoire (particularly among poorer populations), women are even more disadvantaged in that respect. While 23 percent of men have savings accounts, only 14 percent of women do. Similarly, women have less access to loans, with less than 1 percent having received any loans.8 The inequality and discrimination against women has been further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic which negatively impacted women to a greater extent than men, particularly women working in the informal and agriculture sectors.9 In addition, in the context of COVID-19, an increase in levels of intimate partner violence/gender-based violence can be expected.10 4. Côte d’Ivoire is susceptible to a variety of covariate shocks with economic and climate ones being the most relevant. Côte d’Ivoire’s economy is vulnerable to commodity-price fluctuations as its trade balance depends on exports of cocoa and oil (39 percent and 17 percent, respectively).11 In addition, the country is also particularly susceptible to climate risks (ranking 131 out of 181 countries).12, 13 Between 1960 and 2010 temperatures rose by 1.6 degrees Celsius, and projections show they are likely to rise by an additional 1.8 to 2.1 degrees Celsius by 2050 and 2070, respectively. This change in temperature has impacted the timeline and the quantity of rainfall leading both to droughts and floods and affecting particularly rural households who are reliant on agriculture, but also urban ones, particularly in coastal regions. The greatest increase in temperatures is likely to happen in the northern parts, which already have some of the highest malnutrition numbers in the country. Climate change also increases the likelihood of floods.14 Without any action on climate change, it is expected that sea levels could rise 1.2 meters and there will be an increase in flooded areas. Today, floods affect an average of 45,000 people every year, in particular in urban and to a lesser extent in coastal areas.15 5. Côte d’Ivoire’s vulnerability to shocks is a challenge for its long-term poverty reduction. More than 60 percent of Ivorian households report having suffered from a shock in 2018. Idiosyncratic and covariate shocks have an important impact on households and the country’s economy with 41.2 percent of households experiencing an idiosyncratic shock and 32 percent a covariate shock. To mitigate the economic impact of COVID-19 and preserve the poverty reduction gains made in recent years, the Government of Côte d’Ivoire (GoC) rolled out a comprehensive COVID-19 crisis response package, increasing the fiscal deficit to 5.9 percent of GDP in 2020. The response package included emergency cash transfers for vulnerable households, temporary tax cancellation and deferrals to the benefit of vulnerable households and enterprises, emergency funds to provide financing to the private sector, including the informal sector, and specific support to key sectors and State-Owned Enterprises. Despite these efforts, poverty rose to an estimated 41.5 percent by October 202019 as a result of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic. Within this context, fiscal space to invest in the safety nets system is limited. However, the GoC has made it a priority to put poverty back onto a downward trajectory, starting in 2021, including through its commitment to further strengthen the safety nets system. B. The two documents build on the achievements of the National Social Protection Strategy (Stratégie Nationale de Protection Sociale, SNPS) to increase pro-poor spending and coverage of social protection programs. The SNPS action plan is currently being updated under the leadership of the Ministry of Employment and Social Protection (Ministère de l’Emploi et de la Protection Sociale, MEPS) and will be aligned with the strategic directions given in the new PND validated in October 2021 by the Council of Ministries. The PAFS was formulated in October 2021 under the leadership of the Ministry of Solidarity and Fight against Poverty (Ministère de la Solidarité et de la Lutte contre la Pauvreté, MSLP) to operationalize Pillar 4 of the PND aiming to strengthen inclusion, national solidarity and social action. While the SNPS presents the government’s broad vision to build a social protection system that strengthens the capacities of the most vulnerable households and enables populations to better manage risks, the PAFS details the programs, activities and means needed to operationalize the vision. 8. Côte d’Ivoire’s social protection system has experienced important progress over the last five years as a result of strong government commitment to build the foundations of a safety net system and particularly its commitment to developing the country’s first large scale productive cash transfer program. Under the World Bank-financed Productive Social Safety Net project (Projet des Filets Sociaux Productifs, PFSP) (P143332), the GoC progressively rolled out a national productive cash transfer project, which is transitioning into an institutionalized productive cash transfer program. The productive cash transfer program (Programme de Transferts Monétaires Productifs, PTMP), which targets households below the poverty line is currently supported by the PFSP and benefits from significant government co- financing. The GoC financed 30 percent of the total costs of the PTMP during the period 2020-2021. The PTMP expanded significantly over the years, bringing the total number of beneficiaries to a cumulative 227,000 households in 2020, covering rural and urban areas across all regions of the country. Besides quarterly cash transfers20, the program also provides economic inclusion measures to beneficiary households to support their income and build their resilience in view of their exit from the PTMP.21 In addition to financing the PTMP, the PFSP has also been supporting the development of foundational elements of an adaptive safety nets system focusing on the poorest households: a unique social registry (Registre Social Unique, RSU), the PTMP’s Management Information System (MIS), a digital payment system, and a Grievance Redress Mechanism (GRM). 9. While the social protection sector has gained prominence in Côte d’Ivoire, the lack of synergies and common delivery tools is hampering the system’s efficiency. The institutional landscape of Côte d’Ivoire’s safety nets system (Figure 2) remains highly fragmented: there is limited coordination of policies, financing strategies, and delivery services. The PFSP has played a key role in developing the foundational elements of a social protection delivery system and in promoting synergies. Potential common implementation mechanisms exist but need to be strengthened. A coordinated safety nets system could ensure better service delivery and more holistic interventions toward poor and vulnerable households. Such an approach would likely ensure greater impact on Côte d’Ivoire’s long term poverty reduction. (i) The RSU, established in September 2019 by a decree though not yet operationalized, is an important tool to support synergies in identifying and enrolling beneficiary households for different programs, and ensure resources of social programs are oriented towards poor households. 20 Beneficiary households identified through a combination of poverty targeting (Proxy means testing) and community validation are enrolled in the program for 3 years. Quarterly cash transfers of 36 000 XOF are provided through mobile money. 21 Economic inclusion measures include coaching; training on life skills, family budget, and entrepreneurship; the establishment of savings and loan associations; a small business grant; and facilitation to market access. These social centers tend to be underfunded with a limited number of social workers, which limits their capacities to serve as a delivery mechanism for social programs. The centers also currently lack the capacity to link the poor with some critical services such as the government funded health insurance scheme (Régime d’Assistance Médicale-Couverture Maladie Universelle, RAM-CMU), which offers free access to essential health services for the poor. (iii) Furthermore, while the PTMP provides cash transfer payments to beneficiaries via mobile money, the payment system is nonetheless limited: it does not allow beneficiaries to choose the payment providers, and the PTMP does not have access to instant information on the payment delivery. Lastly, the current shock response mechanisms are limited, and delivery systems could be improved and mobilized more efficiently. The adaptive social protection stress test reveals that Côte d’Ivoire has some of the necessary elements to constitute a shock responsive system. Across the four building blocks26 of an adaptive social protection system, Côte d’Ivoire is considered a nascent system, except for “Finance”, which is not developed at all and considered latent. This is in line with the findings that Côte d’Ivoire is very vulnerable to climate risks (ranking 147 out of 169 countries in terms of resilience). Though there are several agencies which are tasked with responding to shocks (through in-kind or cash support), as well as several non-government agencies that have set up ad hoc shock-responses in the past, no unified and nationally appropriate shock response plan exists. The disaster risk management aspect of shock response in Côte d’Ivoire is dissociated from the social protection one. On the latter, there is no clear policy on shock response and the role of social protection or safety net systems and programs is unclear. Several responses have been implemented by various stakeholders at various times to meet specific contextual objectives (e.g., Social Rescue, the National Solidarity Fund [Fonds National de Solidarité, FNS], the COVID-19 Special Solidarity Fund [Fonds Spécial de Solidarité COVID-19, FSS] and the Informal Sector Actor Support Fund [Fonds d’Appui aux Acteurs du Secteur Infomel, FASI]) leading to overlaps and inefficiencies. Undefined and overlapping shock response mandates have shown to delay assistance and demonstrated weaknesses in the system, further exacerbated by the lack of a delivery system conceived with shock response in mind: pre-defined operational emergency response protocols are absent, the RSU has yet to be operationalized, a unique identification number is lacking, etc. Besides the absence or actual use of existing delivery systems, shock response interventions are driven by fund availability, fall short of actual needs, and lack a strategic vision. II. PROGRAM DESCRIPTION A. Government Program 19. The objective of the PAFS is to strengthen the National Social Safety Net System to guarantee greater social inclusion of poor and vulnerable populations over the period 2022-2025. The PAFS was prepared by a multi-sectoral team32 coordinated under the leadership of the MSLP, and approved by the Council of Ministers on March 30, 2022. It is built around three pillars: (a) Harmonized tools are used by all social programs and mechanisms for targeting poor and vulnerable households are more efficient; (b) Social safety nets serve as a platform to increase the impact of social programs on the poor and vulnerable population in a sustainable way and reduce inequalities 33; and (c) Response mechanisms to covariate shocks are strengthened. The PAFS encompasses the GoC’s vision for an integrated and comprehensive safety net system which focuses on the needs of poor and vulnerable households and ensures a holistic approach in supporting them. The enrollment in the RAM-CMU, however is lower, with only about 8 percent of the target group, or 191,000 poor individuals enrolled to date.36 This low enrolment is a result of: (i) low awareness of the program among the poor; (ii) the fact that many poor persons (as much as 60 percent of the poor in rural communities) do not having administrative documents required for enrolment; and (iii) limited capacity of the existing mechanisms to enroll the poor. The GoC has been trying to address these issues by simplifying registration requirements, incentivizing enrolment by linking the CMU social security number to other government services. Integrating support for enrollment in RMA- CMU into social services provided by social centers offers a very promising opportunity to increase the number of the poor participating in the program and accessing health services free of charge. The RSU coverage will be national and thus go beyond the PTMP covered areas, in order to serve all the social and shock response programs. In fact, by covering all villages and neighborhoods, the RSU will ensure it is covering very climate vulnerable areas and will allow to target households impacted by climate change as needed. The 2019 decree creating the RSU establishes the RSU as a primary source of targeting for every social program. The Program will support: (i) the further development of the RSU-MIS, which is under development (currently supported by the ongoing PFSP project); (ii) the identification and registration of poor and vulnerable households in the RSU using a combination of proxy means testing and community validation; (iii) the continuous update of their socio- economic data; and (iv) data sharing with other social programs to increase equity, efficiency and coordination in the delivery of services to poor and vulnerable households. RSU presentation sessions will be organized with governmental and non-governmental stakeholders to promote the use of the tool. Communication campaigns will be deployed so that the populations approach this tool as a gateway to social services. The RSU will develop close partnerships with social programs to ensure a collaborative process in building this core targeting and coordination tool, and to make sure it meets their needs. 30. The GoC will undertake a pilot in six social centers to test a model with the social centers as key platform for delivery of services at local level. Social centers, which are essential access points for populations to social services at the local level, face several challenges to being effective, including location (i.e., long distance from potential beneficiaries), human resources and budget allocation constraints, equipment shortages, etc. These constraints limit their capacity to serve as the delivery mechanism at local level and reach the most vulnerable. The pilot will inform the revision of the service delivery model of social centers to strengthen their local capacities and transform them into the centerpiece of the safety nets delivery system at local level. The GoC will pilot an approach where social centers are given more autonomy in terms of human resources and financial management. To improve synergies across existing social programs, the pilot will also test the benefits of social centers entering into agreements with the RAM- CMU, the RSU and the PTMP to deliver services at the local level. Key direct results are expected to increase Page 29 of 124 The World Bank Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (P175594) efficiency of the social safety nets system, synergies amongst programs, and impact on the poorest households (e.g., number of RAM-CMU beneficiaries registered in the piloted regions, increased access to health services of RAM-CMU beneficiaries, number of RSU registered members with a CNAM unique identification number, etc.). The PforR will support the preparation of the pilot (approval of the operations manual and administrative texts to allow for the roll out of the pilot) as well as the approval of the administrative text defining the new mandate and service delivery model of the social centers, building on the learnings from the pilot. RA 3: Developing a shock response mechanism 34. RA 3 will support the GoC’s ability to respond to covariate shocks. Côte d’Ivoire is vulnerable to a variety of covariate shocks with the dominant ones being economic and climate shocks. There is also growing concern of how the insecurity in the region could spillover leading to an influx of refugees and also Internally Displaced Persons creating a shock on host communities. The GoC does not have a cohesive strategy for shock response, particularly not one which takes into account the role of social protection. As a result, it has many agencies responding in different capacities. In fact, while the government had a COVID- 19 response, its implementation was delayed and faced several challenges to identify and reach households. Shock response mechanisms are intended to mitigate the impact of such effects on the economic wellbeing of the households. The focus of this RA will be twofold: (i) support the government’s pilot shock response to climate (such as floods) and other covariate shocks; and (ii) support the GoC in developing an institutional framework for the shock response mechanism through the adoption of the proper legislative or policy framework to ensure the perennity of the mechanism. Such a mechanism can be beneficial to respond to multiple shocks as protocols and tools can be leveraged. 35. The GoC will pilot a response to shocks, with a likely focus on climate. The objective of the pilot is to test the mechanism proposed for shock response. The pilot will act as proof of concept of the developed operations manual and allow for better adaptation of the defined process. The intention is to be able to reach households just before or within days of the shock with cash transfers, while avoiding overlap with ongoing initiatives but encouraging synergies. The pilot will allow the GoC to test the trigger mechanisms, and their ability to quickly identify impacted households and provide benefits in the shortest delay possible to prevent households from resorting to negative coping strategies. The pilot will target 5,000 beneficiaries with shock responsive cash transfers. 36. The Program will support the establishment of the policy framework concerning the shock response process and ensuring its institutionalization. The GoC intends to develop a permanent shock response mechanism which can be deployed for climate but also other covariate shocks. While the adoption of texts and legislation is not a sufficient condition to ensure sustainability or ownership it is nonetheless a necessary one. Defining clear institutional arrangements and coordination mechanisms, particularly with other relevant entities in this space will also enable greater autonomy and ability to react by the specified agency/ies. Coupled with the risk financing strategy which would define a set of recommendations and actions for the government, these policies will contribute to the establishment of an institutional framework for shock response which can be built on. The PforR will support the GoC in the development of the appropriate decrees and encourage their adoption by the appropriate body. 37. Finally, the PforR will support the government in the intersectoral coordination. The Program will support coordination between sectors and ministries which have not historically coordinated, such as the MSLP and the Ministry of Interior and Environment. Several ministries and government entities play a role in shock response today. However, their communication and coordination on assessments and response is very limited. There is significant evidence showing the strong positive impacts of cash transfers, economic inclusion programs and shock response assistance on poverty reduction and resilience strengthening. Cash transfers smooth the consumption of poor households while economic inclusion programs build their resilience and ability to become self-sufficient. The shock response program provides an additional layer of protection to allow households to mitigate and cope with the impacts of shocks, thus diminishing the impact and likelihood of falling into and further into poverty. 43. There is longstanding evidence for the positive economic returns of unconditional cash transfer programs. A review of 165 studies on cash transfers across middle and low incomes countries shows that cash transfers work. Cash transfers have an impact on household food expenditure, and on poverty. Even more telling is the evidence on human capital outcomes such as health and education. Studies reviewed show clear links between increased school attendance among cash transfer beneficiaries as well as with the uptake of health services.44 Timely cash transfer in response to shocks also have proven to be impactful on maintaining the welfare of households or decreasing negative impacts. A recent large-scale evaluation of the impact of providing temporary cash transfers ahead of floods in Bangladesh revealed that anticipatory support protects adult and child consumption even three months after the shock, as well as decreased negative coping strategies. 44. In recent years, there has been growing evidence on the impact of combining economic inclusion measures with cash transfers, including to simultaneously tackle multiple constraints to productivity. A recent impact evaluation in Niger on a similar economic inclusion program reveals that not only do these measures increase consumption and income but they also build resilience of households as they help them diversify their income sources, particularly away from agriculture (thus reducing their vulnerability to drought). Effects are sustained even 18 months after the program. Across different treatment arms, the Niger study finds an improvement of 5 to 13 percent on per capita consumption relative to the control group.45 Moreover, the evaluation has demonstrated that women retain control over their earnings (when they are the primary target of the economic inclusion measures), have greater productive agency and have greater control over their economic activities. 45. In terms of the Program’s impact on Côte d’Ivoire’s poverty indicators, the effects vary depending on whether all poor or extreme poor households are targeted. Overall, impacts on extreme poverty and the severity of poverty are maximized when targeting transfers to the extreme poor rather than the poor in general. PROGRAM IMPLEMENTATION A. Institutional and Implementation Arrangements 46. The PforR Program benefits from strong commitment from the GoC as it supports the PAFS which is fully aligned with the country’s priorities as set out in the PND 2021-2025. The PforR’s institutional architecture relies on clear roles, accountability lines, and government ownership. It follows the same institutional arrangements as the PAFS, which leverages and complements existing sectoral resources, catalyzing efforts to bring about more coherence and integration across ministerial departments involved in safety nets programs. The governance arrangements rely on mechanisms already established and functioning. 47. The strategic oversight of the PAFS and of the Program is carried out by the National Social Protection steering committee. The steering committee was established to monitor and provide strategic guidance for the implementation of the SNPS in 2014 and has met regularly since. It is chaired by the Prime minister or its representative, and includes all sectoral ministries involved in the program, as well as the MEF, the Ministry of Budget and the Ministry of Plan and Development. In addition to overseeing the implementation of the PAFS and of the PforR, the steering committee oversees resource mobilization for the successful implementation of the program. 48. The BCPFS is responsible for the coordination of the government program and the PforR. The BCPFS is the new safety net coordination operational unit that will report to the MSLP. It will be created by an inter-ministerial decree between the MSLP, the Ministry of Budget and the MEF to coordinate the PAFS, and as a result, the Program. More specifically, the BCPFS will implement the PTMP, and any initiatives linked to social safety nets. The BCPFS will be a continuation of the Project Implementation Unit (PIU) of the PFSP. The PIU was created in 2015 by an inter-ministerial decree for the management of the PFSP (P143332). As the PTMP moved from a donor financed intervention into a government program, the transition of the PIU into a more sustainable management unit was necessary. The creation of the BCPFS is an effectiveness condition of the PforR. 49. The BCPFS is expected to be quickly operational, and its team to have high capacity, acquired through years of overseeing the implementation of the PFSP. The PSFP project management has been Page 37 of 124 The World Bank Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (P175594) rated Satisfactory since the beginning. The PIU team is composed of civil servants and a few consultants, mostly for fiduciary functions. They have accumulated substantial experience in managing a countrywide cash transfer program. The team composed of a coordinator, an M&E specialist, an MIS specialist, a productive inclusion specialist, a financement and administrative manager, and a procurement specialist will be transferred to the BCPFS to ensure a smooth transition and full continuity in the implementation of the PTMP, as well as to ensure high quality implementation of the IPF component of this operation. Consultants, with a profile responding to the World Bank requirements will continue to be in charge of fiduciary and safeguards functions, especially for the IPF component. 50. A technical committee, composed of at least six focal points from each implementing agency or directorate will ensure the coordination with various actors in the implementation of the Program. TECHNICAL ASSESSMENT SUMMARY A. Program Description 1. The PforR will be anchored under the PAFS, supporting the strategic vision of the PND 2021-2025. The PND was adopted by the Council of Ministers in October 2021 and provides a clear vision of the government’s objectives and activities to fight poverty and reduce vulnerabilities. Pillar 4 of the PND focuses on social inclusion and national solidarity and each Ministry contributing to this pillar defines its objectives and actions. To operationalize the PND and increase synergies between the various sectoral actors under this pillar, the MSLP led the multi-sectoral dialogue to develop the PAFS. 2. The objective of the PAFS is to strengthen the National Social Safety Net System to guarantee greater social inclusion of poor and vulnerable populations over the period 2022- 2025. The PAFS was prepared by a multi-sectoral team, under the leadership of the MSLP, and approved by the Council of Ministers on March 30, 2022. It is built around three pillars: (i) strengthening the targeting mechanisms for social inclusion interventions and mutualization of tools; (ii) promoting synergies between and sustainability of safety nets programs, and the extension of their coverage; and (iii) strengthening the response mechanisms to covariate shocks. 3. The Program will support the government’s efforts to increase access of the poorest households to the safety nets system and strengthen its operational capabilities by supporting the three pillars of the PAFS. The proposed PforR builds on the results of the World Bank-financed PFSP (P143332). 4. The PDO is to increase access of poor and vulnerable households to safety nets programs and improve the performance of delivery systems. 5. To achieve the PDO, the PforR focuses on three Result Areas (RA) and foresees an IPF component to provide technical assistance and support operational costs. The PforR’s three RAs are: (1) Strengthening targeting mechanism and delivery systems for social programs; (2) Expansion and sustainability of the productive cash transfer program (PTMP); and (3) Developing a shock response mechanism. B. Strategic Relevance 6. The GoC’s strategic vision to fight poverty and reduce vulnerabilities is clearly enshrined in the PND 2021-2025 and operationalized by the newly adopted PAFS. The two documents build on the achievements of the SNPS 2016–2020 to increase pro-poor spending and coverage of social protection programs. 7. The Program is strategically relevant as it addresses key constraints to poverty reduction in Côte d’Ivoire. By increasing access of the poorest households to safety nets programs and improving the performance of the delivery system, households will benefit from synergies of existing programs. For Côte d’Ivoire to improve the living conditions and resilience of poor and vulnerable households and support their transition out of poverty, improving the performance of its safety nets delivery system to become more adaptive and effective is key. The COVID-19 pandemic has highlighted the importance of strong delivery systems (including social registries), as well as of shock responsive mechanisms. Three elements are needed for the country to provide poor and vulnerable households a pathway to better livelihoods and improved resilience: (i) delivery mechanisms and tools which allow social programs, including the cash transfer program, to effectively and efficiently reach their beneficiaries and deliver Page 80 of 124 The World Bank Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (P175594) their program; (ii) a productive cash transfer program that operates at scale and contributes to women’s social and economic empowerment; and (iii) a strong shock response mechanism leveraging existing safety nets system delivery tools for Côte d’Ivoire to support vulnerable households cope with covariate shocks. 8. The Program builds on the important progress that has been made in Côte d’Ivoire’s social protection sector over the last five years. With the support of the PFSP, the government put in place its first cash transfer project in 2016. The cash transfer program has expanded significantly over the years, bringing the total number of beneficiaries to a cumulative 227,000 households in 2020, covering rural and urban areas across all regions of the country and providing economic inclusion measures to all beneficiaries in addition to the quarterly cash transfers. The PFSP, as well as the setup of the PTMP, supported the development of foundational elements of a social protection delivery system focusing on the poorest households: an RSU, the program’s MIS, a digital payment system, and a GRM. The Program will strengthen these building blocks to increase access of the poorest households to safety nets programs. C. Technical Soundness 9. The proposed Program is technically sound and builds on international best practices. The Program aims to consolidate the achievements of the PFSP and move toward a more sustainable and inclusive safety net system, by maintaining an updated socio-economic registry, scaling up the productive cash transfer program, investing in greater linkages and harmonization of tools between social programs, and developing a shock response system to support beneficiaries transition out of poverty and vulnerability. 10. A technically sound Program is one that is designed and implemented to efficiently yield the required results and achieve its objectives. This can be assessed by the country’s experience to date and in relation to international experience and best practices. Based on literature and international experience, three standards are to be examined to assess a Program that aims to strengthen the national safety nets system: (i) the existence of tools and delivery systems to be used and mutualized by social programs; (ii) the expansion and sustainability of a large safety nets program; and (iii) the capacities of the system to respond to shocks. The key parameters for assessing whether the Program strengthening the national safety nets system is technically sound are as follows and organized as per the above-mentioned standards. Standard 1: Existence of tools and delivery systems to be used and mutualized by social programs - Targeting procedures ensure that that those in need and who meet the eligibility criteria receive support. - The social registry is up to date and comprehensive. Socio-economic data on poor households in the country is available, making it relevant for the identification of eligible households and individuals under other social programs. - A strong delivery mechanism of social programs at local level enables the monitoring and provision of services of beneficiaries. This delivery mechanism is mutualized amongst social programs to increase synergies and reduce costs. - A shared payment platform can be used by several safety nets programs, which enables costs saving, and stronger coordination. - Beneficiaries and citizens are able to raise their concerns about program delivery and can influence the service they receive and future improvements in social program. Page 81 of 124 The World Bank Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (P175594) Standard 2: Expansion and sustainability of a large safety nets program - There exists a national safety nets program targeting poor households with a good coverage that can be leveraged to build the wider safety nets system. - Beneficiaries are receiving adequate, reliable, and accessible payments and services. - Adequate information systems allow to keep track of program beneficiaries and to support program activities. - Institutional arrangements are sustainable and staff capacities are adequate to manage the safety nets program efficiently. Standard 3: Capacities of the system to respond to shocks - A shock response program is in place and triggered in case of covariate shocks, capable of reaching those who would otherwise restore to negative coping mechanisms. - The shock response program leverages and has access to existing systems and tools and does not create parallel systems outside the social protection one. - The system is institutionalized to ensure its perennity and long-term sustainability. Social registry and targeting mechanism 12. Social registries play a key role in inclusive systems, as they can serve as a gateway to one or many programs. When multiple programs use a joint social registry (or “Integrated Social Registry”), they can play an important social policy role in helping coordinate efforts to reach intended populations. They facilitate synergies across programs aiming to deliver complementary benefits and services to common population groups. In fact, integrated social registries serve as a powerful platform that can extend well beyond social assistance programs. Using Integrated Social Registries for multiple programs can reduce transactions costs and increase/facilitate access for citizens, save cost and increase efficiency for user programs, and strengthen social policy coordination. Key ingredients for Integrated Social Registries include inter-institutional coordination, common eligibility concepts and a shared intake questionnaire, and capabilities for information sharing and data exchange.54 Strong MIS and defined legislative environments for data protection are critical for an integrated social registry to successfully serve programs. 13. The Program will support the social center reform through a pilot. In most African countries, social safety nets are the responsibility of national governments, with varying implementation arrangements, including outsourcing to external service providers. However, long-term solutions are required to embed social safety nets at the heart of government systems and ensure sustainability. The issue of incentives for civil servants is generally critical for effective implementation, particularly as cash transfer programs are taken to scale and shift into national systems. In Côte d’Ivoire, social centers can constitute a great opportunity to deliver a comprehensive service package to the most vulnerable at local level. They are essential entry points for populations to access social services, and several programs (cash transfer program, orphans and vulnerable children program, nutrition program, etc.) rely on them to deliver said services. However, social centers face a number of shortages, which limit their ability to operate at full potential and reach the most vulnerable. The pilot will test a new approach to help address the social centers’ main challenges: (i) limited human resources to meet needs (17 social workers per social center are needed to implement the activities with the level of quality required, but social centers only have seven social workers on average due to limited budgets for recruitment); (ii) social workers’ low remuneration that provide no incentives (salaries are low and financial bonuses inexistent); and (iii) insufficient operational budget to enable field assistance and ensure household reception in the social centers under good conditions (the current yearly budget is set at 4,000,000 FCFA per center while operating needs are estimated at 9,000,000 FCFA for a center covering an average population of 10,000 habitants). The pilot will be designed in coordination with the different social programs relying on the delivery of services by the social centers. 16. The PTMP’s extensive use of frontline workers will be addressed in the social center reform to maintain an adequate ratio of frontline workers to beneficiaries. There are 135 social centers in the country with around 800 social workers. Due to limited availability of resources at social centers, the PTMP, under the PFSP, recruited 500 community agents hired on a consultancy basis to deliver key services to the beneficiaries. These community agents worked under the direct supervision of the social center directors. The PforR will embed the PTMP at the heart of the social center delivery mechanisms to ensure sustainability. Some community agents will be integrated as social workers in the social centers, but some will remain involved as consultants. Hiring community agents on a consultancy basis will ensure an adequate level of service, including to allow to quickly increase human resources when new Page 83 of 124 The World Bank Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (P175594) needs arise, in the case of shock responses for example. The objective is to maintain a constant ratio of one frontline worker (either community agent or social worker) to 250 PTMP beneficiaries throughout the Program. With the social center reform, the community agents’ and social workers’ role will evolve slightly to enable a mutualization of delivery mechanisms of social programs and increase synergies. Payment systems 17. Strengthening digital payments and unifying payments systems ensure greater ability to reach beneficiaries in an efficient way and increases their access to multiple services. Payment digitization for social protection programs has the potential to substantially increase financial inclusion, particularly among the poor and vulnerable. By adopting rapidly evolving financial and technological services, countries using modern Government-to-People (G2P) payment systems have improved recipient experiences by allowing them to choose payment providers and set-up their own accounts to receive funds, lowered payment delivery costs for governments, reduced leakage through integrated payment systems, and introduced incentives for payment service providers to compete for their market share.55 A recent World Bank study56 on the G2P payment systems in Côte d’Ivoire has highlighted several recommendations to improve the transfers’ digitizing process and the integration of populations into the digital ecosystem, which would guarantee their financial inclusion. Recommendations include: (i) to set up an interoperable platform between the MIS of social programs and the MIS of selected payment service providers; (ii) to integrate PTMP beneficiaries into a digital financial services ecosystem; (iii) to diversify payment channels and financial service providers to simplify access for beneficiaries; (iv) to improve digital financial education; and (v) to provide each individual with a unique identifier. 18. The Program will support the government’s effort to develop a unique payment platform to be leveraged by multiple programs (including the PTMP and shock responses). Current social programs’ payment systems work in silos. This means that households or individuals receiving different benefits from the government may have to use several payment channels, different mobile accounts, one for each program. Moving toward a multisectoral system whereby several programs leverage similar tools can lead to greater efficiency. Unifying payment platforms can decrease costs and fees through economies of scale and also increase bargaining power. Such platforms, directly linked to payment providers, monitor payments and cash outs in real time and can be leveraged in times of shocks. A review of the global response to the COVID-19 pandemic revealed that even with digital payments enabled, leveraging existing programs and systems proved faster than having to develop new programs and new digital payment systems.57 The Program will support the use of a unique payment platform by safety nets programs under the PAFS. A number of lessons emerge from international experience in bringing cash transfer programs to scale with regard to the program design. The report ‘Realizing the full potential of Safety Nets in Africa’ highlights the following, among others63: First, the value of a cash transfer matters. Ensuring impact requires sufficiently large transfers to help households smooth consumption. Second, the impact of programs relies on predictability. If benefits are not delivered with regularity, households cannot use them as effectively. As programs go to scale, fiscal sustainability (i.e., regular funding) is needed to ensure that programs reach maximum impact. Third, coordination with complementary programs, such as skills training or other employment schemes, is critical for maximizing resilience and promoting economic inclusion. As social safety nets grow, there will be a greater need for a sound institutional framework to tie programs together. RA 2 of the PforR is well aligned with these key lessons. 29. Shock responsive cash transfers which are delivered in a timely manner provide more than just temporary relief but help households mitigate and diminish the impact of the shock. A recent large-scale evaluation on the impact of providing temporary cash transfers ahead of floods in Bangladesh revealed that anticipatory support protects adult and child consumption even three months after the shock, as well as decreased the share of households that restored to negative coping strategies.68 In fact, households who benefitted from this pre- empted support (before peak flood) suffered fewer asset losses and damage as they were more likely to evacuate ahead of time with significant assets. This meant they were less likely to borrow money from friends and predatory lenders. In addition, the anticipatory support also serves as a mechanism to communicate to households about upcoming shocks such as floods when households may not know otherwise. 35. In its quest to develop an adaptive social protection system, which can protect poor and vulnerable households from falling into or further into poverty, the government will leverage the safety nets systems (its programs and/or tools) to respond to shocks. Relying on existing tools rather than creating parallel ones is key to an efficient and effective shock response system. In Côte d’Ivoire, rather than leverage existing systems for its COVID-19 response, the government created the FSS and the FASI under the MEF to support households most affected by the pandemic. On the other hand, countries in the Sahel relied on existing system, thus benefitting from capacities and experiences already acquired in the past. Countries either undertook a vertical or horizontal expansion of their existing safety net programs (Burkina Faso) or piggybacked on existing systems (Mauritania). Mauritania and Senegal relied on the social registry which covers nearly 100 percent of poor households in the country. Mauritania also relied on an existing payment system to reach 200,000 beneficiaries with COVID-19 emergency cash transfers.69 In Mauritania, the COVID-19 response beneficiaries were also able to use existing and highly functioning GRM systems. Piggybacking on these systems allowed Mauritania to be one of the first countries in the Sahel to deploy a response—as early as June 2020. Creating a parallel system delays the ability to respond in a timely manner, but also creates additional structures and new path dependencies. Under the Program, the GoC will pilot a shock response mechanism that leverages the delivery systems and tools of the safety nets systems that will be strengthened under RA 1, i.e., the RSU, the frontline delivery mechanism, the payment system and the GRM. 36. In addition, through the Program, the GoC intends to institutionalize a shock response framework. The efficiency of a shock responsive safety net program lies in its ability to prepare a response ahead of a shock hitting, to correctly identify those who need to be reached and provide the appropriate support in a timely manner. To achieve this, the Program will develop clear protocols with appropriate institutional arrangements, leveraging already existing tools such as the registry and digital payment systems (supported under RA 1). Program’s Economic Impact: There is significant evidence showing the strong positive impacts of cash transfers, economic inclusion programs and shock response assistance. Cash transfers smooth the consumption of poor households while the economic inclusion programs build their resilience and ability to become self-sufficient. The shock response program provides an additional layer of protection to allow households to mitigate and cope with the impacts of shock, thus diminishing the impact and likelihood of falling into poverty. 60. Longstanding evidence on the positive economic returns of unconditional cash transfer programs exists. A review of 165 studies on cash transfers across middle and low incomes countries shows that cash transfers work. Cash transfers have an impact on household food expenditure, and on poverty. Even more telling is the evidence on human capital outcomes such as health and education. Studies reviewed show clear links between increased school attendance among cash transfer beneficiaries as well as with the uptake of health services.74 61. In recent years, there has been growing evidence of the impact of combining economic inclusion measures with cash transfers. A recent impact evaluation in Niger on a similar economic inclusion program reveals that not only do these measures increase consumption and income but they also build household resilience as they help them diversify their income sources, particularly away from agriculture (thus reducing their vulnerability to drought). Effects are sustained even 18 months after the program. Across different treatment arms, the Niger study finds an improvement of 5 to 13 percent on per capita consumption relative to the control group (of only cash transfer beneficiaries) and household food security improves by 6 to 20 percent relative to the control group.75 More than this however, the evaluation found strong evidence on increased income and economic diversification, particularly away from agriculture with an increase in income between 7 and 10 percent relative to the control group. Similar strong evidence is found by other studies, also highlighting the impact on savings and assets. Evidence from Tanzania shows that savings behavior amongst beneficiaries of the program increased by 29 percent relative to the control group, with evidence from Kenya showing this impact lasts beyond the duration of the program. TA will include support in defining the institutional arrangements and strengthening linkages to the national Disaster Risk Management (DRM) response and Early Warning System (EWS) if needed. To ensure a strong and efficient response, it is essential to clearly delegate the mandate and responsibility to respond to covariate shocks. Identifying the institution(s) from the social protection side that is/are responsible for ensuring that households do not have to rely on negative coping strategies to survive is necessary.
