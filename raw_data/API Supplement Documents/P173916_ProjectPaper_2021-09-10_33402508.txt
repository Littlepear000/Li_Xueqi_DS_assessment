    I. PROJECT STATUS AND RATIONALE FOR RESTRUCTURING

    A. Introduction

1.      This Restructuring Paper seeks the approval of the Regional Vice President to restructure the North Macedonia
Emergency COVID-19 Project (P173916). The restructuring will support the revised activities of the project under the
COVID-19 Strategic Preparedness and Response Program (SPRP) using the Multiphase Programmatic Approach (MPA),
approved by the Board on April 2, 2020, and the vaccines additional financing (AF) to the SPRP approved on October
13, 2020. This primary objective of the restructuring is to reallocate funds to support vaccine acquisition and system
strengthening for vaccine deployment, financial bonuses to eligible health workers, and acquisition of medical equipment
for diagnosis, treatment, and follow up of COVID-19 patients. These activities are aligned with the SPRP, using the MPA.

2.       The need for restructuring to support COVID-19 vaccine procurement and deployment was formally conveyed
to the World Bank (WB) by the Ministry of Finance (MoF) on March 23, 2021. The Government of North Macedonia
(GoNM) aims to vaccinate approximately 70 percent of its population. World Bank financing will support the procurement
and deployment of eligible COVID-19 vaccines to cover 17 percent of the total population, or approximately 20 percent of
the population aged 16 and above (the eligible age for vaccination in the third stage of the vaccination program), assuming
a two-dose regimen. Vaccines procured and deployed under the Project will meet the World Bank’s Vaccine Approval
Criteria (VAC),1 and the investments under the restructuring will strengthen immunization systems by prioritizing energy-
efficient equipment and climate-sensitive activities, especially regarding improvements in cold chain, logistics, and waste
management.
3.       As of April 16, 2021, the World Bank will accept as threshold for eligibility of International Bank for
Reconstruction and Development/International Development Association (IBRD/IDA) resources in COVID-19 vaccine
acquisition and/or deployment under all Bank–financed projects: (i) the vaccine has received regular or emergency
licensure or authorization from at least one of the Stringent Regulatory Authorities (SRAs) identified by the World
Health Organization (WHO) for vaccines procured and/or supplied under the COVID-19 Vaccines Global Access Facility
(COVAX Facility), as may be amended from time to time by WHO; or (ii) the vaccine has received WHO Prequalification
(PQ) or WHO Emergency Use Listing (EUL)—see Annex 1 for details. The GoNM has confirmed that vaccination against
COVID-19 will be provided at no cost to the entire population. The financing of this immunization effort will require
internal and external sources of financing. The staged rollout will be guided by the national COVID-19 vaccine deployment
plan, which identifies priority groups in line with the WHO SAGE values framework, as well as the need to vaccinate as
much of the population as possible in light of the wider availability of vaccines and vaccine hesitancy. The proposed
restructuring contributes to the expanded pandemic response in North Macedonia that is supported by partners, including
Gavi, the Global Fund the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), and the WHO, and coordinated by the Ministry of
Health (MoH).
4.       The Project is being restructured for the following changes: (i) revision of Project components and Component
costs, (ii) revision of the results framework and overall risk rating, (iii) reallocation between disbursement categories,
(iv) revision to the financial and economic analysis (within the Appraisal Summary),2 and (v) revision to the social and
environmental analyses.3

5.       The restructuring will support the acquisition of vaccines from a range of sources to support North Macedonia’s
objective of drawing upon a portfolio of options to access vaccines under the right conditions – for example, value-for-
money, regulatory standards, and delivery time. This will allow additional flexibility in the use of the Project funds, given
the current uncertainties in the vaccine market, while complying with the World Bank requirements for COVID-19
operations. North Macedonia expects to receive 833,000 doses under the COVAX Facility in 2021. The GoNM has directly
procured approximately 800,000 doses from Pfizer, which has been identified by the GoNM as one of the contracts to be
potentially reimbursed through this restructuring. The restructuring will support acquisition and deployment of vaccines
that meet the VAC, procured from various sources, including direct acquisition from manufacturers, COVAX, and excess
stocks from other countries, considering value-for-money, regulatory standards, national immunization system readiness,
and delivery time. The GoNM is seeking to secure supplies from other sources, as detailed in Table 2.4 The rollout of
vaccines to nearly 70 percent of the population will occur in three stages, with priority groups for each stage specified as
summarized in Table 3.5 Not all of the vaccines planned for use by the GoNM meet the World Bank’s VAC, as such, those
that do not will not be eligible to be procured and/or deployed with World Bank financing.

6.     This restructuring will also provide funding for broader health system strengthening activities that will support
the immunization program and help to enhance the resilience of the system for the future. Given the evolution of the
pandemic, resources that were originally planned to support unemployment benefits and the Guaranteed Minimum
Income (GMI) activities will be reallocated to support the additional activities. This is due to the fact that the GoNM’s quick
response to support the retainment of workers led to lower than anticipated unemployment rates, thereby reducing the
need for families to apply for these benefits.

Current COVID-19 epidemiological profile and interacting factors

7.      The current epidemiological situation indicates the importance of prompt action on the deployment of COVID-
19 vaccines in North Macedonia. North Macedonia has experienced two significant surges: in November 2020 (peak of
1,402 confirmed cases on November 18, 2020), and April 2021 (peak of 1,132 confirmed cases on April 5, 2021)— see
Annex 2. The number of new daily confirmed cases declined after the GoNM ordered stricter measures beginning on
March 10, 2021. As of August 4, 2021, North Macedonia had one of the highest numbers in the world in terms of deaths
per population, totaling nearly 2,637 cumulative confirmed COVID-19 deaths per million people. In terms of the
cumulative number of cases in the region, North Macedonia is ahead of Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, and Kosovo, but
behind Croatia, Montenegro, Serbia, and Slovenia (Annex 2). In addition to the impact on the health sector, the pandemic
has also had negative effects on the economy, due to the needed mitigation measures and enhanced safety protocols that
the GoNM had to put in place to contain the spread of the virus while maintaining its health systems’ capacity. Vaccines
present an important step in ensuring that resumption of economic activity can continue safely, with due adjustments.

C. Project Performance

10.     The progress towards achievement of the PDO and the overall implementation Progress has been consistently
rated Satisfactory and remains so per the latest Implementation Status and Results (ISR) Report of June 2021. As of
August 4, 2021, 53 percent or $59.62 million has been disbursed. Since Parliament ratified the loan on October 13, 2020,
the Project Operational Manual (POM) and all related Environmental and Social (E&S) documents have been developed
and approved by the World Bank, and the PMU is operational. After the declaration of project effectiveness on October
29, 2020, the Project started disbursing quickly, with a first disbursement of EUR 10.0 million (US$12.0 million) on
November 5, 2020, which included retroactive financing.

11.     All three components are progressing well. Under Component 1, progress has been made in the procurement of
17 prefabricated modular facilities for COVID-19 treatment and their phased installation. All 17 modular facilities are
operational. Progress has also been made in the procurement of essential medicines, rapid antigen tests and medical
equipment. Under Component 2, the GoNM requested retroactive financing for payments made for GMI and
unemployment benefits. Progress is being made in assessing the eligibility of the 24,924 people who applied for temporary
assistance from the GMI based on the revised targeting criteria. Out of these, based on the new eligibility criteria, 9,659
have been approved, 95 rejected, and 3,408 refused, while 11,762 applications are being processed. The distribution of
hygiene packages is progressing, with the contracting of the Red Cross completed. The number of people applying for
unemployment benefits is lower than expected and the GoNM , as a result, has not drawn upon these project funds to
support the expansion of unemployment benefits. The GoNM’s wage subsidy program was credited with preventing

    D. Rationale for restructuring

12.     This restructuring is being proposed at a crucial juncture in North Macedonia’s response to COVID-19 and will
support the GoNM’s efforts to fulfil its vaccine deployment plan. COVID-19 vaccination commenced in high-income
countries in December 2020 and in the Western Balkans in January 2021. For North Macedonia, vaccination started in
February 17, 2021. As of August 4, 2021, 18.8 percent of people in North Macedonia have been fully vaccinated. This
restructuring draws on key lessons learned from the ongoing vaccination campaign globally and in North Macedonia, as
presented in Box 1. A key rationale for the restructuring is to provide financing for safe and effective vaccine acquisition
and deployment in North Macedonia, thus enabling the country to acquire and administer vaccines rapidly, recognizing
that there is still a high demand for vaccines from both high-income and lower-income countries.

                           Box 1: Drawing on lessons learned from the ongoing vaccination campaign

    The COVID-19 pandemic has placed significant pressure on health and social protection systems worldwide. After more than a year
    since the WHO declared the pandemic, much has been learned about how governments can mobilize effective responses. This
    restructuring draws on this global experience and North Macedonia’s own experience in its vaccination campaign that started in
    February 2021. Two examples highlight how the Project draws on emerging good practices:

        1.   Demand promotion and robust supply of vaccines

    Globally, governments are increasingly aware of the synergy and interdependency that exists in having a comprehensive supply of
    vaccines and promoting a resilient demand of vaccines to achieve high and equitable coverage. North Macedonia has commendably
    used a blend of vaccine sourcing options to ensure a robust supply amid global supply constraints. The GoNM has also conducted
    two vaccination surveys to better understand the population’s concerns and mitigate vaccine hesitancy. Additionally, the GoNM,
    in partnership with WHO and UNICEF, is currently preparing a comprehensive risk communication strategy based on the needs
    identified from surveys as part of a broader social engagement and mobilization strategy.

        2.   Leveraging existing health systems for COVID-19 vaccination efforts

    Primary Health Care (PHC) systems are people’s first and most trusted contact points with the health care system. Over the decades
    and even now, these systems have successfully played a vital role in vaccination efforts. As such, it is recommended that PHC be
    leveraged to support the rapid and equitable rollout of COVID-19 vaccination. In North Macedonia, the delivery of COVID-19
    vaccines has so far relied on the existing network of primary health care centers. Vaccination teams comprising nurses and doctors
    have already been set up across primary health centers and sport halls in the country. Vaccine registration and monitoring are being
    conducted through the eHealth system (Moj Termin), which has also been adapted to enhance safety and surveillance. Vaccine
    registration is also done by the general (medical) practitioners (GPs) who were proactively inviting and registering elderly citizens
    for vaccination.

13.     Vaccine access and rollout is critical to the country’s economic recovery, given that the economy contracted by
4.5 percent in 2020.7 The pandemic will cost IBRD/ IDA countries a combined US$43 billion per month in economic activity
throughout 2021 in a downside scenario.8 Global economic recovery will not be possible if any country linked to global
supply chains is left behind. Yet high-income countries representing 13 percent of global population have reserved 51
percent of the initial supply of COVID-19 vaccines and are already making significant progress in mass vaccination (Annex
2). Competition for vaccine purchasing is ongoing as production is also affected by the pandemic in key countries like India
that host large manufacturers of pharmaceuticals. These trends pose the risk that low- and middle-income countries might
not be able to source needed quantities vaccines for their populations to help halt the transmission of the virus. In North
Macedonia, COVID-19 has had a substantial economic impact. Lockdowns, disrupted supply chains, and a prolonged,
adverse epidemiological situation have caused the deepest recession in two decades affecting all sectors of the economy
and economic activity. The World Bank has estimated that in 2020, economic activity in North Macedonia declined by 4.5
percent.

and WHO. UNICEF has taken the lead on risk communication and community engagement, while WHO is the lead agency
on technical issues related to the health and vaccine components of emergency response. The United Nations Population
Fund (UNFPA) and UN Women are focusing on women and gender issues related to the COVID-19 response. The European
Union (EU) is supporting procurement of vaccines through agreements from member countries. On January 14, 2021,
WHO donated three biosafety cabinets – two for the Institute for Microbiology and one for the Institute of Forensic
Medicine. This is in addition to previous donations of personal protective equipment (PPE) and biosafety cabinets for the

gaps and options to address key challenges in equipment availability and vaccine hesitancy among the population. This
assessment has informed the development of the National Vaccine Deployment Plan (NVDP). It has also supported the
estimation of the cost of vaccine deployment, financing, technical needs, and options for addressing the identified gaps.

The National Vaccine Deployment Plan (NVDP)

16.     North Macedonia is one of an estimated 80 self-financing countries under the COVAX Facility, and the GoNM’s
vaccine deployment plan outlines a strategy that begins with deploying vaccines to those most at risk. As supply
constraints are expected in the short term, North Macedonia has identified priority groups for COVID-19 vaccines. Overall,
the approach has been informed by the WHO SAGE values framework for the allocation and prioritization of COVID-19
vaccination, as well as a core focus on vaccinating the most people possible, particularly in the context of high levels of hesitancy
The framework advocates for the prioritization of groups for vaccination, based on the principles of human well-being,
equal respect, equity, reciprocity, and legitimacy. These principles imply that preferential access to COVID-19 vaccines
should be extended to groups that are at significant risk of infection, such as the aged and individuals living with
comorbidities, and to groups that bear a significant burden of the COVID-19 response, such as health and social workers.
In line with these principles, the GoNM has prioritized health and social workers, the elderly, people living with
comorbidities, individuals living in long-term placement centers, and teachers for preferential access to COVID-19
vaccines. These groups are identified in the North Macedonia NVDP for COVID-19 vaccines. Notably, as supply constraints
for COVID-19 vaccines eases partly due to the government’s approach of blending vaccine acquisition plans and sources,
the government is putting more focus on demand generation by adjusting its prioritization framework to enhance
increased uptake of vaccines towards the 70 percent target. This adjustment has been informed mainly by ongoing
behavioral insights surveys that continue to monitor knowledge, risk perceptions, preventive behaviors, and trust to
inform an effective vaccination response.12

reserve by purchasing outside of COVAX, it has made direct indemnification arrangements with manufacturers. As such,
North Macedonia currently has legislation in place to provide statutory immunity for manufacturers and a national no-

20.    The National Immunization Committee is the governing body for national vaccination programs, including the
COVID-19 vaccination campaign. The committee is advised by the NTWG, which is supporting program implementation
through, inter alia, advising on priority groups for COVID-19 vaccination process; updating logistic, technical and scientific
recommendations, standards operating procedures (SOPs), education and training materials; and evaluating the process
of COVID-19 vaccination. Expedited regulatory pathways are in place to ensure emergency approval of COVID-19 vaccines
without compromising the safety and efficacy of the vaccine. These approvals are undertaken by MALMED and MoH,
based on prior authorization by the WHO and SRAs. The procurement of vaccines is coordinated by MoH.

21.     The Customs Administration is responsible for ensuring an accelerated customs clearance procedure when
vaccines enter North Macedonia. Similarly, the GoNM is leveraging existing operators with marketing authorization for
drugs under special temperature conditions – that is, less than 70oC – to transport vaccines from central storage to the
vaccination points. The MoH will provide suitable refrigerators (-70oC or -20oC) at central storage facilities depending on
the vaccine available.

22.      Healthcare workers have been vaccinated centrally within the University Clinics, while vaccination of other
priority groups is taking place at the existing vaccination points, such as Health Homes and Public Health Centers. This
first phase of vaccination is crucial for adaptive learning and, as such, it is being monitored through rigorous data entry
and evaluation of vaccine safety.

23.     While vaccination is not mandatory, it is expected to be slowed down by vaccine hesitancy. Initial reports
indicate that vaccine hesitancy is a challenge. According to a recent vaccination survey conducted in May 2021, vaccine
hesitancy is estimated to be between 30 to 42 percent in North Macedonia.14 This is an improvement from previous levels
of 59 percent reported in December 2020.15 Hence a first phase of a communications campaign is underway in concert
with WHO and UNICEF as part of a broader social engagement and mobilization strategy. Additionally, a more
comprehensive risk communication strategy is being prepared by the MoH in collaboration with UNICEF and WHO.
Communication is highlighting vaccination stages, priority groups, selection criteria, AEFI, grievance redress mechanisms
(GRMs), how to access providers, and other aspects, depending on the information needs identified in the ongoing
behavioral insights surveys and in accordance with the risk communication strategy.

24.     COVID-19 vaccine deployment will require unprecedented efforts to strengthen supply chains and ancillary
systems, including legal, posing enormous implementation challenges to deploy vaccines at scale. North Macedonia has
achieved high immunization coverage rates among children, yet it is facing challenges in identifying and reaching high-risk
groups for COVID-19, including the aged, health and social workers, and people living with multiple chronic diseases. The
country has started investing in strengthening its national immunization system, including upgrading cold chain, carrying
out communication and outreach campaigns, improving infrastructure, strengthening data systems, and preparing trained
and motivated vaccinators. Along with other countries, North Macedonia will need to develop and implement regulations
that ensure vaccine quality, appropriate vaccine management, rigorous safety surveillance, and equitable access.
Investments in vaccine cold chain infrastructure also provide opportunities to improve climate sustainability.

26.     To better respond to the changing needs of the GoNM as the pandemic keeps unfolding, the Project will be
restructured to include the support for vaccines as part of the containment and mitigation measures under Component 1:
Emergency COVID-19 Response. Funding for vaccine acquisition will be reallocated from the resources originally allocated
to support unemployment benefits, creating Subcomponent 1.4 for Vaccine Acquisition and Deployment. Previously,

been assisting COVID-19 patients. In addition, excess funds will be reallocated from Subcomponent 1.3 (EUR 2.94 million)

which was anticipated in the initial Global COVID-19 MPA, will be added as part of the containment and mitigation
measures to prevent the spread of COVID-19 and deaths under Component 1: Emergency COVID-19 Response. North
Macedonia will use the following options for vaccine acquisition, financed through the national budget and the World
Bank: procurement via the COVAX Facility, direct acquisition from the EU or pharmaceutical companies, donations by
other countries, or procurement facilitated by UNICEF. Given the recent emergence of COVID-19, there is no conclusive
data available on the duration of immunity that vaccines will provide. While some evidence suggests that an enduring
response will occur, this will not be known with certainty until clinical trials follow participants for several years. As such,
this project will allow for re-vaccination efforts if they are warranted by peer-reviewed scientific knowledge at the time.
In case re-vaccination is required, limited priority populations such as health workers and the elderly will need to be
targeted for re-vaccination given constraints on vaccine production capacity and equity considerations—tradeoffs
between broader population coverage and re-vaccination.

 III. KEY RISKS

34.       The overall risk to achieving the PDO with the expanded scope for vaccination is rated High. The overall risk
rating at Approval was Substantial. This has now been upgraded to High due to the introduction of vaccine acquisition and
deployment activities in the operation. The risk associated with sector strategies and policies and other risks are rated as
High, while the risks in three categories (political and governance, fiduciary, and environmental and social) are rated as
Substantial, and the macroeconomic risk is Moderate. The large-scale acquisition and deployment of COVID-19 vaccines
entails significant risks. First, global demand for vaccines continues to exceed supply, and vaccines that meet the Bank’s
VAC may not be available to be acquired in a timely manner. Second, a mass vaccination effort will stretch current capacity.
The proposed World Bank support for GoNM to support vaccination acquisition strategies and invest in deployment
system capacity aims to mitigate these risks. The remaining risk must be considered against the risk of the country having
less timely and effective deployment of vaccines to curb the spread of COVID-19, potentially exacerbating development
gaps and eroding past development gains.

35.       Political and governance risks are rated High. The overall risk rating at Approval was Substantial. This has now
been upgraded to High, given the complexities of managing a mass vaccination campaign during a pandemic. After general
elections in August 2020, the political environment has remained stable. However, managing a mass vaccination campaign
during a pandemic is an unprecedented challenge. There are political risks related to the ability of the authorities to ensure
appropriate targeting of the Project-supported vaccines reaching priority populations, based on objective public health
criteria, and ability to manage public sentiment should there be a gap between vaccine targets and vaccine delivery. These
risks will be mitigated by: (i) implementation of the NVDP, which prioritizes intra-country allocation by population groups;
and (ii) transparent communication about the criteria for allocation to prioritized groups, which have been selected based
on the WHO/SAGE Values Framework. There are also risks related to the governance of vaccine acquisition and
deployment, such as potential fraud and substandard quality, and attempts that do not follow approved protocols to gain
access to vaccines to be administered priority populations, or for personal gain. This includes the risk of elite capture and
corruption in the implementation of the vaccination program. These could result in a destabilizing situation that could
lead to resignations or changes in political leadership, potentially leaving the country more vulnerable. Digital monitoring
of the vaccine deployment plan's implementation, including registration and rollout among priority groups, will track
compliance with the procedures in the NVDP. This will be further mitigated through the application of anti-corruption
guidelines for vaccine acquisition and deployment and robust financial management oversight of the use of funds, as
elaborated in the fiduciary risks below.

36.       The macroeconomic risk is Moderate. The overall risk rating at Approval was Substantial. This has now been
downgraded to Moderate. Before the COVID-19 crisis, the country benefited from steady economic growth, averaging 2.4
percent from 2017. Public debt was at a sustainable level, below 50 percent of gross domestic product (GDP). However,
in 2020, GDP shrank by 4.5 percent and the public deficit increased sharply, by 10 percentage points of GDP. In 2021, real
GDP is estimated to rise by 3.6, but with high downside risks given the low vaccination rollout, ongoing health security
concerns, and delayed EU accession negotiations. Nevertheless, the projected increase in GDP has prompted the change
in risk rating. The proposed restructuring specifically aims to mitigate macroeconomic risks by financing COVID-19 vaccines
and promoting prioritized deployment to vulnerable groups. The residual macroeconomic risk to the parent PDO is limited
to risks that cannot be readily mitigated, for example, risks related to significant counterpart financing or other specific
macroeconomic risks that may hinder the operation from achieving its intended results.

37.     The sector strategies and policies risk remains Substantial. The overall risk rating at Approval was Substantial and
this remains the case in the context of vaccine acquisition and deployment. While sector strategies and policies in the area
of social protection are well articulated, the development of a clear and generally acceptable health system policy and
strategy to facilitate broader health system strenghtening and integration with the public health interventions, which is
essential for effective vaccine deploymment, has so far been a challenge. However, with the involvement of WHO and
other development partners, including the World Bank, progress is being made toward achieving a consensus and creating
the basis for a stronger longer term strategy.

is anticipated to be at least substantial in North Macedonia because there is a broader social risk of inequity in access to
vaccines, such as arising from political pressures to provide vaccines to groups that are not prioritized due to need or

group for vaccination. No security forces are anticipated to be used at vaccination sites.

41.     In addition, E&S risks will be mitigated through several measures to ensure vaccine delivery targets the most

includes a mix of the elderly, people with comorbidities, and people in high-population density location such as slums and
refugee camps. The Bank will continue to provide technical and implementation support to mitigate this risk.

 IV.APPRAISAL SUMMARY

     A.     Technical, Economic, and Financial Analysis

44.      The economic rationale for investment in a COVID-19 vaccine is strong, considering the massive and continuing
health and economic losses due to the pandemic. Globally, as of June 1, 2021, more than 171 million people have been
confirmed to be infected by COVID-19 and 3.68 million have been confirmed to have died. Global economic output
declined by an estimated 4.9 percent in 2020, with cumulative losses across 2020 and 2021 exceeding US$12 trillion. The
socio-economic impact assessment of COVID-19 on households in North Macedonia conducted by the UNDP confirms the
significant negative impact of COVID-19 in the country. For example, between February and May of 2020, 16,778 persons
registered as unemployed representing 16.1 percent of the total number of unemployed people in only three months.22

45.      Successful vaccine development, production, and delivery have the potential to reverse the associated negative
impact of severe COVID-19, generating benefits that will far exceed vaccine-related costs. Indeed, a rapid and well-
targeted deployment of a COVID-19 vaccine can help reduce the increases in poverty and accelerate economic recovery.
Even at levels of imperfect effectiveness, a COVID-19 vaccine that is introduced and deployed to priority populations can
assist in significantly reducing mortality and the spread of the coronavirus, and accelerating the safe reopening of key
sectors that are impacted. It can also reverse human capital losses by ensuring schools are reopened. The effective
administration of a COVID-19 vaccine will also help to avoid the associated health care costs for additional cases of
infection and associated health-related impoverishment. Global experience with immunization against diseases shows
that by avoiding these and other health costs, vaccines are one of the best buys in public health. The pandemic is also
having dire effects on other non-COVID-19 health outcomes. The interruption of essential services associated with COVID-
19 containment measures hinder access to care for other health needs of the population, including maternal and childcare
services, which impacts morbidity and mortality. Routine immunization services have also been affected, threatening polio
eradication and potentially leading to new outbreaks of preventable diseases, with their own related deaths, illnesses,
and long-term costs. Substantial reductions in the provision of health services affect the health and productivity of the
labor force and, therefore, the economy of a country. It is worth noting that all countries affected by COVID-19 must
respond to the mental health consequences of the pandemic, which also considerably affect productivity.

46.    The noncommunicable disease (NCD) burden of disease and NCD risk profile in North Macedonia suggests that a
high population infection rate would likely be associated with many severe cases. COVID-19 case fatality rates are much

  E.     Environmental and Social

58.      Activities under the restructuring should have positive impacts as it will improve capacity for vaccine delivery,
add funds to health workers households, and provide much needed equipment for regional health care centers. But it
could also cause environmental, health, and safety risks due to the dangerous nature of the pathogen (COVID-19) and

and chemical waste, and other hazardous by-products that could be injurious to human health. These risks will be

60.      Key social risks associated with the vaccination deployment include inequitable distribution of the vaccine,

their vulnerability to poor health, including chronic disease. Roma people are at greater risk of contracting COVID-19 and
developing more severe forms of the disease due to their generally lower socio-economic status, living in dense
settlements, and other negative social determinants of health. Cooperation with grassroots Roma civil society
organizations, community leaders, and religious leaders can ensure access to reliable information regarding the vaccines,
facilitate enrollment of Roma people on the vaccination lists, and help increase vaccination uptake. The GoNM is providing
COVID-19 vaccination services free of any charges to all its eligible population and is continuously monitoring the
enrollment of vulnerable and at-risk populations. Additionally, the GoNM is working with UNICEF to complete a
comprehensive risk communication plan to further ensure access to reliable information regarding vaccines in hard to

anticipated to be used at vaccination sites. Parliamentarians have also been a priority for vaccination at Stage 2 of the

vaccinating public figures such as parliamentarians is one of the strategies being used to address vaccine hesitancy and
bolster a resilient demand for vaccines in the population.

Emergency COVID-19 Response Project workers will be eight hours per workday, 40 hours per week. While the health
care workers employed in hospitals, health centers, and laboratories are not strictly considered direct or contracted
workers under ESS2, due to the health and safety risks of exposure to COVID-19 they will be included in the LMP as
contracted workers to ensure that they are provided with safety and health measures and equipment in the workplace.
The LMP have been prepared and will apply to all components of the Project under implementation and those introduced
by this restructuring. In terms of the workers’ grievance mechanisms, all identified stakeholders within the Emergency
COVID-19 Response Project can submit a complaint/suggestion regarding the Project implementation to the PMU MLSP
contact person. In situations where the PMU is not able to address the issue verified through the grievance mechanism
or if action is not required, the PMU will provide a detailed explanation/justification as to why the issue was not/could
not be addressed. If a grievance remains open, the complainant will be given opportunity to appeal to the MLSP’s formal
ministry-level 2nd tier Complaint Commission—part of the administrative proceedings. At all times, complainants may
seek other legal remedies in accordance with the legal framework of North Macedonia, including formal judicial redress.

  F.     Gender

     G.   Citizen engagement

68.      Mechanisms to engage citizens, and target beneficiaries more specifically, in providing ideas and feedback on
program delivery, are helpful in identifying gaps at the point of service delivery, such as information availability, access

     H.   Climate Vulnerability and Resilience

69.     North Macedonia, like many other countries, has been affected by both geophysical and climate-related
hazards. Some of these have been increasing in frequency and magnitude. Between 1990 and 2014, about 48 percent of
the recorded disasters in North Macedonia was attributed to flooding involving 11 events in 2002, 2003, 2004, 2005, 2013,
2015, and 2016. These disasters accounted for an average annual monetary loss of around 22 percent over the same
period. With respect to future trends, the only risk classified as “high” relates to river flooding, with the central northern
areas expected to be most frequently affected. Extreme temperatures are identified as the next hazard affecting the
country, accounting for 26 percent of the key hazards; six major events took place between 2012, 2014, and 2017.
Between 1961 and 2012, the number of summer days has increased significantly, as well as the number of tropical nights,
with cold waves occurring less frequently. During the period 2025-2100, models predict an average increase in air
temperature to be 1⁰C by 2025, 1.9⁰C by 2050, 2.9⁰C by 2075, and 3.8⁰C by 2100. The remaining tracked events – drought,
epidemic, wildfire, and earthquake –contributed less than 4 percent each to the hazard occurrences between 1900 and
2018. The country’s extensive use of fossil fuels, particularly the use of domestic lignite for electricity production, means
that there is the potential for the country to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. The GoNM, which ratified the Paris Accord
in 2018, has therefore prepared the Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), which focuses on climate change
mitigation with a view to reducing these emissions by 30 percent, and 36 percent at a higher level of ambition. The GoNM
also recognizes that adaptation is no less important and more detailed analysis, from the point of view of NDC needs, will
be conducted by the GoNM in the future.

70.      The restructuring seeks to address climate vulnerability and enhance resilience and adaptation, as well as
mitigating greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions through several activities. Activities under this Subcomponent 1.2 will
leverage the opportunity to invest in sustainable, climate-friendly, energy-efficient cold chain infrastructure, including for
transportation, minor works, and storage equipment. To protect vaccines from exposure to extreme temperatures, the
vaccines will be kept in the refurbished facilities through energy efficient equipment, such as low global warming potential
refrigerants for solar refrigerators and freezers, cyclopentane insulation, solar direct drive on-site freezers, sterling cycle
refrigeration with helium as the coolant for ultra-low freezers, and non-energy consuming coolant packs in shipping units.
This is a climate adaptation and mitigation measure as: (i) it ensures a reduction in possible vaccine wastage due to
exposure to climate-related extreme heat events; and (ii) the vaccine is kept in non-energy-consuming cool packs thereby
reducing GHGs through reduced energy use. Further, WHO also requires temperature monitoring devices in all vaccine
shipments, which is another climate adaptation measure to prevent wastage. With respect to the use of safety boxes for
the disposal of syringes, this is also an adaptation and mitigation measure as: (i) it reduces the risk of exposure to medical
waste during flooding and other climate related extreme events; and (ii) disposal of syringes in this manner does not
require incineration and hence would reduce GHG emissions. Subcomponent 1.2 will also strengthen supply chain systems
for storage and distribution of vaccines, including prioritizing energy efficiency or resource-use efficiency through less
carbon intensive cold chain resources and the adoption of climate-sensitive approaches when possible. All the described
activities will make the population, health, and social protection systems more resilient to the ongoing impacts and
potential emergencies associated with climate change.
