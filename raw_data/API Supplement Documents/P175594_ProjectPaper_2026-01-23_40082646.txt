    I. PROJECT STATUS AND RATIONALE FOR RESTRUCTURING

A. Background

       1   The approval of the Division Director is being sought to proceed with a Level Two Restructuring of the Côte
           d’Ivoire Productive Social Safety Nets System Strengthening Program (PSSN, P175594, IDA credit CI-7109). The
           Program for Results (PforR) was approved on May 19, 2022, in the amount of a EUR 180.3 million (US$200 million)
           IDA credit (CI-7109) and a US$5 million grant from the Global Financing Facility (GFF), bringing total financing to
           US$205 million and with a total Program cost of US$385.5 million, including an Investment Project Financing (IPF)
           component of US$12.5 million. The PforR became effective on December 6, 2022. The restructuring would: (i)
           revise some of the remaining Disbursement Linked Results (DLRs) and reprogram EUR 28.61 million to maximize
           their contribution to the existing PDO, which remains relevant; (ii) adjust the results framework and (iii) update
           the Program Expenditure Framework (PEF) and the Program Action Plan (PAP) accordingly. The proposed
           restructuring has been requested by the Government of Côte d’Ivoire in a letter dated September 3, 2025. It was
           recommended as a conclusion of the Mid-term Review (MTR) of June 2025. The restructuring responds to the
           need to better align results with implementation realities and evolving country priorities.
B. Program Status

       2   The PSSN PforR is progressing satisfactorily toward achieving the program development objective (PDO) and all
           implementation ratings have been Satisfactory and Moderately Satisfactory since its inception. Following the
           MTR, completed in June 2025, the Program was rated Satisfactory – a rating confirmed following the supervision
           mission of December 2025. Disbursements are on schedule, with rates of 77.8 percent1 from IDA-71090 and 71
           percent from TF-B8338 as of 8 December 2025.2 Out of a total of 29 DLRs, 14 were fully achieved and 1 partially
           achieved, for a total of EUR 95.23 million. Disbursements include EUR 43.3 million advance, processed January
           23rd, 2023, to be recovered through the achievement of remaining DLRs in 2026. Preparations for the verification
           of 3 DLRs in early 2026 are ongoing, for results valued up to EUR 29.7 million. The IPF component, which provides
           necessary complementary technical assistance, has disbursed EUR 2.8 out of EUR 6.95 million available (40
           percent). This is in line with expectations, as an uptake in evaluations, innovations and analytical endeavors is
           expected in the Program’s final year.

C. Rationale for restructuring

   6   Implementation approaches and sector priorities have partly changed, resulting in the need to revise some
       DLRs. The Programme multisectoriel d’Appui au système national des Filets Sociaux (PAFS), the government
       program supported by this PforR, included several new areas of intervention that were untested at the time of
       approval, in particular concerning Result Areas 1 and 3. As activities started and implementation realities became
       evident, operational approaches were adjusted, resulting in a disconnect with some DLIs/DLRs. Specifically:

         a. The institutional anchoring and operational approach of the Unique Social Registry (Registre Social Unique,
            RSU) were revised. DLR 1.2 and 1.3, as currently formulated, are no longer relevant. First, the RSU’s
            operationalization responsibility was moved from the General Directorate for the Fight against Poverty to
            the BCPFS (both under the Ministry of National Cohesion, Solidarity and Fight against Poverty, MCNSLP), in
            September 2024, resulting in a strategic and operational shift, as well as an acceleration of previously stalled
            progress. While expansion continues to be a priority, an increased focus is also placed on generating
            demand for RSU usage across government programs and improved quality of the RSU as a targeting tool.
            Second, the government shifted from an approach based solely on expensive mass surveys covering entire
            urban quarters and villages, to a more efficient, sustainable and demand-driven model. The MTR confirmed
            that it is neither feasible nor cost-effective to conduct large-scale door-to-door (active search) surveys of
            the entire populations of villages and urban quarters every three years to perform RSU data updates. In the

               long term, interoperability with other systems (e.g. vital records, employment or tax registries) could be
               introduced to support regular updates, but this move is premature. The restructuring reflects the new two-
               track RSU model for both expansion and updates, which includes targeted surveys in remote or particularly
               vulnerable villages while also introducing RSU “guichets” (registration points)3 and communication efforts
               for continuous coverage expansion and updates for vulnerable households in urban and less remote rural
               areas. Third, changes in the official classification of villages and neighborhood make the use of the National
               Statistical Institute’s 2018 list as denominator for the coverage indicator used in DLRs no longer viable.
               Finally, delays in the RSU launch combined with the sensitivity of mass registration and data collection in
               2025, an election year, requires a revision of the timeline to achieve expanded coverage results. Despite the
               changes, the DLR targets, combined with progress in the early years of the PforR, still aim to contribute to
               the same PDO indicator’s target on coverage of the RSU, set at 2.1 million households.

           b. Government priorities related to the social centers’ delivery model (DLI 3) have shifted away from
              implementing large-scale reforms of social centers, towards leveraging these structures in support of the
              CMU enrolment drive and facilitating affiliation of PTMP beneficiaries to the CMU’s non-contributory health
              insurance scheme (RAM). The shift in focus was requested by the Minister of Employment and Social
              Protection (MEPS), who raised CMU enrolments as one of the highest priorities for the country in 2024 and
              2025. The refined focus, strong government ownership and smoother inter-ministerial coordination
              enabled the social centers pilot to start in 2025 following two years of delays. The pilot operations manual
              was developed and officially adopted in July 2025, triggering an acceleration of activities.

           c. The more limited level of maturity of the country’s shock response ecosystem than originally envisaged
              became visible after implementation and evaluation of the pilot under DLI 9 in Year 3. The sector priority
              has been redefined, away from formulating an ambitious multi-ministry financing framework for shock
              response – towards socializing institutions on the role of adaptive social protection in this ecosystem. This
              requires dropping DLR 9.4 and supporting the new priority through intensified IPF component’s support.

    7    The restructuring also seizes opportunities to integrate new priorities, in particular laying the ground for
         innovation on digital financial inclusion and young women and girls’ empowerment. Since its inception in 2017,
         the PTMP has been regularly examining lessons from implementation, progressively evolving into an integrated
         community platform for human capital and productive economic inclusion. This evolution continues, as
         monitoring and dedicated analytics have identified the potential for the PTMP to more deliberately advance
         gender outcomes at scale, building on its existing approaches to women’s empowerment. Two areas have
         emerged for deeper future engagement, which reflect government and World Bank’s current priorities: (i) digital
         financial inclusion for women and men, and (ii) empowerment of young women and adolescents, with a focus on
         those who are no longer in the education system. The restructuring proposes the introduction of two foundational
         DLRs on these new areas of engagement, to signal the emerging vision for the medium-term expansion of the
         program. The new DLRs would benefit from funds linked to DLRs not fully achieved.

    8    The RF, PEF and PAP need to be revised in line with DLI adjustments and the experience of the first three years
         of implementation. In addition to ensuring coherence between DLRs, indicators and targets, the restructuring
         provides an opportunity for simplification and inclusion of relevant WBG Scorecard Indicators in the RF. The PEF,

    II. DESCRIPTION OF PROPOSED CHANGES

A. Change in DLRs and reprogramming EUR 28.61 million (US$ 33.45 million)4 under the PforR Component

      9   DLI 1 (National coverage of the RSU reached) remains relevant, but its DLRs require reformulation, new targets
          and removal of timebound requirements. The two remaining DLRs will be revised as follows:

            a. DLR 1.2 (90% of rural villages, whose socio-economic household data are recorded in the RSU MIS in Year
               4, EUR 4.05 million) is revised to: 6,400 rural villages have their socio-economic household data recorded in
               the RSU MIS through active outreach5. About 5,300 villages have been surveyed for inclusion in the RSU thus
               far, of which 1,001 in 2025, based on the newly updated Management Information System (MIS) and
               approach accounting for availability of on-demand registration. While 6,400 villages represent about 75%
               of 2018 official villages (and possibly more of today’s official villages, given changes in classification that
               make percentage measurement not feasible for this indicator), the level of ambition is not reduced, but
               rather shifted. Greater focus is placed on prioritizing villages for active surveys that are remote, thus
               minimizing exclusion of the most vulnerable areas, maximizing impact in reaching the poor and securing
               efficiencies through on-demand enrolments (DLR 1.3) for those rural areas close to urban centers and RSU
               registration points. This revised approach and lower nominal target is more realistic in view of the increased
               qualitative ambition and the pilot’s lesson that a maximum of about 1000 villages per year can feasibly be
               surveyed at this time, especially given the parallel mass enrolment for the CMU.6 The DLR encourages
               further registration efforts in 2026, while rewarding 2025 progress. The funds allocated to this revised DLR
               remain the same. The disbursement formula and verification protocol are detailed in Table 1.

            b. DLR 1.3 (80% of urban neighborhoods, whose socio-economic household data are recorded in the RSU MIS
               in Year 4, EUR 4.05 million) is revised to: 500,000 households have their socio-economic data recorded in the
               RSU MIS through the on-demand approach. The former and new indicators are not comparable. First, the
               classification of neighborhoods is ever changing and imprecise, making it methodologically not feasible to

               and sustainable approach to extending RSU coverage. An ongoing external evaluation is expected to provide

10 DLI 2 (RSU data has been updated) will be revised to Dynamism and utilization of the RSU are introduced. The
   original DLI is no longer considered relevant nor consistent with the government’s vision for the RSU. The revised
   DLI places greater emphasis on sustainability, ownership by local actors, and the desire to ensure the RSU is used
   for targeting across social programs. Regular updates (captured by the original DLI) are only one small part of the
   registry’s dynamism sought. Therefore, although the target for household-level data updates is reduced compared
   to the original DLI (approximately 40%, compared to the 80% in the original DLR), this is complemented by a wider
   set of DLRs and broader ambition. Based on these changed priorities, reflecting global best practices, the three
   remaining DLRs (2.2, 2.3 and 2.4, for a total of US$13 million) will be dropped. They will be replaced by four DLRs
   capturing different aspects of the initial stages of the RSU’s dynamism and utilization:

      a. New DLR 2.5 (An updated formula is integrated into the SIG-RSU to determine poor and vulnerable
         households). This DLR supports the adoption of a new methodology (SWIFT) to estimate poverty that
         reflects more recent living standards than the 2018 formula currently used for the proxy-means testing,
         corrects model decay, and is easier to update in the future. As such, it introduces a new element of
         dynamism and improves RSU targeting accuracy. The new methodology will allow the introduction of shock-
         responsive variables into the assessment of living standards to support development of RSU systems for
         adaptive social protection. This DLR will disburse EUR 1 million. The disbursement formula and verification
         protocol are detailed in Table 1.

      b. New DLR 2.6 (Households from 1,700 villages with RSU data older than 3 years have their data systematically
         updated). While a mass operation to update all outdated RSU data is neither realistic nor a priority,
         progressive data updates continue to be one of the relevant elements for a dynamic RSU. This DLR
         introduces a systematic, efficient and prioritized approach to updating socio-economic data, alongside the
         on-demand option at the fixed guichets. For example, households in villages exiting the 3-year PTMP
         program (whose RSU data are about 3.5-4 years old) will systematically be surveyed going forward. Both
         PTMP beneficiary and non-beneficiaries will be invited to update their information, through mobile guichets
         placed in their villages, staffed with social workers supporting the PTMP. In 2026, this process alone will
         affect 1,700 villages, and at least 50% of households within them, reaching between 20 and 40 percent of
         total households with RSU data older than 3 years. While this may look lower than the original 80 percent
         target for updates, the DLI overall is not comparable, as a wider set of priorities offsets the depth of this
         indicator with greater depth of RSU dynamism and usage targets. This DLR will disburse EUR 5 million. The
         disbursement formula and verification protocol are detailed in Table 1.

      c. New DLR 2.7 (350 guichets RSU, including at least 35 “guichets avancés” in high-need areas as defined in
         the operations manual, have been open and operational for at least 3 months). This DLR promotes the novel
         approach of establishing registration points (guichets) across the country, which would support on-demand
         registration as well as on-demand data updates. 287 guichets were established in 2025, mostly within the
         regional directorates of social ministries and social centers, thus concentrated in the main urban centers.

11 DLI 3 (Social centers service delivery model enhanced) remains relevant overall, but DLRs are revised. The new
   indicators support better alignment between the DLI and the application of the service delivery model being
   tested, which places greater emphasis on the promotion of CMU enrolment and awareness among vulnerable
   populations to promote access to health care. With the time-bound DLR regarding approval of the operations
   manual met late (DLR 3.1) and the revision of a social centers’ overall model no longer relevant nor prioritized
   (DLR 3.2), new DLRs are proposed as follows:

     a. New DLR 3.3 (95% of PTMP household heads and their spouses in the social center pilot areas are enrolled
        in the CMU and have their CMU number registered in the RSU MIS). The new result incentivizes both the
        effective support to stimulate enrolments and the mutualization of efforts by social programs, delivered by
        social centers with increased capacity. By aiming for near-universal CMU enrolment of PTMP beneficiaries,
        intended to capture both household heads and their spouses, the DLR measures impact of social centers’
        improved outreach efforts. The systematic integration of CMU information in the RSU captures also the
        efforts at mutualizing efforts in service delivery to the poorest. This DLR will disburse EUR 2.7 million. The
        disbursement formula and verification protocol are detailed in Table 1.

     b. New DLR 3.4 (50% of PTMP household heads in the pilot areas know and understand the CMU, including the
        non-contributory RAM scheme). This result measures the impact of enhanced targeted sensitization efforts
        by social centers, which contributes to improving access to healthcare among poor and vulnerable
        households. The baseline survey in mid-2025 revealed that less than 10 percent of PTMP household heads
        had any knowledge or understanding of the basic elements of the non-contributory scheme of the CMU
        which is specifically designed to cover this category of individuals. Achieving 50% target will require stepping
        up communication efforts. This DLR will be verified through the survey and is a reformulation of an existing
        intermediate indicator. This DLR will disburse EUR 1.8 million. The disbursement formula and verification
        protocol are detailed in Table 1.

A. Revision of the Results Framework
   6   Minor adjustments to PDO indicators are needed, to ensure they remain measurable and relevant, and are
       aligned with revised DLRs. Changes are summarized below:

         a. Indicator “Beneficiaries of RAM-CMU in regions where the social centers pilot takes place (number)” will be
            replaced with “Share of PTMP household heads and their spouses in the social center pilot areas who are
            enrolled in the CMU and have their CMU number registered in the RSU MIS”. The original indicator had
            achieved 209,138 beneficiaries as of December 2025 (66.4 percent of the original target), but a different
            methodology was used than originally planned, as CNAM did not share reliable information from their
            registry (which is beyond the control of this PforR). The indicator thus required a dedicated survey to
            measure the number of PTMP beneficiaries enrolled in the CMU and eligible to the RAM, in the regions of
            the pilot social centers. The geographic coverage of the revised pilot approach is also different than
            originally envisioned, with a deeper focus on social centers’ catchment areas and more social centers
            included – resulting in an unrealistic target. The revision will allow for alignment of the indicator to the new
            DLI3, relying on an actual baseline and realistic targets based on administrative data from 2025. The same
            approach will be adopted for the gender-disaggregated sub-indicator.

         b. Indicator “Social programs using at least 2 of the safety nets system tools (grievance redress mechanism,
            payment system, social centers, unique social registry, targeting mechanism)” will be slightly reworded to
            include also “social workers, PTMP community delivery platforms” to ensure the list is exhaustive and
            clarifies the full extent of existing safety net system tools used for synergistic approaches. The target is
            unchanged.

B. Changes to the Program Expenditure Framework (PEF)

     8   The PEF will be adjusted to reflect changes in the implementation of the program. The financing agreement was
         signed in June 2022, but the program became effective only in December 2022. The PAFS budget line was
         introduced in the Budget Law and has received annual allocations. The Program was to be financed through an
         IDA credit of EUR 180.3 million (US$200 million, including US$192.5 million PforR and US$7.5 million IPF, a total
         of about FCFA 118.3 billion) and a GFF grant of US$5 million (about FCFA 3.15 billion). As of end November 2025,
         the total amount mobilized was FCFA 151.2 billion: from the Government Budget, FCFA 46.7 billion between 2022
         and 2025; from IDA, FCFA 104.4 billion between 2023 and 2025.10 The detailed budget and execution information
         can be found in annual budget reports by the Treasury (Système Intégré de Gestion des Operations Budgétaires
         de l’Etat, codes 90103000007 for PTMP and code 90103000005 for the Registry (RSU). The details about execution
         of the budget of the program (every 6 months) is reported in “Sources et Utilisation des fonds (par semestre)”,
         PAFS, BCPFS, 2023-2026.

     9   The PAFS has displayed strong Government ownership and continuous expansion of coverage of its core
         element (the PTMP), with support to existing beneficiaries and regular enrollment of new ones. This allowed
         progress towards the expected outcomes and achievement of the PDO and multiple DLIs. It has done so with a
         time lag, as effectiveness was only achieved 6 months after approval and resources were slower to mobilize. It
         has also done so on a smaller scale than expected on some activities (e.g. shock response pilot), which has
         generated significant budgetary savings, but with the expected regularity and rigor.

     10 The Program maintained the overall balance between the total funds available and expenditures by reducing
        the PTMP coverage for 2022-2024 and by changing some of the planned activities under RA 1. Table 3 below
        provides an overall description of the expenditures and sources of funding as executed (till end 2025) and planned
        (for 2026) in comparison with the PEF projections of 2022. The cost for the entire period of execution is expected
