    B. Introduction and Context

    Country Context
1. Recent economic development in Brazil has been characterized by a mix of positive and negative trends. Between
2001 and 2014, Brazil experienced growth averaging 3.3 percent annually in real terms. This growth was propelled by
favorable commodity prices, capital flows, and sound macroeconomic management, enabling the financing of important
social protection programs and helping lift over 27 million people out of poverty. However, by 2014, a combination of
falling commodity prices, political instability, and unaddressed structural challenges had plunged the economy into a
recession. From 2014 to 2019, real annual Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth averaged -0.3 percent. In 2020, the
COVID-19 pandemic caused a decline in the GDP growth of -3.3 percent. By 2021, the unemployment rate jumped to 14
percent.
2. Following a rebound from the COVID-19 pandemic, economic growth slowed down. In 2021, the post-COVID-19
recovery began with a rebound in economic growth of 5 percent. This surge was fueled by fiscal stimulus, a successful
COVID-19 vaccination, favorable conditions in commodity markets, and high demand for services. From 2022 to 2023,
economic growth reached 3 percent, driven by agriculture, exports, and fiscal transfers to low-income households.
Projections indicate a further decline in economic growth to 2 percent over the next five years. Meanwhile, inflation
peaked at 12 percent year-over-year in 2022, fueled by high energy and food prices and a robust domestic economy. Tight
monetary policy resulted in a decline in inflation to 3.9 percent in 2023.
3. Brazil will experience rapid change in climate conditions and its impacts will be spatially varied. By the end of the
century, the average temperature in Brazil is expected to rise, depending on the trajectory of global Greenhouse Gas
(GHG) emissions, by 1.7 °C to 5.3 °C from the 1986–2005 average1. All of Brazil’s biomes are vulnerable to the impact of
changes in temperature and rainfall, though with significant regional differences2. In rural areas, more frequent and severe
climate events such as droughts and floods are likely to cause major impacts on agriculture productivity, logistics (lowering
conditions of rural roads) and delivery of water and sanitation services due to dwindling water supply.
4. In the last months of 2023 and first months of 2024, the state of Santa Catarina experienced various extreme
weather events, highlighting the vulnerabilities of the state to climate change. These changes threaten rural and fishing

    Sectoral and Institutional Context
5. Brazil’s agricultural sector is highly heterogenous. There are nearly 5 million rural properties nationwide distributed
over 65 million of hectares of land. Nearly 3.9 million rural properties are classified as family farms.3,4 They represent 77
percent of all rural properties in Brazil. The Northeast region holds the highest concentration of family farmers, accounting
for 47 percent of all family farms in Brazil, followed by the South region (18 percent), the Southeast region (17 percent), .
the North region (12 percent), and the Center-West region (5 percent). These family farmers co-exist with 1.1 million
small, medium, and large-scale non-family, more commercial farmers. Family farmers exhibit significant diversity: some
are highly productive and commercially oriented, embracing technological innovations5, while others follow subsistence-
oriented farming practices, lack market access, and rely on traditional farming methods.
6. In Santa Catarina, the productive structure of agriculture is characterized by the strength of family farming and the
predominance of small rural properties. In the state, 183,000 agricultural establishments are registered, 78 percent of
which are family farms, with a total area of 6.45 million hectares and an average area of 35.2 ha per establishment. The
agriculture sector employs more than 500,000 people.6
7. Brazil’s agrifood sector plays an important role in the national economy and livelihoods of the poor . The primary
agriculture sector accounts for 5 percent of the national GDP, nearly USD 175 billion. Considering the back and forward
linkages along the entire agrifood system, ranging from input providers, farmers, agribusiness firms and distributors, its
contribution to the national economy reaches 26 percent of GDP. The primary agricultural sector employs 15 million
people, accounting for 20 percent of the national workforce. Brazil is now a top-5 producer of 34 agricultural commodities,
and it is the largest net agrifood exporter in the world. Half of its agrifood production, USD 123.2 billion, is sold in
international markets. The agrifood exports represent 37 percent of Brazil’s total exports.
8. Agriculture in Santa Catarina has the 10th highest added value in Brazil and contributes 6.7 percent of the value
added by the local economy. The 2023 Value of Agricultural Production (VPA) reached USD 13.28 billion and in the last
10 years it has had an average real growth of more than 2.5 percent per year. Despite the great diversification of
agriculture in Santa Catarina, almost 60 percent of VPA results from just four activities: swine, 20.2 percent; poultry, 16.4
percent; milk, 12.3 percent; and soybeans, 10.9 percent. In 2023, state agribusiness exports had the second-best
performance in history, with US$7.49 billion exported, 65 percent of the total value of Santa Catarina's exports in 2023
and 4.5 percent of those exported by Brazilian agriculture.

poverty estimated at 38.7 percent, more than double the urban poverty rate7. Also, it is estimated that one percent of

11. Despite the recent growth and future potential of the agrifood sector in Brazil, there are several challenges to be
overcome.
12. Challenge #1 – Delivery of last-mile agricultural public goods and services. The literature shows that agricultural
public goods like rural infrastructure (rural roads, electrification, irrigation, information and communication technology,
etc.) and services (land administration, food safety and agriculture health, agriculture innovation, etc.) are fundamental
for addressing food security and sustainable development.12,13 Brazil invests only one percent of its agricultural GDP on
agricultural public goods and services, compared to the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD)
average of 3.4 percent.14 As a result, few farmers benefit from them (i.e., 20 percent of farmers benefit from agricultural
extension services) and 63 percent of the rural population has experienced food insecurity. This low coverage is
compounded by the poor-quality of agriculture public goods and services provided.15 Roads are another example of the
challenges of delivering such agricultural public goods. Rural roads near agriculture production areas are often unpaved
and in poor condition16, leading to high transport cost for inputs and outputs.17 This problem is significant for family
farmers located in remote areas.
13. In Santa Catarina, the vast majority of rural communities are located in remote areas, leading to socioeconomic
isolation due to deficient communication infrastructure for Internet access and mobile telephony. Additionally, the
logistical structure is compromised by undersized and poorly maintained roads, which hinders the transport of inputs and
production, thereby limiting the development of regions and production chains. With regard to support to agricultural

14. Challenge #2 - Building assets for an inclusive agrifood system. Most family farmers struggle with poverty, with
household incomes falling below the USD 1.24 daily threshold. This struggle can be attributed, in part, to the lack of
minimum asset endowment. In Santa Catarina, there are significant challenges in the inclusion of family farmers with
limited land and impoverished rural populations, as well as Traditional People and Communities (TPCs). 20 percent
(37,000) of rural producers in Santa Catarina have farms primarily for subsistence production and there are 67,700
agricultural establishments (37 percent of the total) with less than 10 hectares.19 Current literature highlights the
significance of minimum asset ownership for production.20 Also, recent research provides compelling evidence for the
effectiveness of supporting rural households through various productive inclusion phases using a multifaceted "graduation
approach21" and "productive alliances�?22 programs.23 These approaches and programs, combining productive asset
transfers, technical and business management assistance, and access to saving accounts, have demonstrably led to long-
term sustained increases in income and consumption for participating family farmers, therefore, lifting them out of
poverty.
15. Challenge # 3 – Improving agrifood system sustainability. Brazil’s agrifood sector accounts for nearly one third of
total national GHG emissions. When combined with land-use change and forestry, the agrifood system’s contribution
reaches 60 percent of the national GHG emissions.24 In 2019, Brazil was the fourth largest global consumer of fertilizers.
Overuse of fertilizer is associated with leaching of nitrates into groundwater, emission of nitrogen oxides, pollution of soil
with toxic heavy metals, and surface runoff of nitrogen and phosphorus. Furthermore, family farmers are highly exposed
to extreme weather events (i.e., high spatial and temporal rainfall variability), which combined with low asset’s
endowment and income levels, make them highly vulnerable to climate change.
16. In Santa Catarina, the agricultural sector contributes around 36 percent of total emissions estimates in the state
(Estimative System of GHG Emissions - Sistema Estimativa de Emissão de Gases - SEEG, 2021), a situation that places Santa
Catarina in 15th place in the ranking of emissions by state with gross emissions of 59 MtCO 2 equivalent per year. In
addition, the use of outdated farm technologies and farming practices leads to soil degradation and water pollution. To
address the issue, a simultaneous approach is recommended: (i) implementing environmental policies to halt agricultural
frontier expansion (i.e., turning unassigned public lands into protected areas, improving the implementation of the rural

17. Challenge #4 - Agrifinancing for sustainable agrifood system development. Returns from investing in agrifood
production can be highly uncertain because of significant variations in natural conditions and volatility in agrifood markets.
Availability of credit heavily influences agrifood systems (i.e., crop and livestock selection, type of production technologies
and practices, etc). In Brazil, the public sector remains a significant contributor of the agrifinance market providing 33
percent of total credit (USD 75 billion from Plano Safra). Despite the use of preferential credit schemes as a key agriculture
public support policy, only 15 percent of family farmers access credit lines, indicating a pressing need for improved
agrifinance accessibility to family farmers.26 Furthermore, the current available agrifinance instruments could be
strengthened to provide incentives for the adoption of CSA practices and technologies, contributing towards improvement
in agrifood productivity, environmental goals, and improved livelihoods.
18. Challenge # 5 – Enhancing women participation in agrifood system development. Despite comprising over 32
percent of Brazil’s farmers, women face marginalization within the agrifood system. Sociocultural norms limit their access
to essential resources such as land, water, financing, inputs, and innovation services.27 Furthermore, women
disproportionately bear the burden of unpaid domestic and caregiving responsibilities. They spend 2.3 times as much time
on unpaid domestic and caregiving work than men.28 Moreover, women’s leadership within farmers’ organizations, such
as cooperatives and community associations, remain underrepresented.29 Recent findings indicate that female-owned
properties are 61 percent less productive30, on average, than male-owned ones.31 Female farmers yields are 20-30 percent
smaller than those of male farmers.32 Additionally, agricultural commercialization is lower when farmers' organizations
are led by women, as compared to those led by men.33 Addressing these gender inequalities in the agrifood system of
Brazil could improve yields, incomes, and food security.

  Key Results (From PCN)
        1. Program Results Chain


20. The MPA leverages recent research on agrifood system development, acknowledging that a singular approach
cannot address the multifaceted causes of lack of access to markets, low productivity, poor resilience to shocks, and
food insecurity. Recognizing the context-specific nature of these challenges, the program’s Theory of Change (ToC)
depicted in Figure 1 outlines a causal pathway towards achieving program impacts through a three-pillared approach: (i)
pillar 1 will improve access to direct support for targeted family farmers and their POs, thereby reducing financial
constraints for investment in productive activities; (ii) pillar 2 aims to enhance the delivery of last mile agricultural public
goods and services, which will improve the enabling conditions for the agrifood system to develop; (iii) pillar 3 will support
governments to enhance monitoring and evaluation of public policies and programs to repurpose them to foster green,
resilient, and inclusion within the agrifood system. The MPA’s pillars are presented in more detail in the framework
section. Incorporated in the overall MPA strategy is a set of activities (social, agronomic, and financial measures) designed
to increase resiliency of farmers to shocks (e.g., climate change).
21. The successful delivery of expected outcomes in the MPA hinges upon several key assumptions: (i) family farmer’s
constraints to adopting and maintaining new agricultural technologies and practices (higher-yielding, more profitable,
resilience building technologies) do not significantly change; (ii) timely development of national-level governance
frameworks to facilitate coordination and cooperation between federal and state government levels throughout program
implementation; (iii) political support to the establishment of effective monitoring and evaluation systems to validate
outcomes and steer implementation efforts; (iv) food consumers are aware of the benefits of nutrient-rich food; and (v)
there is no significant changes to the sectoral environment (business/policy) that affects family farms profitability and/or
productivity. The MPA is designed to recognize that the impact pathway from activities to be implemented by each phase
to the MPA’s impacts is long, complex, and context specific.

 D. Concept Description


23. The project aims at increasing resilience of family farmers by lifting the bottlenecks that prevent their sustainable
intensification and development. The project will support family farmers in targeted areas to (i) improve the agrifood
system sustainability, through the adoption of climate-smart agriculture (CSA) practices, sustainable management of
natural resources and environmental regularization, (ii) build minimum asset ownership for agrifood production, through
productive assets transfers, access to finance and innovations, technical and managerial assistance, and (iii) delivery of
last-mile agriculture public goods and services by supporting farms connection to rural roads, on-farm electrification and
digital connectivity, access to information. The project will also provide support to the State of Santa Catarina to integrate
this approach into its agricultural policies and programs.
24. The project will target areas where economic, environmental and inclusion challenges overlap and hinders
sustainable development of the agrifood system. Economic indicators may include poverty level, access to markets and
agriculture public goods and services, environmental indicators may include water resources, deforestation rates,
inclusion indicators may include gender, identification as Traditional People and Communities (TPCs). Project preparation
will allow to map those areas, with the support of available information at the State level.
25. The proposed project is an Investment Project Financing (IPF) of US$120.0 million to be implemented over a six-
year period. The total project cost is estimated to be US$150.00 million, including US$30.00 million of counterpart funding
from the State of Santa Catarina and beneficiaries. The project will be implemented through 4 components: (i)
Environmental management, (ii) Entrepreneurship and Innovation, (iii) Agriculture public goods and services and (iv) Public
management of the agricultural sector.
26. Project design will build upon the successful results and lessons learned from previous projects implemented in
the State.34 Project design will also leverage lessons learned from similar on-going or completed projects in in Brazil,
funded by World Bank or other development partners35 and will benefit from the design of the first 2 phases of the MPA.


Beneficiaries
27. The Project’s direct beneficiaries are family farmers, as defined by the Brazilian law . The number of these direct
beneficiaries is estimated at 50,000 farmers in total, representing 35 percent of the State’s family farmers. To ensure
inclusion of women and Traditional People and Communities (TPC), the project will develop a targeting approach to ensure
outreach and tailored capacity building for these groups as well as disaggregate beneficiary indicators to track their
participation. In addition, indirect project beneficiaries will include: (i) institutions and organizations that will see their
capacities strengthened through the various components, (ii) rural households who will benefit from better natural
resources management and (iii) the 142,000 family farmers of the State of SC who will benefit from improved agricultural
institutions, policy monitoring and evaluation.

and restore degraded areas, increase carbon storage in soil and vegetation, reduce on-farm GHG emissions thus
contributing to climate mitigation and enhance climate resilience of family farmers.
Component 2. Agrifood Entrepreneurship and Innovation (Total cost US$52.45 million; IBRD loan US$41.96 million;
counterpart funding US$10.49 million).
30. This Component aims to increase the socio-economic inclusion and competitiveness of family farmers, by (i)
supporting access to technologies and finance of family farmers, in particular TPCs; (ii) improving access to markets; and
(iii) strengthening the agriculture public services to create an enabling environment for the market inclusion of family
farmers.
31. The component has 3 main activities: (i) support through technical assistance and access to finance demand-driven
productive investments by Family Farmers’ Organizations (FFO), including diversification and CSA improvement of
production systems, agro-processing, support to meet legal environmental and sanitary requirements, marketing and
logistics; (ii) support research, generation and development of sustainable technologies and innovations adapted to family
farmers, through partnership between the State public research agency (EPAGRI) and startups, science and technology
institutions, and small private innovation companies, and (iii) support agricultural services for increased market access
and competitiveness, in particular through enhancing dissemination, adoption and monitoring of good food safety and
sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) practices, agri-product traceability and development of agrifood quality labels (such as
geographic indication) to enable greater access to markets for agri-food products.
32. The component will ease family farmers’ capacity for investment and act as a de -risking tool to ease investment
decision making by lowering the upfront costs. The access to finance provided by the project is expected to jump start
innovations and spark the adoption of new technologies and practices to enhance competitiveness and sustainable use of
natural resources.
Component 3. Agriculture public goods (Total cost US$37.40 million).
33. This Component aims to provide last-mile agricultural public goods to family farmers benefitting from the other 2
components to improve the structural rural competitiveness framework. Investments will be supported to solve the
bottlenecks that affect the outflow of agri-food products in the State. The improvement of rural infrastructure will
enhance the quality of life in rural areas and foster the competitiveness and sustainability of agrifood businesses.

34. The Component has three main activities: (i) support digital connectivity and inclusion of targeted beneficiaries,

35. This Component aims to enhance the governance of the agrifood system, improve the monitoring and evaluation

36. The Component has three main activities: (i) support the collection and provision of data on the agrifood system and

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts



37. Environmental risk is moderate. The moderate risk rating accounts primarily for the moderate impacts from the road
rehabilitation works, which may include: the generation and disposal of construction wastes; water pollution from
construction runoff; noise, dust emissions and land degradation from quarries, construction workplace and traffic hazards
for the workers and communities; and workplace construction OHS risks. Also, moderate risks can be expected from
investments on agri-food marketing chain (food processing) such as wastewater generation and disposal (subcomponent

1.1). On the other side lower and diffuse impacts are also expected from smallholder’s (family) agriculture activities
supported by the project (subcomponent 1.2) and community-based tourism (subcomponent 1.3). The prior experience
and demonstrated capacity to manage E&S risks built from Project’s phase 1 experience has also been considered as part
of the risk rating determination.
38. The social risk rating also is Moderate. The positive social impacts of the Project far outweigh its potential negative
impacts. Although it cannot be ruled out that the recovery works of critical points of secondary roads may require the
acquisition of small areas of bare land, the Project is not expected to need land acquisition with significant adverse impacts
related to physical or economic resettlement. Adverse impacts related to the influx of workers and issues related to sexual
harassment or abuse and violence against women are also not expected. The adoption of clear socioeconomic criteria to
prioritize groups in situations of greater social and economic vulnerability in the selection of beneficiaries and participatory
processes in the planning of activities reduce the risk of elite capture. Indigenous peoples and traditional communities will
be prioritized and assisted separately from other beneficiaries due to their cultural peculiarities, with free, prior, informed
and culturally adequate consultations planned for the identification and planning of their community-driven demands.
