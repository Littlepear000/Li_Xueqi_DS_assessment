B. Introduction and Context

1. Burundi, a landlocked country with a population of 12.9 million, faces significant challenges in delivering quality and
reliable services and creating jobs for its rapidly growing population (annual growth rate of 2.7 percent). 25.9 percent of
the population has access to power, compared to the Sub-Saharan Africa average of 52 percent. Urban areas are relatively
better served: 72.5 percent of residents in the economic capital, Bujumbura, and 42.6 percent in other urban centers have
access to electricity. In stark contrast, access drops to just 1.8 percent in rural areas, severely constraining economic, social,
and human capital development.
2. GoB is committed to increase access to energy through the Energy Compact which was officially launched in September
2025. As part of the M300 initiative, GoB has embarked on developing a National Energy Compact which charts a pathway
towards reaching 70 percent electricity access and 40 percent clean cooking access by 20301. In addition, Burundi’s national
least-cost electrification strategy, adopted in 2024,2 states that 60 percent of the population will be connected to the main
grid, 28 percent to stand-alone solar solutions, and 12 percent to mini grids by 2030. The investment required to achieve
universal electricity access under SDG7 and guarantee access to clean cooking solution is estimated at US$3.49 billion, an
amount that calls for leveraging financing from the private sector3. The WB is leveraging on its active involvement in the
sector, close dialogue with the GoB, as well as its convening power to mobilize other donors active in the sector to join efforts
and provide technical assistance and financing to support GoB to meet its objective of reaching universal access by 2030.
3. Achieving these ambitious national targets will require addressing the stark disparities in energy access across the
country. While the national electrification rate is low, especially in rural areas4, this energy gap, compounded by weak digital
infrastructure, severely limits public service delivery and socio-economic opportunities. Remote health centers and schools
lack dependable power and internet connectivity are increasingly essential for digital learning, medical data management,
effective emergency communication and for maintaining infrastructure and assets through innovative remote monitoring.

8. The PDO remains to expand access to energy services for households, enterprises, schools, and health centers in rural
areas of Burundi. The AF will be an opportunity to introduce four intermediate indicators to the results framework including:
(i) two digital indicators for public facilities, (ii) an indicator on maintenance and (iii) one indicator on private solar and CEC
companies, financial institutions and other entities receiving business advisory, capacity-building and TA. Furthermore, the
Results Framework (RF) will be revised to reflect the focus on new connections through the M300 initiative and the new
scorecard (i.e. “People provided with access to electricity (Number)�?. The AF focuses on leveraging Digitalization at the
Digital-Energy Nexus to Strengthen Connectivity, Remotely Track Solar Asset Performance in Public Facilities, and Expand
Access via targeted RBF in surrounding communities.
9. The PDO aims to be achieved through 3 components: (i) Energy services for public institutions; (ii) Energy services for
households and; (iii) Technical assistance. The two-year extension will enable the project to finance and complete O&M
services, fully implement the O&M scheme for health centers, and establish a remote monitoring mechanism. Under
Component 2, the extension will (i) strengthen off-grid market development by supporting additional RBF rounds for an
additional 10 000 connections and assisting companies in the longer run, particularly through ASCENT Green, to access the
regional RBF facility, and (ii) launch the RBF program for clean cooking, which is ready for implementation.
10. The additional financing is fostering Smart Connectivity and Remote Performance Tracking of Public Facility Solar
Assets via a Unified Monitoring Platform under Component 1. The project will connect up to 800 rural health and education
facilities to high-speed satellite internet, enabling real-time system monitoring and supporting essential digital tools. Given
mobile network constraints, low-orbit satellite services from providers are proposed. The plan includes hardware
procurement and subscription costs, aligned with national digitalization efforts.
11. The additional financing is also expanding solar access through Results-Based Financing under Component 2. Building
on the success of the private sector vehicles under the project, this pillar aims to scale access to solar energy in underserved
communities where up to 800 public facilities are electrified (component 1) through results-based financing. Performance-
based incentives will be used to support private sector-led market expansion into those targeted communities, reduce
upfront costs, and stimulate demand for quality solar solutions.

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
 Environmental and Social risk is currently Moderate. Initially, the environmental risk classification was deemed
 Substantial, whereas the social risk was considered Moderate. The Substantial environmental risk was primarily
 attributed to the Recipient's limited capacity to manage environmental risks and impacts, as well as the nature of
 project activities involving hybrid systems under Component 2. Following the Mid-Term Review Mission, the

environmental risk rating was revised downward to Moderate for two main reasons: (i) the Recipient's capacity to
manage E&S risks and impacts has improved, evidenced by the current staffing of four E&S specialists; and (ii) certain
activities previously identified as potentially generating substantial risks, specifically the construction and installation
of mini-grids, are no longer financed under the project. The project continues to be implemented in accordance with
the Environmental and Social Commitment Plan (ESCP), and no major issues have been reported. The E&S compliance
performance for the Project is currently ‘Moderately Satisfactory’. All required E&S instruments have been prepared,
adopted and disclosed in-country and on the WB website. These include Environmental and Social Management
Framework, Stakeholder Engagement Plan, Labor Management Procedures, Environmental and Social Commitment
Plan and site-specific Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs). The current Additional Financing (AF) will
contribute to increase the electricity access rate in Burundi, in line with the M300 initiative and in a sustainable
manner. The additional financing focuses on leveraging Digitalization at the Digital-Energy Nexus to Strengthen
Connectivity, Remotely Track Solar Asset Performance in Public Facilities, and Expand Access via targeted RBF in
surrounding communities. It also includes a restructuring, consisting of: (i) extension of the project closing date, (ii)
reallocation of budget between categories and (iii) reflecting the adjustment of results framework. The AF project will
build on the existing Environmental and Social Management Framework (ESMF), which guides subproject screening,
risk categorization, and ESMP preparation, review, and disclosure. As the AF is financing project scale up, the existing
environmental and social instruments would not require any modification. Importantly, the AF is not expected to raise
the environmental and social risk level of the project or to make relevant a new Environmental and Social Standard
(ESS).


E. Implementation


12. The Project is implemented by a Project Implementation Unit within the Ministry of Mining Resources, Energy,
Trade and Tourism, on behalf of the Government of Burundi. The line Ministry’s main role is to lead national energy
policy design, oversee project implementation, and monitor sector results.
13. Institutional Arrangements for procurement and financial management will remain the same as in the parent
project. The PIU has dedicated procurement and financial management staff working full time on the project.
14. Financial Management (FM) and Disbursement Arrangements. The fiduciary management of the project will be
under the responsibility of the existing Implementation Unit (PIU). The FM arrangements are robust and build on the
systems and experience established under the parent project. The PIU has demonstrated adequate FM performance,
with ongoing efforts to address minor issues and strengthen capacity.
15. Procurement. The parent project was under the ‘World Bank Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers’ dated
July, 2016 and revised in November 2017 and August 2018 (Procurement Regulations). All substantial procurements
have been completed, and the current Additional Financing is to scale up the scope of the current contracts under
implementation and this will be done through contract amendments rather than conducting new procurement
processes. Therefore, no changes are expected to procurement approaches and Standard Procurement Documents
(SPDs). However, in case of any new procurement activity, the procurement will be carried out in accordance with the
Procurement Regulations for IPF Borrowers, namely Procurement in Investment Project Financing (IPF): Goods, Works,
Non-Consulting and Consulting Services, dated September 2025.
