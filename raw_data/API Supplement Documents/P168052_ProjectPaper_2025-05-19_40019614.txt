 I.    BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING

1.      This Project Paper seeks the approval for a US$25 million IDA grant to the Government of Republic of Tajikistan
for an Additional Financing to the Socio-Economic Resilience Strengthening Project (SERSP). SERSP was approved on
June 20, 2019, for an IDA grant of US$37 million, and became effective July 8, 2020. The project is implemented by the
National Social Investment Fund of Tajikistan (NSIFT).
2.      Background. The Socio-Economic Resilience Strengthening Project (SERSP) is one of three operations financed by
the International Development Association (IDA) 18 Risk Mitigation Regime (RMR) to support Tajikistan’s Resilience
Strengthening Program.
3.      Project development objective and components description. The project development objective (PDO) is to
strengthen participatory local governance, improve the quality of local infrastructure, both in targeted communities, and
increase extracurricular or livelihoods opportunities for youth. SERSP seeks to achieve these objectives through financing
community-driven investments and providing youth inclusive services and livelihoods opportunities. SERSP is structured
around three components:
(a) Component 1: Inclusive Local Development Initiatives (US$22.6 million IDA) provides subgrants to jamoats (sub-
    districts) to finance local-level, climate-resilient, socioeconomic infrastructure that addresses communities’ priorities,
    including those of women and youth, identified through a participatory village needs assessment and decision-making
    process (such as kindergartens, schools, health facilities, water supply and local electricity). It also finances technical
    design of the subprojects (SPs). The component aims to strengthen the capacity of jamoat administrations and
    community-level institutions for participatory local development, disaster risk management, and operations and
    maintenance of project investments. Community mobilization and capacity-building activities are implemented by
    Training and Facilitating Partners (TFPs) contracted by NSIFT. This component also provided resources to refurbish
    youth facilities operated by the Ministry of Labor, Migration and Employment (MoLME) and the Ministry of Education
    and Science (MoES), which were used to deliver Component 2 activities.
(b) Component 2: Youth Inclusion and Livelihoods (US$12.5 million IDA) provides youth-inclusive services (i.e. trainings
    and psychosocial support services) and livelihoods activities (including tools and equipment) to disadvantaged and
    inactive young men and women to increase their overall resilience to risks of exclusion, inactivity, and disaffection.
    Component 2 activities are implemented by Implementing Partners (IPs) contracted by NSIFT.
(c) Component 3: Project Management and Monitoring and Evaluation (US$1.9 million IDA) finances NSIFT to conduct
    project coordination and supervision, procurement, financial management, annual audits, monitoring and evaluation,
    communications activities, and management of the beneficiary feedback mechanism and the management
    information system.
4.      Geographic coverage. The project operates in four districts in the Khatlon region (Kulyob, Panj, Hamadoni, and
Farkhor) and in three districts in GBAO (Vanj, Ishkoshim, and Shugnan). The target districts were selected for having a
shared border with Afghanistan, a relatively large population, a combination of rural and urban areas, and limited coverage
by other donor interventions. Two additional districts, Shamsiddin Shohin in Khatlon province and Darvoz in GBAO
province, were added at restructuring.
5.      Project restructuring. The project was restructured once in May 2023. The restructuring involved: (i) reallocation
of US$4.7 million to Subcomponent 1a and US$0.2 million to Subcomponent 1b to expand the subcomponents ’ activities
to two new districts, Shamsiddin Shohin in Khatlon region, and Darvoz on in Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Oblast
(GBAO) region; (ii) revising the target for an intermediate-level indicator related to Subcomponent 2b; (iii) extending the
project’s closing date by 18 months from June 30, 2024 to December 31, 2025; and (iv) aligning the results indicators’ end
targets with the project’s new closing date. In addition, the restructuring removed a legal covenant for the establishment

6.      PDO and implementation progress ratings. The project’s progress towards achievement of the PDO and its
implementation progress are currently rated as Moderately Satisfactory, reflecting the steady and satisfactory progress
in implementing project activities. The project is in its last year of implementation, with US$32.48 million (91%) disbursed
and the remaining US$3.28 million is committed to be disbursed by December 31, 2025, based on current disbursement
schedule. Three of the four PDO results indicators already exceed targets. PDO3 “percentage of youth beneficiaries
(male/female) who continue their livelihood activities after receiving livelihood tools and equipment” has not yet reported
progress, because it will only be measured after the related livelihood support activities have been completed.
7.      Implementation progress of SERSP is summarized below:
a) Component 1: Inclusive Local Development Initiatives: Social mobilization for the original 7 districts, 44 jamoats in
   GBAO and Khatlon resulted in the prioritization of 193 subprojects. Of these, 153 are completed and the remaining
   40 subprojects are scheduled to be completed by August 2025. In the two districts (Sh. Shohin and Darvoz) added
   during the restructuring, 15 subprojects are under implementation and are expected to be completed by September
   2025. Around 75,000 community members (56% women) across 55 jamoats and 507 villages were mobilized, leading
   to formulation of Village and Jamoat development plans, as well as village-led prioritization of 208 social infrastructure
   subprojects. Other donors have supported 53 subprojects that communities identified in village and jamoat
   development plans, but were not selected for SERSP funding. Communities have expressed high levels of satisfaction
   with the project’s decision-making processes via the project’s social audits. Under sub-component 1c, NSIFT
   completed the renovation and upgrading of 17 youth facilities, which have been utilized to provide youth-inclusive
   services under Component 2 and handed the facilities over to the Ministry of Labor, Migration and Employment and
   the Ministry of Education and Science.
b) Component 2: Youth Inclusion and Livelihoods: Under sub-component 2a, targets for all youth trainings have been met
   (digital literacy, Mind Masters, Upshift, healthy lifestyle, psychosocial support). Under subcomponent 2b: Youth
   Livelihoods, an initial cohort of 1,255 beneficiaries (47% women) received training, prepared basic business plans, and
   received tools and equipment, and are carrying out livelihood activities with the support of mentors. Monitoring
   indicates that beneficiaries are utilizing equipment and making value chain connections with bigger businesses and
   creating local jobs. An additional cohort of 1,145 beneficiaries to receive tools has been identified from youth who
   attended initial training, but did not receive equipment, and from youth in the two new districts.
c) Component 3: Project Management, Monitoring and Evaluation: NSIFT’s SERSP project implementation unit is
   adequately managing its coordination and supervision, procurement, financial management, monitoring and
   evaluation, and communications activities, including oversight of its beneficiary feedback and grievance redress
   mechanisms. NSIFT has established a management information system (MIS) that is regularly updated and tracking
   project activities and results. It has also developed a publicly accessible, functional interactive map with information
   on SERSP subprojects across the target jamoats, plus information on top priority investments identified by
   participating jamoats and villages but not yet funded. The baseline survey was completed in September 2021 and the
   project endline survey is planned for 2025.
8.       Gender. The project made significant progress in addressing gender gaps by implementing a range of targeted
activities to enhance women’s participation and empowerment. These included leadership training programs aimed at
building women’s capacity to take on decision-making roles; a mandatory requirement for at least 50% female
representation in community committees to ensure inclusive governance; and the promotion of community-based
approaches that foster an enabling environment for women’s socio -economic inclusion at the local level. Furthermore,
the project focused on expanding and facilitating access to economic opportunities for women and supporting their
engagement in income-generating activities. The project has achieved its gender targets. Women’s participation increased
from 30 to 50 percent after implementation of social mobilization activities despite initial concerns that women would

9.       Procurement and financial management. The project’s financial management and procurement aspects are rated
Satisfactory. Project management, and monitoring and evaluation are Moderately Satisfactory. Based on the latest World
Bank financial management review, the Borrower is in compliance with financial management requirements and the
project does not have overdue financial audit reports.
10.      Environmental and social performance and compliance. Environmental and social (E&S) performance of the
project is Moderately Satisfactory. The environmental risk rating is Moderate, while the social risk rating Substantial, as
the subprojects are being implemented in intrinsically diverse regions. The overall E&S risk rating is Substantial. NSIFT has
prepared and disclosed all required E&S framework instruments, including the E&S Management Framework. NSIFT has
also prepared and disclosed Environmental and Social Management Plans or Environmental and Social Impact
Assessments for each subproject. The high volume of subprojects requires close supervision from E&S risk management
specialists. The social audit results of the community mobilization process indicate communities’ overwhelming
satisfaction with the processes of implementation. Communities felt their opinions were taken into account during the
community mobilization process and that the management of the SERSP subgrants in their community had been equitable
and transparent. The project’s website (https://nsift.tj) includes a beneficiary feedback mechanism module to collect and
track the status of beneficiary feedback and grievances received. NSIFT has also assigned grievance redress mechanism
(GRM) focal points to collect grievance-related data at the regional offices. There is also a national hotline to report
grievances.
11.      Rationale for Additional Financing. In August 2024, the Ministry of Finance (MoF) submitted a request to the
World Bank for an Additional Financing (AF) to expand SERSP social infrastructure and livelihood opportunities. The
proposed AF will extend the coverage of SERSP’s community-driven investment and livelihoods approach to additional
districts and jamoats in the Rogun Hydropower Plant (HPP)1 affected area and in other rural areas with higher shares of
disadvantaged population in the country. This will expand the Government’s successful experience with community-driven
development (CDD), as SERSP and the CASA-1000 Community Support Project (CASA-1000 CSP),2 also implemented by
NSIFT, have demonstrated significant results at the community level.
12.      The AF is designed to complement the Sustainable Financing for Rogun Hydropower Project. First, it will
complement the livelihood restoration activities included in component 3 of the Rogun Project by further enhancing
livelihood opportunities in the project area communities. While the government is providing livelihood restoration support
to project-affected persons (PAPs) who are resettled, livelihood restoration takes time and significant resources.
Stakeholders have requested additional funding and technical support to set up small businesses as well as small local
infrastructure investments. The AF will support Rogun HPP host villages and jamoats and other communities with
additional infrastructure investments, livelihood training and technical support for individuals, groups and small
businesses. Second, the Government can use SERSP AF resources to supplement the limited revenues for Rogun HPP benefit

sharing that will be available in the early years of Rogun construction. The Rogun HPP design provides for the
establishment of a Benefit Sharing Program (BSP) which will be financed through a portion (increasing from 3% to 5%
based on achievement of dam construction milestones) of the revenue from the sale of electricity generated by the HPP.
These revenue streams are limited in the initial years, and are expected to scale up gradually, as all turbines become
operational and the reservoir reaches full supply level. Therefore, the funds flowing into the BSP will be relatively limited
initially and only allow for modest investments in socioeconomic improvements. The proposed AF is intended to provide
complementary funding to the Rogun BSP, allowing for more significant investments over the duration of the project and
tangible improvements in the lives of people living in Rogun-affected communities and other targeted communities in the
country. A third rationale is that the AF project will strengthen the institutional capacity of target jamoats to use a
participatory, community-led approach to plan, budget, and implement local investments and services. This in turn will
help them effectively utilize revenues from future Rogun HPP benefit sharing as well as from other sources. It will also
build a strong sense of ownership on the part of communities for the investments that they select for financing and
demonstrate enhanced responsiveness on the part of government to the prioritized needs of communities.
13.       An additional rationale is the use of the CDD approach for the BSP is one of the top options emerging in the
ongoing process to design the Rogun BSP – although a final decision on the BSP design will not be made until after the AF
is approved. Globally, CDD approaches are used for benefit sharing for large infrastructure projects in multiple countries.
This is in part due to built-in stakeholder engagement and feedback mechanisms that enhance participation, include
women, youth and vulnerable groups, and in turn build local ownership and strengthen risk management. In Tajikistan,
the CASA-1000 Community Support Project, also implemented by NSIFT, has successfully used a CDD approach to provide
infrastructure and other services to communities in the CASA-1000 electricity transmission corridor before and during
construction with 97% of beneficiaries reporting that they consider the CSP an effective response to the local impacts of
CASA-1000. This CDD experience is one of the options being considered in the ongoing BSP design process. The design of
AF is intended to be flexible, to be adapted to the BSP approach that will eventually be approved by the Government.
14.     The AF will also align with priority Government objectives for improving the role of public institutions in economic
development, alleviation of social and gender imbalances, and adaptation to climate change, and WB CPF (135875‐TJ)
objectives for 2019-2024 to enhance people’s knowledge and skills, address regressive gender norms, promote livelihood
opportunities for women in rural settings, enhance public sector transparency and accountability, and ensure affordable,
climate resilient energy. Finally, it will help to establish a platform for other financiers to support, via co-financing or other
means, Rogun BSP and place-based, community-led infrastructure and livelihoods.

15.      The proposed AF will expand SERSP geographic coverage and adjust selected activities to incorporate lessons
learned from SERSP and CASA1000 CSP. Coverage will expand to include Rogun HPP-affected areas and other jamoats

proven to be effective in the rural Tajikistan context at mobilizing rural communities to participate in a bottom up and

     III.      KEY RISKS

41.     The overall risk rating of the AF is assessed as ‘Moderate’ given satisfactory implementation of the Project to
date and of other related operations. Some political and governance risk remains due to the cross-government
commitment to work with NSIFT to extend coverage of community driven development to the Rogun-affected area, to
complement Rogun HPP livelihood restoration and benefit sharing. While senior government officials have expressed
strong support for the AF, cooperation with Rogun HPP stakeholders will be needed to ensure that the AF delivers sought-

Management Group (PMG), Directorate for the Flooding Zone (DFZ), as well as high-level discussions between the
Government of Tajikistan (Government) and World Bank management, plus coordination arrangements that will be built
into the Rogun BSP. Political and governance risk related to AF plans to increase engagement of local governments will
be mitigated by robust community mobilization, participation, information and communication, social audit and GRM,
together with continued decision making by village and jamoat representatives selected by communities. E&S risks under
the AF are expected to remain Substantial due to social risks related to SERSP’s focus on diverse, remote regions, and
geographic focus on Rogun-affected areas. Stakeholder risks related to Rogun-affected and other new target areas will
continue to be mitigated by the built-in stakeholder engagement and feedback mechanisms in SERSP that enhance
community participation, inclusion of women and youth, and local ownership. Macroeconomic risk related to cost
overruns due to inflation or exchange rate risks will continue to be mitigated by maintaining adequate contingencies and
oversight by NSIFT, Government and the Bank. Sector Strategy, Technical Design, Institutional Capacity, Fiduciary risks
are assessed as moderate based on NSIFT’s track record and successful implementation.

      IV. APPRAISAL SUMMARY

    A. Economic Analysis
42.      Economic analysis. Benefits of the investments to (a) provide villages and agglomerations of villages in targeted
areas with improved infrastructure, facilities, and social services, and (b) to support individual and group livelihoods and
micro and small enterprises are expected to be substantial. Benefits from investments in infrastructure come through
eight main channels: (a) improved village water supply and sanitation systems will provide access to safe and more
reliable piped drinking water supplies, which will reduce the time households spend collecting water and contribute to
better health; (b) rehabilitation of on-farm irrigation systems will provide farmers with water when they most need it
raising productivity of agriculture and increasing incomes for farmers; (c) rehabilitating kindergartens will lead to higher
educational attainment and therefore lifetime incomes of children attending the schools and will lead to increased
incomes for their mothers who will be able to engage in income-earning activities during the time they would otherwise
be occupied with childcare; (d) refurbishing local health clinics will contribute to reduced maternal and infant mortality
and increased productivity of workers; (e) rehabilitation of rural roads will reduce travel times and vehicle operating costs
and will bring significant benefits of improved connectivity, such as linking agricultural producing areas with population
centers; (f) investments in energy efficiency technologies in construction and rehabilitation of infrastructure and facilities
can reduce energy use by 15–20 percent and investments in renewable energy can pay for themselves in six to nine years;
(g) using resilient construction techniques will reduce loss of life, injuries, and property damage due to natural hazards;
and (h) strengthening capacity of communities, local leadership forums, and local governments to select, manage, and
maintain projects will help ensure that they can execute similar activities after the project closes. As the investments to
be supported under the project will not be known until after the communities select them, the economic analysis is based
on estimates of net benefits from other projects investing in similar types of infrastructure, facilities, and livelihood
support. Such economic analyses show economic internal rates of return to range from a low of 17 percent for
rehabilitating irrigation systems to 160 percent for improving health services.
43.     Support for individual and group livelihoods generates significant returns in the form of higher lifetime incomes
of the people benefiting from the support, according to results of similar programs in other countries. For example, an
impact evaluation of Uganda’s Youth Opportunity Program (P002952), which supports livelihood groups, shows that
people benefiting from project interventions had 38 percent higher incomes than those that did not.3
44.      Investments in training and business development services for people working in micro and small businesses
are expected to be substantial. Training and business development services targeting micro and small business owners
lead to increases in lifetime income of beneficiaries of between of between 10–40 percent, according to an impact
evaluation of a project in Uganda.4 Another impact evaluation showed that such positive impacts on income persist over
time. 5 An impact evaluation of a training program as part of the Ethiopia Women’s Entrepreneurship Development
Project (P122764) carried out by the World Bank showed that profits of the micro and small enterprises led by the
entrepreneurs who completed business and personal initiative training were 30 percent higher than those of
entrepreneurs who did not. 6

  B. Technical
46.      The technical dimensions of CDD operations in Tajikistan, including the SERSP parent project and the CASA1000
CSP project have performed well. Mobilization has been effective based on social audits, as well as field visits. The quality
of infrastructure subprojects has been repeatedly reviewed by independent consultant engineers who have in some cases
requested improvements but overall have found quality to be good and improving over the parent project lifetime. New
livelihood activities have been designed based on lessons learned from the parent project, as well as consultations with
representatives from multiple livelihoods and rural support projects implemented in rural Tajikistan.
47.      The proposed AF project will support the nation's Nationally Determined Contribution goals and national
climate action strategies and plans and is aligned with the Paris Agreement. Among key mitigation activities identified
in the 2021 NDC is the use of renewable energy sources and the promotion of energy efficiency in utilities such as energy

the same time, investments will account for regional climate hazards and incorporate necessary adaptation and resilience
measures into the design. To keep any climate change-related material impacts to an acceptable level, the project includes
several risk reduction measures, such as: i) the induction of climate ambassadors to spread awareness about climate risks
and responses; ii) capacity building for local authorities to develop local development plans that address climate risks,
informing community investments based on climate risks, as outlined in Annex 2. The project has been screened for climate
change and disaster impacts and specific potential resilience-enhancing measures have been identified and properly reflected

Operation Abstract: Summary Description of Proposed Operation
Operation Abstract (Approved as part of Approval Package on 16-Jun-2020)
The proposed project is part of the Risk Mitigation Regime (RMR) included in the upcoming World Bank CPF for the
Republic of Tajikistan for the period FY19-23. The RMR allocation—during the IDA-18 cycle—is in the amount of US$87
million to finance policy reforms and interventions for preventive support aimed at addressing FCV risks that have
constrained development progress in Tajikistan.


The proposed project will contribute to RMR objectives by strengthening resilience at the individual, community and
local government levels to mitigate fragility risks. At the individual level, the project will provide psychosocial support,
soft skills (including critical thinking skills), and support for youth self-employment and micro-entrepreneurship. At the
community level, proposed interventions include the provision of locally-identified infrastructure and the
empowerment of youth to lead development initiatives. At the local government level, these interventions include
strengthening the capacity of jamoat administrations and community-level institutions, including mahalla committees,
to engage with citizens, and to deliver services efficiently, fairly and in response to community’s needs. Geographically,
the proposed project will target Khatlon and Gorno Badakhshan Autonomous Oblast (GBA) regions, the regions with
the highest levels of absolute and relative poverty measures, respectively, and which face fragility risks due to the
proximity to unstable parts of Afghanistan, large youth populations, disparities in service delivery outcomes, and
legacies of violent conflict.
Proposed Operation Abstract
An Additional Financing (AF) in the amount of US$25 mln. will expand the coverage of SERSP’s community-driven
investment and livelihoods approach to additional districts and jamoats in the Rogun Hydropower Plant (HPP) affected
area and in jamoats with higher shares of disadvantaged population in other parts of the country. In the Rogun-
affected area, the AF will cover jamoats in the three districts and two cities (Faizobad, Nurobad, and Danghara districts,
Rogun and Tursunzoda cities) that include resettlement villages under the second phase of resettlement in the Rogun
HPP – 5 jamoats and Darband small town with 8 resettlement villages and an additional 27 jamoats across the five
districts. In addition to the Rogun affected area, two additional districts will be selected from regions of the country
(Khatlon and GBAO) that have lower income per capita, and selection of target districts will be based on criteria such as
population, investments per capita and unemployment.
