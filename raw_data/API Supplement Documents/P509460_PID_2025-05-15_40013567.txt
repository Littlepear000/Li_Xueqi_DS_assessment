B. Introduction and Context

Country Context
 1.    Thailand has made remarkable progress in social and economic development, transitioning from low-income to upper-
 middle-income status in less than a generation. From 1960-1996, the Thai economy grew at an average annual rate of 7.5
 percent before the Asian Financial Crisis, and at 5 percent between 1999 and 2005. This growth generated millions of jobs,
 reducing poverty from 50 percent in the 1990s to 6.8 percent in 2020. Despite past success, the high growth prospects of
 Thailand’s export-led model have significantly diminished since the Asian Financial Crisis of 1997. Private investment fell from
 over 40 percent in 1997 to 16.9 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 2019. But macroeconomic fundamentals remain
 conservative. The public debt-to-GDP remained below 45 percent on average for 15 years (2004-2019) and has increased to
 above 50 percent since the COVID-19 pandemic.
 2.     The World Bank estimates that the Thai economy will grow by 1.6 percent in 2025, slower than 2024 (2.5 percent) as
 global trade policy shifts and heightened uncertainty weigh on exports and investment. Tourism may also soften amid a global
 slowdown. Private consumption will ease with declining earnings and deleveraging, although a fiscal stimulus—especially the
 Digital Wallet and targeted business support—may cushion the impact. Headline inflation turned negative for the first time in
 more than a year, falling to -0.22 percent (year-on-year) in early 2025, the lowest among ASEAN countries. Poverty declined to
 7.1 percent in 2024.
 3.    Thailand's tourism sector is an economic driver contributing about 20 percent of GDP. Thailand’s beaches and islands
 are among the most sought-after travel destinations in the world. More than 12.9 million tourists visited Thailand during the first
 six months of 2023, spending more than THB 262 billion (US$ 7.7 billion), of which 75 percent (THB 196 billion) was spent in the
 Southern Region of Thailand. Songkhla and Krabi provinces are among the top five tourist destinations in the South. The tourism
 industry also contributes to more than 11 percent (about 4.3 million jobs) of total employment in Thailand.
 4.    Conserving its rich natural resources and key biodiversity assets remain the foundation of Thailand’s tourist attractions.
 In addition to the country’s most celebrated species, such as the Indochinese tiger and the Asian elephant, the Southern region

  boasts a wide array of both terrestrial and marine flagship species, such as the Irrawaddy dolphin. Rapid economic development

  5.      Thailand is susceptible to many natural hazards, including floods, tsunamis, storms, droughts, landslides, and forest
  fires. Droughts, storms, and floods present the greatest risks, posing a threat to sustained economic growth. Their frequency has
  increased over time, imposing a toll on environmental and social inclusion. Resilient infrastructure and all-season accessibility
  will be crucial in ensuring that economic activities and the well-being of the local population.
 Sectoral and Institutional Context
  6.      Thailand’s Transport Development Strategy 2018-2037 serves as the country’s comprehensive plan for the transport
  sector, outlining key objectives and priorities. There are three guiding pillars: (i) green and safe transport, (ii) transport efficiency,
  and (iii) inclusive transport. The first phase of the strategy (2018-2022) focuses on addressing critical transport issues, missing
  links, and bottlenecks for the main transport corridors as well as for lower income groups. With this strategy, Thailand continues
  to invest in its transport infrastructure to improve accessibility and promote local economies. Thailand’s transport sector
  investment plan for FY23 amounts to THB 311.5 billion (US$ 9 billion), or about 1.6 percent of GDP. The Department of Highways,
  the Department of Rural Roads (DRR), the Department of Airports (DOA), and the State Railway of Thailand (SRT) are among the
  key implementing agencies to carry out the investment plan.
  7.     The DRR’s development strategy (2017-2026) covers the far-end of transport network connectivity and transport
  efficiency, including last-mile connections and missing links. This includes the proposed bridges for Lanta Islands and Songkhla
  Lake to resolve transport bottlenecks, improve resilient accessibility, and support tourism development of the Southern region.
  The Songkhla lake bridge (7 kms) aims to address a missing transport link around Songkhla lake and to shorten the travel distance
  from 80 kilometers to 8 kilometers – benefiting a population of approximately 308,000 on both sides of the lake. The Lanta
  Islands bridge will connect the islands to Krabi province’s mainland via a 2-kilometer bridge over the sea. While last-mile
  connectivity is currently served by ferry operation, the proposed bridge will provide improved transport accessibility for the local
  population, tourism activities, waste management, and utilities for the islands.
  8.     In Songkhla Lake, a robust conservation program that combines enforcement (of effective dolphin protection measures
  and appropriate fishing regulations), incentives (through the provision of livelihood alternatives to fishing communities
  around Thale Luang), and research (to better understand the population and its ecology) is urgently needed to address the
  declining trend observed in the Irrawaddy dolphin population over recent decades. Existing information and research on the
  dolphins is limited and the risk of human-induced dolphin mortalities is high. The World Bank’s involvement in the project can
  be catalytic in terms of convening key stakeholders and in mobilizing global expertise to strengthen conservation measures for
  the Irrawaddy dolphin in the Thale Luang.
  9.    The proposed bridge construction provides an opportunity to also build commitment for a robust conservation
  program for the Irrawaddy dolphin in Thale Luang. An Expert Roundtable consultation1 confirmed the momentum and interest
  by public and non-government stakeholders to drive conservation efforts of the Irrawaddy dolphin in Thale Luang and confirmed
  that construction impacts can be adequately managed. Recommendations by the Roundtable for long-term dolphin
  conservation included better understanding mortality and conservation threats, implementing mitigation measures to counter
  and ringfence potential impacts from bridge construction (based on an acoustic and visual survey conducted for the project’s
  Environmental Impact Assessment, the dolphins appear to be in an area around 8 kilometers north of the bridge alignment), and
  eliminating bycatch and other fishing-gear related threats.
  10. The Government has prepared a long-term strategy in 2023 for the conservation of Irrawaddy dolphins in Thale Luang..

    D. Concept Description
     14. The proposed project has three components:
    Component 1 – Transport Infrastructure Connectivity to Lanta Island and across Songkhla Lake (Total US$ 196.43 million; IBRD
    US$ 137.50 million and Royal Thai Government (RTG) US$ 58.93 million). This component has two sub-components:
    15. Sub-Component 1.1: Lanta Island Bridge. This sub-component supports the construction of a new 2-kilometer bridge
    connecting Lanta Island (consisting of Lanta Noi Island and Lanta Yai Island) with Krabi province’s mainland, including
    environmental and social mitigation measures. The bridge will also serve as the infrastructure to carry utilities, such as waste,
    electricity, water, and fiber optic internet, and address the demand for reliable and consistent emergency transportation to
    mainland medical services which cannot be met by the ferry service during off-hours and during low tide or hazardous wind

conditions. The bridge’s climate resilient design will accommodate high tides tsunami waves similar to the tsunami that struck
the Koh Lanta coast in 2004 (approximately 4 meters), as well as using construction materials resilient to seawater corrosion.
16. Sub-component 1.2: Songkhla Lake bridge. This sub-component includes the construction of a new 7-kilometer bridge
across Songkhla Lake, including environmental and social mitigation measures. The two-lane bridge is expected to reduce the
travel time and transportation cost for the 308,000 local beneficiaries who live on both sides of the lake. The bridge would save
up to 2 hours (or 80 kilometers) of travel by road around the lake, facilitating trade and reduce logistics costs between two sides
of the lake. In addition, the bridge will establish an emergency evacuation route in case of natural disasters, especially along the
east shoreline (Krasae Sin District and Sathing Phra District of Songkhla Province) to enable rapid evacuation to Phatthalung
Province. It will also improve access to healthcare and universities in Songkhla City for the local population. The bridge is designed
to be resilient to climate events, such as strong winds, with higher design speeds (161km/h) than those recorded during ‘Typhoon
Gay’ (about 120km/h).
17. Under Sub-component 1.2, key activities to mitigate construction and operation impacts on the Irrawaddy dolphin
population include: (i) establishing real-time visual and acoustic detection of dolphins to provide early warning of dolphin
presence near the construction site, (ii) establishing a work stoppage protocol, which includes an observation zone and shut
down zone, based on international best practices to protect dolphins present near construction activities, (iii) monitoring water
quality and other key parameters during bridge construction, and (iv) implementing all in-water bridge pier construction activities
(piling, concreting) inside a silt curtain fully enclosing the work site from the surrounding water column to minimize turbidity and
pollution.
Component 2: Irrawaddy Dolphin Conservation and Ecosystem Management in Thale Luang (Total US$ 12.17 million, PROBLUE
Trust Fund US$ 4 million, RTG US$ 8.17 million). This component is designed to support the DCP and includes activities to
facilitate the implementation of the Thale Luang Non-Hunting Area (NHA) management plan:
18. Sub-component 2.1: Strengthening the management of the Thale Luang NHA for dolphin protection. This sub-
component will address the most significant threat to Irrawaddy dolphins, namely their entanglement in fishing gear. This will
be done by supporting the implementation of key elements of the new NHA management plan – new core zone, buffer zone,
and transition zone. Successful implementation of this new plan will require a combination of community communications and
outreach, and improved NHA management (including clear boundary demarcation and enhanced enforcement and patrolling
practices, communication and outreach campaigns with fishers, and capacity building of DNP staff).
19. Sub-component 2.2: Supporting community conservation of Irrawaddy dolphins and improving access to alternative
livelihoods for fishing communities. This sub-component creates incentives for fishing communities around Thale Luang to avoid
fishing practices and gear that pose threats to the dolphins, by facilitating access to livelihoods that are compatible with
conservation, enhancing incomes, and reducing dependence on already depleted lake fisheries. Climate resilience will be built
at the foundation of alternative livelihoods, thus reducing community vulnerability to climate change and climatic shocks. Grant
support will be provided to fishing communities following an "open menu" approach - any type of sub-project would be eligible
for funding as long as it falls outside a negative list of activities and items that cannot be implemented or used, as they would
jeopardize the achievement of project objectives or have adverse social or environmental impacts. A dedicated Grant Manual
will be prepared to define implementation details of the sub-component.
20. Sub-component 2.3: Improving knowledge about Irrawaddy dolphins in Thale Luang. This sub-component, aims to
conduct research and periodic monitoring and surveying to help stakeholders improve their understanding of the Irrawaddy
dolphin population in Thale Luang. The research will focus on the different threats to the dolphin population and key ecosystem
parameters with long-term implications for the conservation of the sub-species, such as siltation and water quality and the
effects of climate change on those. This will inform adaptive conservation and strengthen the implementation of the Dolphin
Conservation Plan, enabling prompt corrective action when required. It will also facilitate communication and outreach.
Component 3: Institutional Development and Project Management (Total: US$5.90 million by RTG).

 Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts




 22. The overall risk classification is High, driven by the High Environmental risk rating. Under Component 1, most
 environmental risks and impacts are common to greenfield road bridge construction in a modified rural environment of
 wet tropics and include: clearing of vegetation, and small areas of mangrove and forest; benthic habitat disruption; land
 occupation by disposed dredging material; erosion; pollution (air, noise, water (including silt dispersion), waste); aquatic
 noise and vibration; visual impacts; accidents; and threats to CR species like the Irrawaddy dolphin in Songkhla Lake.
 Based on the government approved EIAs, individual environmental risks are largely low to moderate and are readily
 avoided or mitigated by the selection of bridge alignment, construction methods, and standard measures prescribed in
 the environmental and social management plans (ESMPs). There is a risk that the potential construction impacts, if not
 carefully mitigated, can affect the habitat of the Irrawaddy dolphins. This includes potential impacts of underwater
 construction (and operation), noise on the dolphins’ ability to echolocate, feed and communicate. Under Component 2,
 there is a risk that misinformed efforts at saving the Irrawaddy dolphin will fail to create conditions for arresting the
 decline. There is also a risk that dolphin conservation measures will reduce access to local fishing livelihoods leading to
 resentment of the dolphins by fishing communities. The high magnitude of dolphin related risk arises primarily from the
 small size of the sub-population (14-20 as per last survey) providing a minimal safety margin because if man-made
 mortalities continue, the sub-population may become non-viable. Cumulative impacts on a broad range of valued
 environmental and social components are expected to be High with respect to the dolphins at Songkhla and the marine
 environment at Lanta.
 23. The social risk rating is Substantial largely due to access restrictions and possible displacement of fishers from Component
 1 and 2 activities in the Songkhla bridge. For Component 1, a physical survey conducted along the bridge right-of-way (ROW)
 and construction safety zone for the Songkhla project in July 2023 revealed three sections of fish-aggregating devices (FADs)
 owned by about 8-10 fishers. Additional 40-50 fishers were found to be using nets to catch fish. For Component 2, the NHA
 management plan might cause economic displacement; namely, (i) to fishers owning FADs who are affected by no-fishing
 regulations inside the proposed new core zone; and (ii) fishers who are affected by gillnet restrictions inside the buffer and

transition zones. A rapid assessment of fishing communities in Thale Luang conducted from July to September 2023 suggests
use of gillnets, regardless of mesh size, by 40-76% of fishers surveyed in 12 of 15 survey locations, providing an estimate of
potentially affected fishers.
24. Social due diligence conducted during project preparation showed adequacy of the land donation processes to national
laws and ESS5 requirements. On the Lanta bridge, the closure of ferry operations and the impact on the ferry operator and 100
to 150 workers would be mitigated through project-related transitional support (e.g., commissioning of ferry services for
materials sourcing during construction, offering bridge construction as an alternative employment to ferry workers). The risks
associated with labor and working conditions are considered low. Community health and safety risks are deemed low to
moderate as risks associated with pollution are mitigatable while sexual exploitation and abuse/sexual harassment (SEA/SH)
risks are considered low using the Bank’s SEA/SH screening tool.
25. A detailed Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) was prepared and approved for the two bridges. The EIA has been
reviewed by the World Bank and supplemented with additional studies, to ensure compliance with the Environmental and Social
Framework (ESF). These studies, management plans and frameworks form the Supplemental Lenders Information Packages
(SLIP) for both bridges. The SLIPs include (i) extended Non-Technical Summaries (NTS) of the EIAs; (ii) Critical Habitat
Assessments; (iii) Underwater Noise Assessment; (iv) Dolphin Conservation Plan; (v) Cumulative Impact Assessments; (vi)
Environmental and Social Management Plans (ESMPs); (vii) Labor Management Procedures (LMP); (viii) Resettlement
Framework (RF); (ix) Process Framework; and (x) Stakeholder Engagement Plans (SEPs). The EIAs were consulted upon through
an extensive stakeholder engagement process during EIAs preparation. The SLIPs have been consulted and disclosed during
project preparation consistent with the requirements of the relevant environmental and social standards (ESSs). The EIAs and
the SLIP documents together comprise the compliance documents for the project. The final package of all SLIP documents have
been disclosed on DRR’s and the World Bank’s websites in May 2024 and will be re-disclosed before project appraisal.
