  I. BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING


         A. Country Context
         1.        Burundi is a low-income country (Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita of US$307 in 20191)
         with an estimated 62 percent of its 12.9 million residents living below the international poverty line as
         of 2022 (World Bank Macro Poverty Outlook, 2024). Burundi is the third most densely populated country
         in Sub-Saharan Africa, with an estimated 463 inhabitants per square kilometer in 2022. As a result of high
         fertility rates (5.1 children per woman in 2021), its population is expected to double by 2050. A growing
         and young population can be a dynamic force, but the current economy is characterized by limited
         economic opportunities, high underemployment, and limited wage jobs. Nearly 90 percent of non-
         agricultural workers are employed in the informal sector. In recent years, GDP has grown more slowly
         than the population, constrained by structural challenges such as limited public investment, low capital
         accumulation, low productivity in agriculture and across the economy, and limited economic
         diversification. Private sector development is hampered by a challenging business environment for private
         sector activities, limited infrastructure, and limited access to finance. Despite these challenges, Burundi’s
         economy is improving gradually, with a GDP growth projected at 3.8 percent in 2024, and 4.4 percent in
         2025.

         2.       This Project Paper describes a proposed Additional Financing (AF) for the Eastern Africa
         Regional Statistics Program-for-Results (PforR, P176371). The parent program, a PforR operation with
         Investment Project Financing (IPF) components, includes Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania, as PforR
         components. The East African Community (EAC) and the African Union Commission (AUC) are included as
         standard IPF components. The proposed AF will scale up to include Burundi as an Investment Project
         Financing (IPF) component. The original operation became fully effective in January 2023.2 The latest
         Implementation Support Mission (ISM), held in March 2024, confirmed solid progress on achieving of
         Disbursement Linked Indicators (DLIs) and key project activities, including the recruitment of international
         verification agencies. While implementation delays persist, especially in the AUC component, the mission
         found strong progress toward achievement of the PDO and a large pipeline of Disbursement-Linked
         Results to be achieved in the coming quarters.

         Sector Context

         participating in a regional program with aspirational peers. Statistical performance varies considerably

         4.       Data dissemination and use in Burundi is limited. A lack of an effective producer-user dialogue
         leads to discrepancies between data supply and demand. In addition, weak analytical capability and
         limited micro-data dissemination have constrained the use of existing economic and social data. The
         Institut National de la Statistique du Burundi (INSBU) holds a significant amount of macro- and micro-data
         that the Government of Burundi (GoB) does not sufficiently use during preparation of policy reforms and
         development strategies. Micro-data are not systematically shared with ministries and researchers, and
         there is a limited understanding of how these data can be used to inform alternative policy scenarios.
         More than 50 percent of micro-data for surveys conducted by INSBU are unavailable on their website.

         5.       The overall quality of data produced needs improvement. While there is significant amount of
         statistical production at the INSBU, the quality of many of them needs to be improved. As demands rise
         for a greater variety of data, there are risks of further quality deterioration from overloading the collection
         and operations as the existing staff is stretched thin. Some quality issues are more data-specific: for
         instance, welfare data series are not frequently produced. The national accounts base year is nine years
         old, while the international standard requires rebasing once every five years. It also needs to be upgraded
         to the 2008 system of national accounts (SNA2008). Enterprise/business registries are incomplete
         because they do not cover the informal sector and there is no sample frame for firm surveys due to the
         lack of a complete (formal and informal) enterprise census. The last population census took place in 2008,

         Relevance to Higher Level Objectives

         6.       The project objective is aligned with the World Bank’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF)
         (FY19–23) for Burundi, report No. 122878-BI, extended by one year to FY24 (Performance and Learning
         Review, dated April 27, 2023. report No. 181794-B), particularly with Focus Area II on Strengthening
         Foundations for Economic and Social Resilience. The CPF states that “Greater citizen engagement will be
         promoted where possible in WBG interventions to ensure social inclusion through broader participation in
         development programs, production of more timely and reliable statistics through more frequent household
         surveys and publication of their results”. The Burundi Program Learning Review emphasizes the need to
         increase regional program synergies on universal energy access, health emergency preparedness and
         resilience, and statistics. The project is aligned with the knowledge compact of the World Bank Evolution
         roadmap which acknowledge that countries cannot measure the impact of their policies without data.

         7.       The AF operation is consistent with Burundi’s Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) . The
         2021 NDC, under section 7, acknowledges the importance of monitoring and evaluation. Under the
         Environment Ministry's oversight, the NDC's sectoral and technical services ensure the effective
         monitoring and evaluation of climate actions and policies. This involves tracking the implementation of
         activities and various NDC indicators. In addition, a capacity-building plan targeting related sectors is
         integrated into the NDC implementation plan with the costs included in the action plan's priority
         expenses. The AF directly supports the INSBU (the central entity in charge of monitoring the NDC) and its
         sectoral entities. Burundi does not yet have a Country Climate and Development Report (CCDR).

         C. Summary of the Program’s implementation progress

         8.       The last Implementation Status and Results Report (ISR), dated May 6, 2024, rated the parent
         operation's Program Development Objective (PDO) and Implementation Progress as "moderately
         satisfactory." This is due to delays in the approval of harmonization guidelines, commitment to statistical
         dimensions, and the establishment of the Project Implementation Unit (PIU) at the AUC. However,
         significant progress has been made within last six months with the disbursement rate doubling and the
         fiscal year disbursement rate increasing notably. Both International Verification Agencies have been
         recruited, DLI submission and verification is progressing on a rolling basis, and disbursement rates are
         expected to rise further.

         9.       For Kenya, despite a funding shortfall, implementation has improved with a request for an
         advance under the Program for Results (PforR), three Disbursement-Linked Results (DLRs) have been
         achieved as another seven are due for verification. Tanzania experienced delays due to the 2022 Census
         but has updated its work plan accordingly. Compliance with essential items in the Program Action Plan is
         being monitored, with DLR verifications scheduled for July 2024. Rwanda is on track with its Year 2 results,
         and despite some delays in the Program Action Plan, these have not impacted overall progress. The EAC
         component has faced procurement challenges due to the unexpected departure of a specialist, but a new
         officer is expected soon, and temporary support is in place. The AUC component has faced delays due to
         regulatory conflicts between AUC and World Bank procurement rules; however, this has been resolved,

         D. Rationale for the Additional Financing

         10.      Burundi is a dedicated supporter of the regional approach to statistical harmonization, as
         evidenced by its active participation in the EAC Sectoral Council on Statistics. Burundi’s NSDS-3
         specifically aims to harmonize core economic and social statistics with EAC harmonization guidelines and
         with the continent-wide Strategy for the Harmonization of Statistics in Africa (SHASA). As a member state
         of the EAC, Burundi is committed to aligning its statistical practices with regional standards. However,
         there has been substantial underinvestment in official statistical data systems in Burundi and the National
         Statistical System (NSS), coordinated by INSBU, requires substantial technical support to bridge the gap
         and catch up with other countries in the region. This proposed AF aims to add Burundi as an IPF
         component to the parent regional project to fill critical data gaps and strengthen the National Statistical
         Office (NSO)’s capacity to produce, disseminate and use regionally harmonized statistics. Like the other
         partner countries, technical support and assistance for the application of regional harmonization
         guidelines will be provided by EAC’s Department of Statistics and AUC’s STATAFRIC, both of which are
         included as IPF components in the parent program.

  II. INTRODUCTION


         11.    This Project Paper seeks the approval of the Executive Directors to provide an AF (P181404) in
         the amount of US$20 million to the Eastern Africa Regional Statistics Program-for-Results. The AF is to
         be implemented by the national INSBU.

         12.      The AF, as an IPF component, will support the implementation of the three strategic pillars of
         the NSDS-3. Like the three countries in the parent project, Burundi has developed and adopted its third
         NSDS (2022-2026). It aims to “provide the Government and various users with quality statistics relating to
         all areas of national life.” The NSDS-3 is based on the following three strategic pillars: (i) strengthening the
         regulatory framework and governance of the National Statistical System (SSN); (ii) Improving the coverage
         and quality of statistical production; and (iii) Improving the human, material, and financial resources of
         the SSN. Like Kenya, Tanzania, and Rwanda, Burundi is an EAC member state whose NSDS aims to
         harmonize national statistics according to regional EAC guidelines. The AF for Burundi is planned to fund
         core activities from 2024 to 2027 as an IPF Component. There will be no changes to the boundary of
         broader PforR government programs in Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania.

         13.      Project Beneficiaries. The AF is expected to stregthen Burundi’s SSN, coordinated by INSBU.
         Direct beneficiaries include data users in government, academia, and civil society. The public good nature
         of the project and the expectation that it will support evidence-based policy making and strengthen
         accountability implies that the entire population will benefit from having a stronger statistical system as
         a result of the project.

  III. PROPOSED CHANGES

         A.      Summary of Proposed Changes to the PDO and the Program.
         14.      The addition of Burundi to the regional program will warrant a change in the PDO. The original
         PDO is to strengthen the regional harmonization, dissemination, and use of core economic and social
         statistics for Kenya, Rwanda, and Tanzania. The new PDO is to strengthen the regional harmonization,
         dissemination, and use of core economic and social statistics in participating countries. This change will
         reflect the inclusion of Burundi.
         B.      PDO-Level Indicators
         15.     The achievement of the PDO-level results will remain the same and will be measured by three
         PDO-level indicators. The indicators cover harmonization, availability, and use of statistics: (i)
         Harmonization: Number of published statistical dimensions that are harmonized according to the
         applicable regional technical guidelines by participating countries; (ii) Availability: Average score for the
         customized Statistical Performance Index (SPI) across participating countries; and (iii) Use: Number of
         strategy and policy documents utilizing or referring to data or statistics produced by the operation across
         participating countries. The original program duration will remain the same with a closing date in 2027.
         The overall financing increases by US$20 million with the addition of the Burundi IPF component.

      18.       Harmonized Consumer Price Index (CPI) with EAC. This activity will support the establishment of
      a CPI harmonized with the EAC criteria. It aims to support INSBU in producing high-quality monthly CPI data.

  IV. APPRAISAL SUMMARY

  A. Technical, Economic and Financial Analysis

      34.       Technical Soundness. To ensure the project is technically sound, activities are following
      internationally recognized best practices, principles, regional harmonization standards, and compliance
      frameworks. Specifically, activities are guided by the World Bank’s Statistical Performance Indicators (SPIs)
      and Data-for-Policy (D4P) scores. Our express goals are an increase in SPI and D4P scores. The project
      guarantees the technical quality of statistics and their comparability via compliance with international best
      practices, reinforced by regional harmonization standards and compliance frameworks in line with the
      SHaSA2 guidelines.

      35.      Strategic Relevance. The strategic relevance remains valid and will not change. The program aims
      to enhance the technical and institutional capabilities of regional and national statistical systems. It also
      promotes the production, harmonization, and timely distribution of national-level statistics.

      36.       The Burundi AF uses the IPF instrument, allowing the World Bank to provide hands-on technical
      support to INSBU to address certain capacity constraints. In addition, Burundi has no experience with the
      PforR instrument and could face capacity constraints when implementing a PforR.

      37.         The economic justification for the Burundi AF is the same as the parent project. Public investment
      in statistics is vital in successful regional integration, planning, policy-making, and monitoring of local and
      national policies. It provides an array of development, social, and economic statistics that the private sector
      may not be incentivized to pursue. If left to the private market, there could be a significant underinvestment
      in the acquisition of these statistics, hence missing out on the benefits a thorough dataset would provide.
      Moreover, these statistics are a public good. Once collated, different regional organizations, governments,
      citizens, and businesses can access and utilize them to facilitate evidence-based decisions. Consequently,
      public investment in statistics carries positive externalities. Burundi has a a significant parallel exchange rate
      premium, which averaged 100 percent in May 2024. Reforms that would eventually lead to exchange rate
      liberalization is part ofthe IMF’s Extended Credit Facility (ECF), which was approved by the IMF’s Board in
      July 2023. While important reforms have been done, the exchange rate unification has not been achieved
      to date, and no staff-level agreement was reached at the conclusion of the first IMF review in January 2024.
      The IMF and the World Bank continue to engage with the country’s authorities on a path to complete all
      the reforms including in the foreign exchange market. For the AF, the bulk of activities is expected to be
      financed by local expenditures (91 percent). As a result, an exchange rate unification or a significant
      depreciation would increase the cost-effectiveness of the AF. Finally, although the benefits of the project
      are difficult to quantify, the project's net returns in terms of informed policy making are likely to be high
      even taking into account the dual foreign exchange rate market.

  B. Fiduciary

      38.        The World Bank’s default of using the country’s PFM system or some aspects of the system,
      provided they are reliable, applies to the current project. Hence, the fiduciary management of the project
      will be under the responsibility of INSBU. INSBU's financial management system was assessed to check that:
      (i) it has adequate financial management arrangements to ensure that project funds will be used for
      purposes intended in an efficient and economical way; (ii) project financial reports will be prepared in an
      accurate, reliable, and timely manner; and (iii) the entity’s assets will be safeguarded. The assessment was
      done in accordance with the World Bank Directive: Financial Management Manual for World Bank IPF
      Operations issued on September 7, 2021.

      39.        For the Burundi AF, INSBU will lead the implementation of the proposed AF. INSBU is a public
      establishment of an administrative nature endowed with asset and financial autonomy under the Ministry
      of Finance, decree n. 100/153 dated November 16, 2022. INSBU has a financial team comprised of a director
      of administration and finance, a chief accountant, two accountants and a cashier. The team has experience
      in accounting and financial management. However, this shall be the first World Bank -financed project
      implemented by INSBU. INSBU manual details the procedures for preparation of annual budgets and regular
      financial reports, internal controls, and annual external audit processes. The internal audit functions are
      carried out by the monitoring and evaluation service team (two statisticians and one economist). The
      external auditors (Commissaire Aux Comptes) are appointed by the President of the Republic for a three-
      year mandate. Internal and external auditors’ available reports have noticed inconsistencies in the review
      of the financial data. Further, (i) audit reports are not publicly available on the website; (ii) the annual
      financial reports are not completed on a regular basis (the data/documents available are 18-24 months old);
      and (iii) no quarterly interim reports are produced. The accounting software in place is basic and financial
      reports extracted from it are reprocessed in Excel before finalization. INSBU has been planning to replace
      and upgrade the accounting software to a more efficient one.

      40.        The overall financial management risk is Substantial. The rating is driven by: (i) potential delays
      in census and surveys (Component 2 representing 52.5 percent of the project) that may require budget
      adjustment due to increasing costs of inputs; (ii) the non-performing accounting software; (iii) the non-
      consistency in the availability of financial reports; and (iv) the lack of PIU experience in monitoring World
      Bank supported projects. To mitigate these risks, (i) the internal audit functions shall be completed by
      INSBU’s current monitoring and evaluation service team after training and support from experienced PIUs
      on the portfolio; (ii) INSBU financial team will be paired with experienced teams on the portfolio for coaching
      and monitoring purposes; (iii) INSBU shall acquire a new accounting software to ensure generation of
      reliable financial statements and an optimal financial management budgeting; (iv) external audit shall be
      completed by an external auditor meeting international standards, based on terms of reference acceptable
      to the World Bank; (v) INSBU procedures manual shall be updated prior to any disbursement to include all
      fiduciary aspects of World Bank-financed projects; and (vi) budget execution reports shall be included in the
      Project's quarterly and annual financial statements. The World Bank will continue to support the PIU in the
      various FM aspects. In addition, the Bank will conduct at least two onsite implementation support visits
      every year to ensure that robust FM systems are maintained for the grant throughout the life of the Project.

      51.        Climate. The parent project identified and mitigated the impact of climate change. Potential
      impacts of climate change on activities related to construction, resource utilization, health, and safety of

      Burundi is one of the countries in the world most vulnerable to climate change. Burundi emits less than 0.02
      percent of the world's greenhouse gases (GHGs) that contribute to global warming and yet the country is
      ranked at the bottom of the most climate ready country and near the top of the most vulnerable to climate

      subsistence farmers, and is highly vulnerable to climate shocks. From 1979 to 2018, the mean temperature

      makes the country vulnerable to extreme precipitation, flood risk, and landslides. A World Bank analysis
      estimated that Burundi loses almost 38 million tonnes of soil per year, at a cost of US$120 million in 2014,
      or 3.9 percent of GDP. From 2017 to 2020 alone, more than 33,000 ha—1.2 percent of Burundi’s land area—
      experienced acute degradation.7 Since the start of 2024, over 200,000 people have been affected, and over
      100,000 displaced, by unusually heavy rains amplified by climate change and the El Nino phenomenon,
      which have caused severe floods and landslides in several parts of the country. Future rainfall projections
      indicate an overall pattern of wetting in the north, with associated risks of floods and landslides, and drying
      in the south of the country.
      52.        Extreme temperatures can put additional pressure on data centers and other infrastructure
      installations in Burundi. The impact of rising temperatures, an increased frequency of droughts, storms and
      flooding on the statistical system is expected to be limited in scope and can be managed through the
      implementation of environmental management plans. Technology choices for infrastructure will, when
      possible, take climate resilience into account and data centers will include backup and data loss
      contingencies in their design.
      53.       Paris Alignment.        The operation is aligned with the goals of the Paris Agreement on both
      mitigation and adaptation.
      54.        Assessment and reduction of mitigation risks. Burundi's GHG emissions are disproportionately
      small relative to its per capita income. The country accounted for less than 0.02 percent of global GHG
      emissions in 2018, producing only 8.35 million tons of CO2 equivalent (CO2e). Its emissions per person were
      less than a quarter of the sub-Saharan African average. Over 75 percent of its emissions originate from
      agriculture, land use, and forestry. The AF does not significantly contribute to GHG emissions. Nevertheless,
      the AF in its implementation will prioritize environmentally friendly practices from low carbon alternatives.
      This will include the purchase of energy-efficient equipment for data collection, servers, and the
      construction of an energy efficient building.
      55.       Assessment and reduction of climate change adaptation risks. Burundi is highly vulnerable to
      climate change. This vulnerability stems mainly from factors like high dependence on natural resources,
      majority rural population, inadequate infrastructure, low crop yields, and low adaptive capacity. The AF does

  V. KEY RISKS

      58.      The overall Operation risk rating remains Moderate. Adding Burundi to the regional program
      poses some additional risks, but these will be mitigated.
      59.        Political and Governance risk is substantial given Burundi's fragile institutions and the 2025
      legislative elections. This risk is mitigated in two ways. First, activities are sequenced such that more complex
      activities (e.g. census) are implemented before the election. Second, activities are designed to allow
      implementation through phone surveys if the need arises to minimize disruptions.
      60.        Macroeconomic risk is substantial given Burundi’s strained public finances and weak
      macroeconomic framework, characterized by a large gap between official and parallel exchange rates. This
      risk will be managed through the World Bank’s and IMF’s support to strengthen the macroeconomic
      framework.
      61.       Fiduciary risk is substantial as INSBU has never managed IDA-financed projects. This risk will be
      mitigated by strengthening the PIU’s capacity through additional staff and training..


  VI. WORLD BANK GRIEVANCE REDRESS
      62.       Grievance Redress. Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected as
      a result of a Bank supported PforR operation, as defined by the applicable policy and procedures, may
      submit complaints to the existing program grievance mechanism or the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service
      (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly reviewed in order to address pertinent
      concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint to the Bank’s
      independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines
      whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non-compliance with its policies and
      procedures, and the Dispute Resolution Service, which provides communities and borrowers with the
      opportunity to address complaints through dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted at any time
      after concerns have been brought directly to the Bank's attention, and Bank Management has been given
      an opportunity to respond. For information on how to submit complaints to the Bank’s Grievance Redress
      Service (GRS), please visit http://www.worldbank.org/GRS. For information on how to submit complaints to
      the Bank’s Accountability Mechanism, please visit https://accountability.worldbank.org.

                       ANNEX 2: IMPLEMENTATION ARRANGEMENTS AND SUPPORT PLAN

   Project Institutional and Implementation Arrangements
   63.     The Project will be implemented by the INSBU. The proposed implementation arrangements seek to build strong
   ownership and sustainability. Its goal is to depart from the traditional approach of creating isolated PIUs towards
   implementation units that are integrated in the regular operations of INSBU. Therefore, all aspects of the project will be
   executed by relevant staff within INSBU but seconded with assistance when needed. The project management team
   that will be established will conduct the day-to-day implementation of the activities. The project management team will
   work in close collaboration with the National Statistical System. The Director General of INS will be in charge of the
   fiduciary responsibility in his role as project coordinator.


   Financial Management
   The key FM arrangements include:
   64.      Budgeting. INSBU will prepare an annual budget based on the procurement and work plans to be submitted to
   the World Bank at least two months before the project’s fiscal year begins. The budgets will follow budgeting guidelines
   in the financial management manuals or Project Operations Manual(POM). During the financial year, the budget will be
   monitored quarterly using IFRs. The POM will define processes for the preparation and execution of the annual work
   plan and budget, and quarterly IFRs will be used to monitor its execution.
   65.     Staffing and accounting system. The members of the fiduciary team have the required qualifications to handle
   the activities, subject to adequate training and mentoring.
   (a) The accounting Information Systems: The software used by INSBU is SAGE SAARI and the version is outdated and
   should not be used to manage the accounting entries of the financed WBG project. The recommendation is for INSBU
   to continue with the acquired software during the PPA phase.
   (b) Accounting Standards: The national accounting system, accrual accounting that follows internationally recognized
   accounting standards, will be used.
   66.     Internal audit and control. INSBU’s internal audit department in charge of Monitoring and internal audit tasks
   shall be training on internal audit standards & requirements. This shall include pairing the department with another
   project’s internal audit team on the portfolio. The specifics of the activities to be implemented by the project shall be
   considered and included in the POM. The internal audit unit of INSBU will review compliance with FM procedures for
   the project activities and conduct internal audit missions on a risk-based approach basis. INSBU’s staff must ensure
   continued adherence to the financial management procedures defined by the POM throughout project implementation
   to ensure that funds are used for their intended purpose.
   67. Governance and Anti-Corruption Arrangement. INSBU will follow existing anti-corruption legislation when dealing
   with fraud and corruption. Financial management arrangements will strengthen internal control systems and audits
   conducted to prevent and mitigate fraud and corruption. In addition, the project will comply with World Bank Anti-
   Corruption Guidelines.
   68. Financial reporting. The PIU will prepare separate quarterly Interim Financial Report (IFR) for submission to the
   World Bank within 45 days of the end of the reporting quarter using IFR formats to be agreed during the negotiation.
   The PIU will prepare consolidated annual financial statements within three months of the end of the fiscal year. At the
