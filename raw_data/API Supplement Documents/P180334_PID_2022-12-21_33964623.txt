                         B. Introduction and Context

                         Country Context

                         1.        Lebanon is three years into multiple unprecedented crises that have erased 15 years of economic growth. The
For Official Use Only




                         crisis started with the acute economic and financial collapse and was compounded by the COVID-19 pandemic, the Port
                         of Beirut explosion, and the spillover effects of the war in Ukraine. The collapse of the local currency and ensuing
                         economy-wide shortages in foreign exchange (FX) have resulted in sharply increasing costs across sectors. By the end of
                         2021, Lebanon’s real GDP index had contracted by 34.6 percent since 2018, comparable to Lebanon’s real GDP index in
                         2007. In July 2022, the World Bank downgraded Lebanon’s economic standing to a low-middle income country.

                         2.       Unemployment rates, including the gender gap, have increased dramatically since 2019, driving job seekers
                         into informality in sectors such as agriculture. The unemployment rate increased from 11.4 percent in 2018-2019 to
                         29.6 percent in January 2022 and the youth unemployment rate (15–24 years old) increased from 23.3 percent in 2018-
                         2019 to 47.8 percent in January 2022.1 The gender gap in labor force participation (LFP) stands at 44 percent, with
                         female LFP at 22.2 percent versus a male LFP at 66.2 percent. The sector with the highest share of informal employment
                         is Agriculture, Fishing and Forestry with 98.1 percent.

                         3.       The adverse shocks have driven an overwhelming share of the population into poverty and vulnerability to
                         food insecurity. Lebanon, a small country of 5.2 million people (July 2021, estimate), hosts the highest per capita
                         concentration of refugees in the world. The country hosts 1.5 million displaced Syrians, including 879,598 registered as
                         refugees with UNHCR, and 257,000 Palestinian refugees.2 In 2021, it was estimated that 81 percent of the Lebanese
                         population live in multidimensional poverty.3 Phone surveys conducted in June–July 2021 found that 46 percent of
                         households reported challenges in accessing food when inflation was at 145 percent.4 Since then, food and beverage
                         inflation on an annual basis surged further to 332 percent in June 2022, water and fuel prices hiked to 594 percent,
                         while the overall consumer price index rose to 210 percent in the same month.5 Food price inflation was further

                         4.       The crisis is disproportionately affecting lagging and under-served rural areas. Existing disparities across urban
                         and rural as well as between rural areas have been accentuated by the crisis. The poorest areas - Baalbek-Hermel, Akkar
                         and the South – have seen disproportionate declines across a range of socioeconomic indicators. At the governorate
                         level, the unemployment rate has increased in all governorates without exception. The largest increase in employment
                         was observed in Baalbek-Hermel (40.7 percent), followed by South Lebanon (36.5 percent) in 2022. Employment in the
                         informal sector has increased by 13.1 percentage points, from 35.2 percent in 2018-2019 to 48.3 percent in January
                         2022.7

                         Sectoral and Institutional Context

                         5.       The agri-food sector has demonstrated resilience during the crises and continues to generate livelihoods and
For Official Use Only




                         income for a large share of the poor. The primary agricultural production’s contribution to GDP increased from 3.2
                         percent (USD 1.77 billion) in 2018 to 8.9 percent (USD 2.32 billion) in 2020. It is estimated that approximately 20 percent
                         of Lebanese households generate primary or secondary income from the broader agri-food sector, with agribusiness as
                         the largest industrial sub-sector of the economy4. The agri-food sector accounted for over 17 percent of total exports in
                         2020 (or USD 0.7 billion on average between 2014 and 2020).8 Lebanon’s top five categories of agricultural and food
                         exports by value were preparations of vegetables, fruit, or nuts; edible fruits and nuts; beverages, spirits, and vinegar;
                         miscellaneous edible preparations; and animal or vegetable fats and oils. Although domestic agri-food production
                         satisfies only 20 percent of local demand, it contributes the largest share to livelihood generation for poor segments of
                         society and is concentrated in the poorest areas, the Beqaa valley and Akkar in particular,5 which have been most
                         heavily affected by the Syrian refugee crisis9. In fact, the agri-food sector is one of the three sectors where Syrian
                         refugees are legally allowed to work, and agriculture is the second-highest employer of displaced Syrians. The multiple
                         challenges facing the sector are putting its resilience and sustainability at risk and posing threats to food security and
                         rural livelihoods.

                         6.       The global rise in fertilizers and fuel prices, compounded by the foreign exchange crisis is severely impacting
                         local agricultural production. Increasing global fertilizer prices and energy costs compounded by the foreign exchange
                         crisis are undermining the viability of agriculture, and threaten to diminish the contribution of local production to food
                         security. According to a survey of agricultural households carried out in July and August 2022 by FAO, over 80 percent of
                         crop producers faced production difficulties, with the most frequent being access to fertilizers (72 percent) and
                         pesticides (59 percent). High food and fuel prices were the most common shocks cited (90 percent and 87 percent,
                         respectively), a significantly higher share than reported in previous rounds conducted in late 2021 and April 2022.10

                         smart and modern modes of production. Even before the financial crisis, the vast majority of Lebanese farmers had
                         little or no access to capital. It was estimated that agriculture represented only about 1 percent of total credit before the

                         8.       Weak value chain integration, lack of aggregation, poor food safety and post-harvest practices result in
                         limited economies of scale and reduced competitiveness. The small size of agricultural land parcels (70 percent of
                         farming households in Lebanon are small-scale) constrains horizontal and vertical linkages across value chains and the
                         lack of access to post-harvest facilities and services is reducing the competitiveness and bargaining power of
                         smallholders. Furthermore, there is a weak regulatory oversight and information and services deficiencies pertaining to
For Official Use Only




                         quality control, including non-existent information on required standards in export markets. These factors limit access to
                         international markets and decrease the competitiveness of Lebanese agricultural products, both locally and
                         internationally. The recent outbreak of cholera further jeopardized the competitiveness of the agricultural sector as it
                         reduced the demand for fresh products out of precaution.

                         9.       The economic and poverty situation in Lebanon is significantly affecting the delivery of basic services which is
                         constraining the agri-food sector’s potential to drive growth in rural areas . The crises have led to a severe
                         deterioration in the already weak provision of basic services. Access to basic infrastructure and services such as
                         electricity, municipal water services or water treatment plants and solid waste management and roads is poor, and
                         more so in rural areas. According to an assessment conducted by the Bank in collaboration with the Ministry of
                         Environment (MoE) of the status of public and private solid waste management (SWM) facilities above 50t/day has
                         revealed that while Lebanon has an available treatment capacity of 5,640 t/d (which is equivalent to the current daily
                         generation rate), the current operational treatment capacity is only 440 t/d which is equivalent to 8 percent of the
                         available treatment capacity. The National Water Sector Strategy of 2021 indicated that only 7 percent of the
                         wastewater in Lebanon is treated and open discharge of untreated domestic wastewater is the main source of
                         contamination of surface water; while other sources include point sources from industrial, healthcare, and touristic
                         establishments, quarries, and agricultural run-off. Impacts are particularly felt by: (i) vulnerable and poorer households
                         with limited access to those essential basic services, directly affecting their exposure to health hazards such as cholera;
                         (ii) farmers as deteriorated water quality reduces the food safety of their products and thus their commercialization
                         potential; (iii) agribusinesses who do not have access to reliable and affordable electricity or the means to invest in
                         alternative energy sources to maintain production capacity and (iv) tourism facilities and other rural economic actors
                         very vulnerable and exposed to environmental deterioration.

                         11.      Climate change is putting additional pressure on already scarce water and land resources. In Lebanon, climate
                         change will intensify temperature and precipitation extremes, causing longer drought periods and seasonal
                         prolongation. Climate change is already shaping the current reality in Lebanon, for example, the mean precipitation in
                         the 2000s was 15 percent lower than in the 1960s.18 The temperatures throughout the mid-century could increase by up
                         to 1.7 °C and 3.2 °C by the end of the century in a high global emissions scenario (SSP3-7.0).19 Projected decreased snow
                         cover by 40 to 70 percent and changes in precipitation will significantly impact water availability. It is estimated that a 10
                         percent reduction in water supply would reduce GDP by 1.6 percent, and significantly reduce crop yields20. Lebanon’s
                         updated 2021 Nationally determined contribution (NDC) lists strengthening the resilience of the agriculture sector

                         agri-food value chain including innovative technical solutions and improved access to climate finance and insurance, and
                         (v) strengthening the enabling institutional environment.21

                         12.      The employment conditions of Lebanese and refugees in rural areas and in the agri-food sector are often
                         poor. The sector is characterized by high levels of informality, especially among low-skilled daily workers, lack of
                         knowledge about or access to adequate social protection, and poor Occupational Safety and Health (OSH) measures.
                         Given the seasonality of farm labor, workers tend to work for multiple employers, further increasing the risk of labor
                         rights violations. Child labor remains a significant concern, particularly among vulnerable refugee and migrant worker
                         communities. Child labor among refugees is the highest in agriculture in rural areas; a 2019 UNICEF survey in the Bekaa
                         Valley, where migrants are concentrated, showed that around 75 percent of refugee children engaged in labor worked
                         in agriculture.22

                          The environmental risk is substantial. There will be many environmental benefits from the project such as better
                         resource efficiency from the climate smart interventions, improving use of pesticides through improved digitized
                         extension, improving wastewater treatment and waste management, introducing organic farming and Biological Pest
                         Control (BPC) which will minimize the use of agrichemicals and enhancing food safety. On the other hand, the project will
                         be associated with some environmental risks including risk of unregulated water lifting pumps; risk on public safety and
                         ecosystem services from rehabilitation of canals and roads and installation of wastewater treatment and waste
                         management facilities; risks and impacts related to operation of SMEs including waste management, air emissions and
                         workers health and safety; risks to health and safety of workers and community related to constructing infrastructure
                         works; risk of overuse of pesticides by some farmers leading to food safety risks; risk of inadequate management of
                         agriculture waste that could lead to wild fires; risk of discharging drainage water contributing to the contamination of
                         water courses; risk of unregulated expansion of agriculture lands by smallholder farmers; risks related to the downstream
                         activities recommended by technical assistance and capacity building activities; and risks related to e-waste management
                         and energy consumption as part of Component 3.1.


                         Social risks are rated 'substantial' and are mainly associated with the potential risk of exclusion of some of the more
                         vulnerable targeted beneficiaries like women led farmers, women rural entrepreneurs, smallholder farmers, poor
                         farmers, youth, refugees, workers/laborers the risk of SEA/SH associated with labour influx, child labour which is already
                         existing in the agricultural sector in Lebanon and forced labour and child labour associated with primary supply workers
                         for solar panels, the risk of weak and ineffective communication, consultation and dissemination of the project grievance
                         mechanism thus resulting in the perception of exclusion and potential rising social tensions, and the potential for
                         restrictions on land use during rehabilitation of irrigation canals and potential land acquisitions associated with
                         construction of water reservoirs. Other risks include SEA/SH risks that may arise during technical assistance and capacity
                         building activities.
