     I.   BACKGROUND AND RATIONALE FOR ADDITIONAL FINANCING

A. Background

1.      The US$20.0 million Bhutan Human Capital Recovery and Resilience Program (HCRRP), which became effective in
March 2022, aimed to promote investments in human capital toward Bhutan’s economic recovery from the coronavirus
(COVID-19) pandemic-induced economic contraction. The Program supported National Key Results Areas (NKRAs) 7 and
11 of the country’s 12th Five-Year Plan (FYP) (2018–2023), which respectively addressed ‘Quality of Education and Skills’
and ‘Productive and Gainful Employment’. An additional financing (AF) of US$3.0 million from the Global Partnership for
Education was made effective in August 2023, to further support NKRA 7.

2.      The 13th FYP (2024–2029) of the Royal Government of Bhutan (RGoB), approved in July 2024, seeks to ensure the
continuity of interventions to support the country’s long-term objective of becoming a ‘High Income Gross National
Happiness (GNH) Economy by 2034’.1 Implementation of the 13th FYP comes at a time of significant national challenges,
including persistent spatial and gender inequalities, high youth unemployment and outmigration, attrition of the human
development (HD) workforce, declining fertility rates, and increasing old-age dependency. These significantly strain the
country’s accumulated human capital and prospects for increased economic productivity, in a country with high exposure
to climate-related risks and a commitment to carbon-neutral economic development.

3.      In this context, a second AF of US$46.2 million for the Program will build on its achievements — including the
expansion of early childhood care and development (ECCD) services, promoting information and communications
technology (ICT) in education service delivery, diversifying skills development programs, and strengthening employment
services and entrepreneurship promotion — toward supporting the 13th FYP for its human capital agenda. At the same
time, this AF seeks to address emerging challenges to human capital, particularly the rising incidence of non-
communicable diseases (NCDs) which are putting a greater strain on the population, affecting overall productivity as well
as the capacity of the health system to respond. RGoB expects the AF to provide much needed resources in the context of
its ambitious 13th FYP goals which would entail both a scale-up of Program results and an expansion of its boundaries.

B. Sectoral Context

4.      Bhutan has experienced a slow post-COVID-19 pandemic economic recovery that is affecting the country’s
capacity to improve human capital, employability, and productivity. The pandemic exposed the country ’s vulnerability to
external shocks and structural challenges, which have resulted in economic contraction and surging unemployment and
brain drain, including from the civil service. Moreover, macroeconomic vulnerability has exacerbated fiscal pressure on
RGoB to invest in the HD sector thus affecting the quality of services delivered. Bhutan faces significant climate change
challenges, with rising risks such as floods, landslides, forest fires, and droughts, driven by shifting weather patterns and
the rapid retreat of glaciers in the Himalayas. The country’s unique geography, with its high-altitude mountains and
sensitive ecosystems, also makes it particularly vulnerable to glacial lake outburst floods, which can devastate downstream
communities. These increasing and more frequent climate hazards threaten vital sectors such as agriculture, hydropower,
and water resources, which are central to the country’s economy and livelihoods, and despite its minimal carbon footprint,
exposure to hazards underscores the urgent need for robust climate adaptation and resilience strategies across all sectors
to sustain economic growth.

5.     The unemployment rate in Bhutan is estimated at 3.7 percent (3.1 percent male and 4.5 percent female) which is
more pronounced for the youth for whom the rate is 19.2 percent (18.9 percent male and 19.6 percent female).2 Specific

horizontal reforms to improve the business environment, strengthen human capital accumulation, and increase the
effectiveness of employment support programs.

6.       Bhutan has been relatively successful in providing core HD services despite the difficult geographical terrain and
dispersed population, both highly exposed to the impacts of climate change. Although the country spends more on
education per child compared to most of its income group peers, the learning-adjusted years of schooling remains
comparatively lower. Consequently, the general skill level of school graduates remains low and the mismatch between
the school curriculum and labor market needs is growing. Greater emphasis on technical and vocational education and
training (TVET), which RGoB is promoting, can create a job market appropriate and future-ready workforce.4 Among small,
medium and large-scale establishments, which have hired TVET graduates, 81.4 percent of employers find TVET graduates
to have the appropriate technical skills and competencies, and 87.0 percent are interested in accepting TVET graduates
for on-the-job training (OJT).5

7.       Although Bhutan has made notable progress in gender equitable development, women still face challenges in
accumulating and utilizing human capital. While gender parity has favored girls in school enrollment, there are gaps at the
TVET (39.9 percent female enrollment)6 and tertiary education levels. Lower enrollment of women in TVET may be linked
to mobility constraints due to social norms around household responsibilities coupled with limited provision of ECCD
services, with ECCD enrollment rates at 38.7 percent.7 Even when women enroll in skills programs, they are more likely to
enroll in traditional trades, agriculture, or hospitality, instead of ICT-related or other high-value skills.

8.       Life expectancy in Bhutan is 72.2 years8 with NCDs as the leading cause of death. A primary reason is the limitation
in the health system’s financing, efficiency, and service provision. The prevalence of NCDs such as cardiovascular disease,
diabetes, and mental health disorders, is rising and the health system faces constraints in managing NCDs due to limited
and unequally distributed diagnostic and treatment infrastructure, insufficient availability of essential NCD medicine, and
shortages and limited capacity of health workers with specialized skills. Universal health coverage remains stagnant at
51.3 percent (as of 2019) with an annual change of −0.1 percent.9 The availability of providers recently trained on NCDs is
low — less than 30.0 percent of health facilities had at least one health care provider who received training on diabetes,
cervical cancer screening, and hypertension in the last two years. 6.9 percent and around 17.0 percent of facilities had at
least one health care provider with recent training on detoxification/rehabilitative services or diagnosis/management of
common mental disorders, respectively. 10 Recent increase in mental health issues, especially among the youth, is
compounding concerns on youth inclusion and productivity, with 16.2 percent of anxiety and depression problems
reported among those ages 15–19. 11 Capacity within the health sector to address these issues is in its nascent stage. There

C. Rationale for Bank Support

9.       An AF was considered the most suitable option to continue supporting Bhutan’s transition to the 13th FYP. The
original Program already addresses key elements of the 13th FYP where human development remains prioritized, now
with a focus on economic development as its driving force. Among its ten national goals toward becoming a high-income
GNH economy, the 13th FYP intends to increase the Human Capital Index in Bhutan from 0.48 to 0.61 by 2029 (and to 0.71
by 2034), create full employment with quality jobs by 2027, and quadruple the income of the bottom two quintiles by
2029. The original Program, via its investments in foundational learning and employment services, is already positioned
to contribute toward these national goals, and the AF would serve to enhance the support provided to date. In view of
these ambitious goals, RGoB has outlined four programs in the 13th FYP — (a) Education Transformation Program (EdTP),
(b) 21st Century Skilling Program, (c) Economic Transformation Program (ETP), and (d) Healthy Drukyul Program (HDP) —
with an expected outlay of BTN 140 billion (approximately US$1.62 billion).

10.     With this significant HD agenda underpinned by constitutional commitments and driven by consecutive FYPs,
Bhutan has received significant financial and technical assistance from several development partners. The Asian
Development Bank (ADB) has been supporting improvements to the TVET system toward enhancing coverage and
addressing youth unemployment. ADB has also had an extensive engagement in the health sector addressing the
enhancement of the system’s performance. The Japan International Cooperation Agency has provided significant financing
which has helped address economic and social impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic for greater resilience and continues to
provide technical assistance for using digital technology in the health sector. The European Union is currently supporting
education and skills digitalization for improved education and employability outcomes. The Government of India (GoI) has
committed BTN 15 billion (approximately US$180 million) over the course of the 13th FYP. A focus of the Economic
Stimulus Plan, supported by the GoI financing, will be on enhancing domestic production, reducing youth unemployment,
increasing new businesses, reviving the tourism sector, and improving foreign currency reserves. Due to the extent of the
needs in Bhutan, gaps remain within this financing environment which the AF would help fill while synergizing efforts with
development partners.

11.     The second AF is fully aligned with the Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Bhutan FY2021-2024 (Report No.
154927-BT), discussed by the World Bank Board of Executive Directors on January 14, 2021, which remains relevant. The
new CPF is currently under preparation. Specifically, the AF continues to support ‘Focus Area 1: Human Capital’ and ‘Focus
Area 2: Resilience’, of the CPF, which seek to address a continuum of interventions along the lifecycle. The AF would help
strengthen foundational learning in the early years and school-going years, enhance the school-to-work transition by
imparting relevant skills, improve employment services to better connect the working age population to the labor market,
and strengthen basic services to address rising health concerns. Overall, the support for core HD services will continue to
enhance the resilience of the population from shocks, provide opportunities, and contribute toward poverty reduction.

 II. INTRODUCTION

A. Context

13.       This Program Paper seeks the approval of the Executive Directors of a second AF in an amount of US$46.2 million
to the Kingdom of Bhutan for the HCRRP (P174399). The AF will expand the RGoB program boundary and introduce new
DLIs to reflect new and scaled-up activities given the continued needs of the HD sector. New results will be consistent
with, and contribute further to, the PDO to support building, protecting, and utilizing human capital in Bhutan, which will
remain unchanged. Accordingly, the AF would build on the achievements of the original Program by continuing to support
interventions prioritized by RGoB toward better educational and employment outcomes, adding activities to enhance the
impact of earlier investments, and include support for the health sector to expand the broader contribution to human
capital. It would address the full lifecycle to ensure basic services are strengthened while protecting the population from
climatic shocks and increasing their resilience. In doing so, the Program will be recalibrated to transition its support for
the 12th FYP to the 13th FYP to cover its full period (the AF will have a closing date of June 30, 2029). Annex 2 maps the
education and employment priorities across the two FYPs and illustrates the health priorities under the 13th FYP. The
addenda to the Technical Assessment and Fiduciary Systems Assessment (FSA) provide details on specific ‘projects’ and
‘activities’ under the 13th FYP which expand the boundaries of the Program.

14.      To address the earliest stages of the lifecycle and facilitate female labor force participation, the AF would seek to
reduce the burden of childcare by continuing to support the quality and coverage of ECCD services, enabling women to
work outside their homes. To address the foundational learning environment, investments would be needed to improve
the pedagogical and physical environments, and governance of the education system.13 Essential elements would include
school curriculum transformation in line with global standards, focusing on science, technology, engineering, and
mathematics (STEM); providing teacher development to enhance competencies and motivation; building capacity to
identify and refer individuals to mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) services; and establishing fit-for-purpose
infrastructure (including internet connectivity, climate-resilient measures, and WASH facilities), with better use of existing
digital platforms.

15.      To facilitate the school-to-work transition, the TVET system would promote labor market appropriate skills
training and certification. Employment outcomes can be boosted by scaling up active labor market programs (ALMPs) such
as the SDP (providing numerous trades training and a modest stipend for participation), Youth Engagement and Livelihood
Program (YELP) (an OJT program connecting jobseekers to employers and providing a temporary wage subsidy), and
programs which facilitate overseas employment and returnee migrants’ opportunities for economic reintegration, among
others. The AF would support enhancement and expansion of entrepreneurship programs by providing access to finance
and promoting small business development in priority sectors, including climate-resilient trades and sectors less
vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. This would necessitate support for jobs-focused services by strengthening
the technical capacity of Employment Service Centers (ESCs) and BICs, including strengthening the Bhutan Labor Market

16.     Protecting human capital would also entail support for the health sector where priorities include providing health
workers’ training and care management, investing in the primary health care system’s capacity, and leveraging digital
health solutions to advance NCD-focused care quality and efficiency, to address emerging challenges.14 The AF would

strengthen health system management and human resource capacity, while building capacity to adapt and cope with

improvements in MNCHN outcomes, reduced prevalence of NCD, improved mental health, increased life expectancy and
healthy years, increased climate resilience, and ultimately the productivity of the population. As is the case for the
education workforce, the AF would provide a special focus on the capacity building of health workers for better career

C. Revised Results Area

(a) Results Area 1: People and Programs (Enhancing Capacity and Quality of HD Service Providers and Interventions)

18.    DLI 1 (ECCD Service Quality), DLI 5 (SDP Inclusion), DLI 6 (SDP Employment Linkage), DLI 7 (Entrepreneurship
Promotion) and DLI 9 (SDP Climate Resilience) are being remapped under this new results area as they primarily relate to
the improvement of human resource capacity and program quality. Six new DLIs are being introduced.

19.      DLI 11: ECCD Workforce Professionalization (ECCD facilitators provided higher qualifications) (US$2.0 million).
This DLI would build on DLI 1 (ECCD Service Quality) under the original Program by incentivizing further enrollment of in-
service ECCD facilitators in the Diploma in ECCD and gradually in the Bachelor’s program, aiming to reach at least 500
facilitators with the Diploma course (around 80 percent of facilitators in the country) and eventually 100 with the
Bachelor’s program. Financial incentives such as scholarships or stipends would encourage enrollment. Input to the
Bachelor’s program may include development of a standardized, inclusive, and culturally appropriate curriculum that
integrates modern teaching methodologies and best practice child development principles. Continuous professional
development opportunities would be provided to ensure skills remain updated and facilitators would maintain a sustained
commitment toward improving early childhood outcomes.

20.    DLI 12: Teaching Workforce Professionalization (School teachers upskilled on new methods and subject matter)
(US$4.0 million). This DLI would build on DLI 3 (ICT in Education) and DLI 4 (Climate Resilience in Education) under the

                                                                                                                                Page 8

          Human Capital Recovery & Resilience Program (P174399) - AF Seq 2                                      PROGRAM PAPER


original Program by promoting professional development for at least 9,000 teachers (around 90 percent of teachers in the
country) toward improving foundational learning outcomes. A standardized training program for teachers would cover
pedagogy with an emphasis on digital fluency, mental health to support students’ well-being, SEN training to enhance
inclusivity, and climate awareness to address emerging challenges, among others. Training content on climate awareness
would be based on specific vulnerabilities relevant to Bhutan, address relevant disaster response protocols, and include
consumer awareness on energy and resource usage in line with the country’s carbon neutral goals. Teachers would be
able to apply newly acquired skills to support schools in observing social forestry events, make use of nearby water streams
and protecting sources, and create awareness on reduction of plastic use, among others. These trainings would help
address evolving national and global needs for the future workforce. Ensuring that training initiatives are accessible and
supported by clear career progression frameworks will empower teachers to continuously acquire new skills in an evolving
educational landscape and contribute toward their motivation, and post-training assessments and on-the-job evaluations
would ascertain training effectiveness.

21.      DLI 13: TVET and SDP Scale-Up (Jobseekers receiving labor market relevant skills) (US$5.0 million). This DLI
would build on DLI 5 (SDP Inclusion) and DLI 9 (SDP Climate Resilience) and promote uptake of both TVET and SDP courses
across technical training institutes, aiming to reach 23,000 enrollments. Regularly updated enrollment plans would
promote courses relevant for priority sectors and would be informed by the gender-inclusive and climate-responsive
strategies for SDP supported under the original Program. The climate-responsive courses incorporated in SDP are expected
to be linked to Bhutan’s specific climate vulnerabilities and include mitigation-related awareness modules. There would
be a focus on promoting courses carrying better national certifications to better meet private sector expectations
particularly in terms of green skills such as installing solar panels, retrofitting buildings for energy efficiency, and managing
waste and recycling systems. Inputs would include promotion and outreach, industry collaboration, stipends to ease
financial burdens and encourage participation, capacity building of instructors, as well as course curriculum enhancement
to align with emerging industries and technological advancements. Overall, these are expected to enable the current
workforce to obtain the right skills for available jobs as well as adapt to future market needs, also considering Bhutan’s
climate vulnerability.

22.     DLI 14: Employment Service Enhancement (Jobs placed via targeted programs) (US$4.6 million). This DLI would
build on DLI 6 (SDP Employment Linkage) and DLI 8 (Employment Support) and enhance the capacity of employment
service delivery, particularly via the ESCs, aiming to reach 32,000 job placements. This would require the standardization
of ESC functions and regular training for the workforce for better client engagement and ability to make more effective
job referrals and placements via programs such as YELP, NRP, and OEP, among others. With enhanced capacity,
employment services would be in a position to connect prospective workers, particularly those emerging from relevant
TVET and SDP courses to jobs in emerging green sectors or in less climate-affected regions. Inputs would include
development of training content, promotion for ESCs and the various ALMPs, wage subsidies for YELP, establishment of
partnerships with industries, and training on new features of the BLMIS, among others. Outcomes analyses of jobs
placements via targeted programs would be carried out to inform enhancements to program design for better impact.

23.      DLI 15: Entrepreneurship Service Enhancement (Businesses receiving start-up financing) (US$4.1 million). This
DLI would build on DLI 7 (Entrepreneurship Promotion) and enhance the capacity of entrepreneurship service delivery,
particularly via the BICs, aiming to provide access to financing for 350 small businesses. More small businesses receiving
access to start-up funds are expected to improve their productivity and create a modest number of new jobs. This would
entail the standardization of BIC functions to better serve as a one-stop shop for entrepreneurship development needs,
regular training for the workforce including on new features of the BLMIS, and development of guidelines for the operation
of a business start-up fund which will be established. The enhancement of current entrepreneurship courses,
establishment of mentorship networks, and fostering peer communities would also enhance the support system for
prospective entrepreneurs. The start-up fund would be evaluated to assess its process and impacts.

24.      DLI 16: Health Workforce Professionalization (Health workers upskilled on new protocols and clinical
competencies) (US$4.0 million). This DLI seeks to promote professional development for at least 750 health workers (over
50 percent of health workers in the country) toward improving reskilling or upskilling a range of professionals, covering
PHC health assistants, medical officers, nurses, general specialists, across the primary, secondary and tertiary health care
levels, and community groups. Training would also cover 400 community members to extend outreach and enable better
referrals to the health system (intended to cover 100 gewogs [subdistricts] in the country). A standardized training
program for health workers would cover the use of digital systems, health data, and care management, covering emerging
health issues such as climate-sensitive diseases with a focus on NCDs and mental health. Inputs would include adoption
of the Bhutan Healthcare Standard for Quality Assurance (BHSQA) to support better quality and adequacy of care; policies
on data sharing, security, and privacy for improved patient digital safety; and clinical pathways to better address NCD,
MNCHN, mental health needs, and help address the additional burden of disease due to climate change. Training initiatives
would be designed to provide refresher courses and certifications to ensure that health workers continue to acquire
updated skills throughout their careers, and post-training assessments and on-the-job evaluations would be embedded
to measure the effectiveness of such training and understand how skills are applied in practice.

(b) Results Area 2: Facilities (Enhancing Physical Infrastructure and Connectivity of HD Service Locations)

25.     DLI 2 (ECCD Service Access), DLI 4 (Climate Resilience in Education), and DLI 10 (WASH Facilities for Education) are
being remapped under this new results area as they primarily relate to the overall improvement of features of ECCD
centers and schools. Two new DLIs are being introduced.

26.      DLI 17: Education Facilities (Schools upgraded to improve learning environment) (US$7.0 million). This DLI would
build on DLI 3 (ICT in Education), DLI 4 (Climate Resilience in Education), and DLI 10 (WASH facilities for Education) in two
ways. First, this DLI would support the upgrades of 40 schools with selected facilities, 15 which may prioritize the
establishment of model schools across Bhutan with comprehensive standards for facilities with a minimum package of
selected interventions, covering improved internet connectivity and ICT equipment, safety and disaster-resilience
features, climate-responsive features, and gender and SEN-inclusive WASH facilities, among others. These would
holistically address the physical environment for foundational learning in line with national standards for school
infrastructure. Second, this DLI would support 60 other schools where WASH facilities are found to be substandard to
ensure greater student coverage with the most fundamental infrastructural amenity and help address the additional
burden of climate-sensitive diseases, particularly water-borne diseases. Upgrades would be specifically adapted to
relevant climate challenges and the location in which schools are situated, which may address the risks of mudslides,
flooding, or drought (such as by improved drainage or earthquake-resilient retrofitting). Schools making the minimum
package of upgrades would benefit from improved ventilation and insulation, and heating and cooling systems, to improve
energy efficiency. These upgrades are expected to support learning continuity during adverse weather conditions.

27.      DLI 18: Health Facilities (Health facilities upgraded to improve NCD diagnostics and control) (US$7.0 million).
This DLI seeks to support the upgrades of 100 outpatient facilities16 spanning selected PHCs and hospitals as well as 50
laboratories with quality internet connectivity, basic equipment, and diagnostic tools, accessibility features, safety and
disaster-resilience features, climate-responsive features, in line with national standards for health facility infrastructure.
Similar to the package of upgrades for selected schools, upgrades would be tailored to relevant climate challenges and
locality considerations, to improve both health workers’ and patients’ experience, and address the additional burden of
disease by enhancing service delivery for climate-sensitive diseases, focusing on NCDs, and strengthen service continuity

(c) Results Area 3: Digital (Enhancing Information Management Systems of HD Services)

28.     DLI 3 (ICT in Education) and DLI 8 (Employment Support) are being remapped under this new results area as they
primarily relate to the improvement of information management systems used in service delivery. Three new DLIs are
being introduced.

29.      DLI 19: Education Systems (LMS enhanced to improve coverage and service quality) (US$2.0 million). This DLI
would build on DLI 3 (ICT in Education) to leverage digital connectivity for greater use of the LMS, aiming to reach at least
6,000 teachers (around 60 percent of teachers in the country) and 100,000 students (around two-thirds of students) with
active use of the system. This would entail enhancing the LMS to incorporate the school curriculum, with e-content also
addressing newly introduced topics such as climate change and related impacts as per the national curriculum, as well as
incorporating functions to enable continuous outcome assessments to provide feedback to the learning process. Climate
change topics integrated across a range of subjects would cover awareness of vulnerabilities as well as mitigation
measures. Inputs would include interface redesign across devices, infrastructural assessments to ensure smooth
performance with high access volume, development of innovative tools as virtual classrooms to foster active learning, use
of artificial intelligence (AI)-driven algorithms to customize learning paths based on individuals’ progress and needs,
advanced analytics, among others.

30.      DLI 20: Employment Systems (BLMIS enhanced to improve service quality) (US$2.0 million). This DLI would build
on DLI 8 (Employment Support) to further improve the BLMIS’ capacity to capture more data relating to employment and
entrepreneurship services for decision-making and integrate with other relevant information systems. This would facilitate
central tracking of client footfall in ESCs and BICs to enable follow-up on jobseeker needs and administration of business
incubation services. Improved tracking systems would help better identify gaps in green skills provision which would
inform policymaking for targeted training programs and assess the impacts of climate hazards on climate vulnerable
sectors and of green policies on job creation or changes in occupational needs in the private sector. Inputs would include
the development of AI-driven search and matching algorithms, entrepreneurship program tools, dynamic job posting
capabilities, and advanced analytics, among others.

31.     DLI 21: Health Systems (ePIS and telehealth program enhanced to improve coverage and service quality)
(US$2.0 million). This DLI seeks to improve the health information ecosystem to better use data analytics for policymaking
and resource allocation, by linking the ePIS with the District Health Information System (DHIS2) with the integration of
BHSQA quality indicators, aiming to reach regular ePIS usage in 180 PHCs. It would also support the development of digital
health applications under a national telehealth program, where the objective is to initiate the use of at least one AI-
assisted service, such as teleradiology and telepathology, among others. Telehealth services would contribute to both
adaptive and mitigative aspects of climate change, addressing climate-related hindrances to travel and facilitating care
delivery to under-resourced areas, supporting timely detection of outbreaks, improving distribution of work across
outpatient facilities, addressing the additional burden of climate-sensitive diseases, and strengthening service continuity
during climate hazards, among others, which would contribute to reductions in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Inputs
would include development of data mapping and transformation tools, adoption of interoperability standards, design of
scalable infrastructure to handle increasing user loads as adoption grows, and advanced analytics, among others.

B. Fiduciary

34.     No substantial changes have been made to the FSA that will affect the AF. MoESD, MoICE, and MoH follow country
systems, and financial management and procurement systems are considered adequate and reasonably advanced, based
on the ongoing successful experience under the parent Program. The residual fiduciary risk is assessed as moderate and
the ongoing effective mitigation measures will be continued for the AF. Fiduciary actions under the PAP are being complied
with and would continue to apply with some modifications.

35.      Bhutan has good fiscal discipline in terms of a comprehensive coverage of the budget, orderly execution, good
control over expenditure commitments and fiscal risks, timely and reliable financial reporting, and robust audit and
legislative scrutiny. An addendum to the FSA has been prepared covering implementation progress of mitigation actions
included in the original FSA and its first addendum, as well as other changes in the fiduciary environment affecting the AF.
There are no overdue audit reports or any ineligible expenditures under any of the implementing agencies. In FY2023, the
overall budget utilization across the three implementing agencies was 78.01 percent and rose to 80.65 percent in FY2024.

36.     For the IPF Component to be newly implemented by MoICE and MoH, financial management arrangements
followed by MoESD will now also be applicable to MoICE and MoH. The IPF Component would have a separate budget
code outside the Program boundaries. The funds flow for the IPF Component will take place through a separate Designated
Account in the form of a government budget fund account from where payments would be made. Project Letters of Credit
will be opened by MoICE and MoH under the Designated Account from which payments would be made. Segregated
interim unaudited financial reports for the IPF Component will be prepared by all implementing agencies for submission
to the World Bank within 45 days of the close of each semester. The Royal Audit Authority will carry out the external audit
of the segregated annual financial statements, which would be submitted to the World Bank within nine months of the

process given the nature of expenditures.

C. Environment and Social

39.      The environmental and social (E&S) risk of the PforR portion (DLI-based financing) of the AF is moderate. Among

interventions, covering internet connectivity and equipment requirements, safety and disaster-resilience features,
climate-responsive features, and continue to support gender and SEN-inclusive WASH facilities. Upgrading selected

(a) Gender

46.       The Program continues the original gender-focused activities and includes new activities to further narrow gender
gaps related to human capital accumulation. It contributes to lessening gender gaps by expanding ECCD services to
promote women’s economic participation (supported by DLI 2) and enhancing TVET and SDP strategies to promote
employability of women (supported by DLI 5). The gender digital divide is also being addressed by ensuring that female
teachers are covered by training on digital curriculum delivery and by strengthening access to remote and digital learning
that benefits girls in school. Emphasizing SDP outside the three major urban centers is also expected to help women
overcome mobility challenges and accessing skills training would help diversify their engagement from agricultural and
unpaid family work. Under the AF, further efforts in improving infrastructure in educational, employment, and health
facilities are expected to further broaden outreach to women and girls. The inclusion of mental health services in the AF
is also expected to enable better educational outcomes and professional opportunities for the female population as more
women than men reported mental health problems.18

(c) Climate Change and Paris Alignment

49.     The climate and disaster risk screening for the AF has assessed a low exposure rating of the Program location. To
ensure better resilience to climatic shocks, the original Program supports the introduction of climate-focused content in
the education curriculum, as well as supporting climate-adaptive environments in selected schools to safeguard students
from climatic impacts (DLI 4, total value of US$1.0 million). Additionally, the original Program incentivizes training on
climate-responsive occupations under the SDP and requires the adoption of a strategy to mainstream climate-
responsiveness of skills training programs (DLI 9, total value of US$1.0 million). Under DLI 9, from a total of 67 courses
offered under SDP by MoESD as of October 2023, 12 are climate responsive. This has fulfilled the requirement of one of
the climate-focused DLRs and a quarter of the financing allocated to these initiatives under the relevant DLIs has been
disbursed. Progress in meeting the other climate-focused DLRs is underway. The AF would expand on the existing DLIs
including by establishing standards for school buildings to include climate considerations and additional upgrading of
schools in line with such standards (DLI 17, total value of US$7.0 million), as well as technical support for climate risk
mitigation assessments of existing and future school sites (through the IPF Component). It would further promote training
on green jobs and the support skills diversification from agriculture, toward contributing to the improved productivity of
small entrepreneurs (DLI 13 and DLI 15, total value of US$9.1 million).

50.      Climate change can exert an additional strain on the health system due to increased maternal mortality, increased
pulmonary diseases, increased cases of cancer, as well as vector-borne and waterborne diseases. The addition of health
sector activities contributing to the protection of human capital would support improved capacity to mitigate climatic
effects. The introduction of a telehealth program (two DLRs under DLI 21, total value of US$1.0 million) would contribute
to reduced carbon emissions of travel and additional physical facilities, as well as improved service delivery in terms of
accuracy of diagnoses and speed of response. The Program furthermore contributes to mitigation efforts by building the
professional capacity of health workers, upgrading health infrastructure, and investing in innovative systems.
Infrastructural improvements would enable better quality of services including during climate-related events and
outbreaks and contribute to enhancing the climate resilience of health services that benefit climate-vulnerable
populations (DLI 18, total value of US$7.0 million).

Change. These include a pledge to remain carbon neutral and lay out priorities for both climate mitigation and adaptation
measures. These are further expanded upon in Bhutan’s Long-Term Low Greenhouse Gas Emission and Climate Resilient

and in the National Adaptation Program of the Kingdom of Bhutan, which focuses on strategies to adapt to climate change.

        will specifically include designs to improve energy performance in line with the updated national standards

V. KEY RISKS

53.     The overall risk rating for the Program, inclusive of the AF, remains moderate as no additional risks are foreseen.
The E&S, political and governance, sector strategies and policies, fiduciary, and stakeholder risks remain moderate. The
remaining three risk categories are deemed substantial, arising from potential budgetary constraints (macroeconomic
risk), multisectoral design complexities (technical design of the Program), and HD workforce challenges (institutional
capacity for implementation and sustainability risk).

54.     Macroeconomic risks continue to be substantial. Bhutan faces inherent structural challenges being a landlocked
state, having a small labor force, and a small domestic market. There has been a widening fiscal deficit and rising public
debt, exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic and global geopolitical crises elsewhere, which have highlighted Bhutan’s


                                                                                                                    Page 18
             The World Bank
             Human Capital Recovery & Resilience Program (P174399) - AF Seq 2                                PROGRAM PAPER


vulnerability to external shocks, caused significant economic contraction, and disrupted the country’s growth trajectory.
Bhutan’s upcoming graduation from the UN’s LDC status will also gradually limit access to concessional loans and overseas
development assistance, which accounts for a significant portion of its budget. Furthermore, the reliance on the
hydropower sector, which is subject to volatility, poses a significant fiscal risk.23 Unemployment rates have increased since
the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly among young and educated individuals in urban areas, which has also been a driver
for the post-pandemic surge in outmigration. While the outmigration trend has slowed down in 2024 compared to its
earlier stages, and tourist arrivals contributing to economic activity has risen at the same time, concerted efforts are
needed for critical workforce retention. The Program’s contribution to mitigating the effects of such risks, together with
the AF, would be via continued support for common objectives across the 12th and 13th FYPs, such as expanding skills
training programs for the HD workforce and providing entrepreneurship development opportunities in priority sectors
such as digital services and tourism, for which long-term budgetary commitments are expected.

55.      Risks arising from the technical design of the Program are substantial. Building, protecting, and utilizing human
capital across the lifecycle requires coordinated efforts across education, employment, and health services, all of which
are facing capacity constraints. Maximizing the impact of such efforts with limited financing would entail rigorous planning
of investments in HD services addressing spatial and gender disparities, as well as climate resilience needs given Bhutan’s
vulnerability to climatic shocks. The PforR instrument itself continues to be a challenge in terms of ensuring adequate and
timely government program financing for sectoral activities driven by DLIs. Investments in digital administrative systems
of MoESD, MoICE, and MoH to minimize data gaps within and across agencies for better decision-making (potential DLRs)
would contribute to mitigating such risks, while sharpening of responsibilities in the existing Implementation Guidance
Note for the Program, as well as specific actions in the PAP, would further help address technical design complexities.

56.     MoESD and MoICE would largely focus on scaling up existing activities, with selected new interventions, while the
inclusion of MoH introduces a new set of sectoral activities to the Program. On balance, the institutional capacity for
implementation and sustainability continues to remain a substantial risk factor. This capacity for implementation overall
has been constrained by the attrition of the HD workforce, however, new DLIs to improve reskilling/upskilling toward
improved service delivery are expected to contribute to efforts on workforce retention. In addition, the IPF Component
would support all implementing agencies with dedicated resources for improved Program coordination, implementation,
and monitoring.

 VI. WORLD BANK GRIEVANCE REDRESS

57.     Communities and individuals who believe that they are adversely affected as a result of a Bank supported PforR
operation, as defined by the applicable policy and procedures, may submit complaints to the existing program grievance
mechanism or the Bank’s Grievance Redress Service (GRS). The GRS ensures that complaints received are promptly
reviewed in order to address pertinent concerns. Project affected communities and individuals may submit their complaint
to the Bank’s independent Accountability Mechanism (AM). The AM houses the Inspection Panel, which determines
whether harm occurred, or could occur, as a result of Bank non-compliance with its policies and procedures, and the
Dispute Resolution Service (DRS), which provides communities and borrowers with the opportunity to address complaints
through dispute resolution. Complaints may be submitted at any time after concerns have been brought directly to the
Bank’s attention, and Bank Management has been given an opportunity to respond. For information on how to submit
complaints to the Bank’s GRS, visit http://www.worldbank.org/GRS. For information on how to submit complaints to the
Bank’s AM, visit https://accountability.worldbank.org.
