   B. Introduction and Context

           Strategic Context

           1.       The proposed Project aims to connect more people, enterprises, and institutions across rural Georgia
           to high-quality, affordable broadband internet, and to promote the use of digital services. Georgia’s economic
           growth has been strong over the past decade, with far�?reaching reforms improving governance and the business
           environment. A key challenge for Georgia is to overcome economic dualism, especially to ensure that people and
           enterprises in rural areas have equal access to opportunity as their urban compatriots. Investing in digital
           connectivity across rural Georgia and promoting the digital services that use improved connectivity will bridge
           this divide and help equalize access to digital opportunities. The Project will also support Georgia’s recovery from
           and help build resilience to natural disasters and crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic.

           Country Context

           2.      Georgia’s economy has grown significantly over the past decade and positioning it as an upper -middle
           income country. A small and open economy, Georgia has a population of 3.7 million, a land area of 69,700 km2,
           and ranks seventh in the global Ease of Doing Business rankings.1 Between 2010 and 2018, the Georgian economy
           grew at an average annual rate of 4.8 percent, fueled by reforms in economic management and governance, and
           a move to boost exports and integration with the regional and global economy. Poverty has declined, even though
           inequality is still a concern.

           3.      Georgia will need to build bridges to overcome income inequality and a lack of economic opportunities,
           especially in rural areas, to address economic dualism. Poverty and inequality remain high in rural areas; one in
           every five people is still poor. As of 2019, rural households earned 11 percent less than urban households on
           average; the gap on a per capita basis is 16 percent.2 And especially in rural areas, households headed by women
           are particularly vulnerable to poverty.3 The Systematic Country Diagnostic identified addressing economic dualism

           4.      The COVID-19 pandemic and its aftermath has worsened the outlook for the Georgian economy and
           could disrupt the country’s growth trajectory. Real gross domestic product (GDP) is expected to decline by over
           4 percent in 2020.5 Key challenges foreseen stem from declines in domestic consumption and investment, and in

           share of the employed might see their incomes reduced due to the contraction in economic activity; some people
           might be pushed into poverty in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic. At the same time, the Government of
           Georgia (GoG) has taken decisive steps to manage the economic fallout. A cautious reopening of the economy is
           underway as of June 2020.

           5.       Digital technologies can build and support resilience to natural disasters, the effects of climate change,
           and future events such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Georgia is highly vulnerable to climate change and natural
           disasters. Risks include the negative effects of a rise in temperatures on agriculture, and of the increasing
           frequency and severity of droughts, floods, frosts, and extreme temperature events. These effects could be more
           severe in rural and remote mountainous regions, where agriculture is still the major economic activity.
           Investments in climate-resilient infrastructure, and which promote climate-smart agriculture and enable
           economic diversification will be part of the response. In addition, investments that help people and enterprises
           stay connected and working during situations such as natural disasters and pandemics will also boost economic
           resilience. Improved connectivity can boost remote learning and support livelihoods hampered by pandemic-
           required social distancing. And they will support wider adoption of digital technology solutions for post-disaster
           service delivery and emergency response in affected rural communities.

          Sectoral and Institutional Context

           6.      The expansion of Georgia’s digital economy has continued over the last two decades. Following the
           Rose Revolution in 2003, government focus has been on improving public service delivery and management by
           using digital technologies.7 Since 2013, the GoG has integrated national digital and related initiatives in its
           programs: to support innovation and the use of technology across the economy, to expand access to the internet,
           and build digital skills among students and young people.8

           7.      Open markets and private-sector investments have worked well together to bring many people online.
           Access to and use of basic internet has developed at a steady pace and has been driven by private sector
           investment. As of 2019, four-fifths of households have at least basic internet access, while over 70 percent of
           people use the internet. Broadband adoption is also on the rise with 67 percent of households subscribing to fixed
           broadband services, and mobile broadband connections accounting for over 50 percent of total mobile
           subscriptions. Fifth generation (5G) mobile networks are expected to be introduced in 2020.9

           8.       Despite this progress, access to high-quality broadband networks is limited in rural areas; this
           contributes to economic dualism and could worsen other social divides. Fixed broadband networks are less
           common outside of the cities: a third of rural households connect to fixed broadband as compared to over 90
           percent of urban households. And only a quarter of rural households subscribe to optical fiber cable (OFC) based
           internet services (compared with 57 percent nationally).10 And even though mobile broadband networks cover
           most of the population, the quality of service varies, with rural areas often not well-served in terms of connection
           speeds or reliable coverage. These gaps imply differences in the ability of people and enterprises outside of the
           larger cities to access the full range of digital opportunities.

           9.      The fixed broadband market is underdeveloped due to high deployment costs and limited competition.
           The costs of building high-quality broadband infrastructure for many rural settlements are significant for private
           investors, especially given the perception of limited potential returns on investment. In addition, limited
           competition in means that there has been underinvestment in middle-mile infrastructure, which connects
           settlements to the national network and thence the global internet. Only 3 of 29 major middle-mile network
           routes in the country have more than two competing service providers.

           10.      These market failures—limited competition, high deployment costs, and underinvestment—limit the
           expansion of middle-mile infrastructure capable of delivering high-speed broadband in many rural settlements.
           Investments in upgrading and expanding middle-mile networks have remained limited. The result is that internet
           services available in rural areas (and some smaller towns) are either of limited quality; upstream networks cannot
           deliver the capacity needed for high-speed connectivity to downstream users. Or, services are unaffordable for
           many households, and especially for rural households or households headed by women. Moreover, even where
           internet services are available, consumers regularly complain of limited choice of plans and lower-than-advertised
           speeds, implying that they are in effect paying more per unit than say consumers in larger cities.11

           11.      Boosting rural broadband internet adoption will also require addressing the lack of relevant use-cases
           to attract rural users. Internet use has been increasing nationally, but people in rural Georgia lag people in urban
           Georgia and in rural EU in activities such as internet banking, seeking health information online, or searching for
           jobs online. In the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, anecdotal reports suggest that many people have switched
           to digital services where possible; this might trigger a change in how people, enterprises, and institutions use the
           internet in the longer-term and highlight the possibilities of use-cases such as online learning and telemedicine.
           Even so, many households are not connected or do not have access to devices; this constrained the reach of
           online learning during school closures, for example. Improved digital connectivity can also help rural populations
           build resilience against climate change and related natural disasters such as droughts, earthquakes, floods.

           12.     There are specific gender gaps in internet use. A small yet constant gender gap—about three percentage
           points—persists among recent internet users. This is even as the share of women who have never used the
           internet has fallen sharply from 41 percent in 2016 to 29 percent in 2019.12 The limited depth of use of broadband
           internet extends to enterprises as well. Low awareness of how to implement use-cases may be why relative few
           enterprises subscribe to broadband (connections above 30 Mbps) even where it is available: in 2019, only 16

           13.     The policy context is in line with international good practices. Georgia has signed an Association
           Agreement with the European Union (EU) and is a member of the World Trade Organization (WTO). The line
           Ministry of Economy and Sustainable Development of Georgia (MOESD), sets policy and strategy for the
           information and communication technology sector through a dedicated department. The ComCom is a statutorily
           mandated independent regulatory agency.14 The legal and regulatory frameworks reflect global best practices
           and are transposing EU practices in line with Association Agreement commitments. To improve access and
           affordability, the Government—through policymaker MOESD—and the regulator, ComCom have identified or
           implemented a range of measures to increase competition in markets or to regulate wholesale markets.15

           14.       In January 2020, the Government adopted a National Broadband Development Strategy (NBDS) to
           2025. The NBDS—prepared with technical assistance of the World Bank (P167086), and with the support of the
           EU4Digital initiative of the EU16—sets ambitious goals for the sector, in line with the EU’s Gigabit Society targets.
           It recognizes the importance of available, affordable, and reliable broadband connectivity as a foundational
           infrastructure to implement its economic development vision. The strategy also recognizes the need to promote
           digital inclusion and build skills and demand for broadband internet access, especially in rural areas.

           15.     The Government plans to prepare and adopt a digital Georgia strategy in 2021. There has been much
           progress in individual public and private agencies’ efforts to digitize services, and to support digital innovation by
           entrepreneurs, businesses, and government. However, the overall strategic vision for digital economy
           development in Georgia is still unstated and there is no institutional mechanism to coordinate these efforts across
           stakeholders. This represents a missed opportunity for efficiency and to generate visibility to mobilize resources,
           especially from private stakeholders, to support digital economy initiatives. The World Bank is currently providing
           technical assistance to the MOESD as it scopes out and considers the elements of such as digital strategy.17

           Development Objective

           D. Project Description

           17.      Through a combination of IBRD funding and counterpart financing, the proposed Project will support
           activities that will increase access to and realization of digital opportunity across Georgia, with a focus on rural
           areas. The Government has requested that the proposed Project include retroactive financing for eligible
           expenditures to accelerate Project implementation.

           Component 1: Increasing access to broadband

           18.      This Component will help expand access to broadband internet in rural settlements across Georgia and
           improve the enabling environment for digital development. Subcomponent 1.1 will support the Government’s
           Open Net Program, which seeks to expand access to broadband services to selected rural settlements.
           Subcomponent 1.2 will finance activities to enhance the enabling environmental for Georgia’s digital
           infrastructure development.

           Component 2: Promoting the use of broadband-enabled digital services

           19.      This Component will support the development of Georgia’s digital economy through a strengthened
           enabling environment, promoting digital use-cases of broadband, and addressing barriers to the participation of
           individuals in the digital economy. Subcomponent 2.1 will strengthen the enabling environment to develop
           Georgia’s digital economy. Subcomponent 2.2 will identify and promote broadband-enabled digital use-cases to
           help solve meaningful problems in the connected settlements and for specific user-groups (women-headed
           households, ethnic minorities, and persons with disabilities). Subcomponent 2.3 will support activities that ensure
           the digital inclusion of specific groups of individuals that are at risk of missing out on digital opportunities.

           Component 3: Project implementation support

           20.      This component will support the management and implementation of the Project and associated
           activities, including capacity building of the implementing entity and agencies. This would include hiring of
           consultants needed for key areas including for project management, technical input, procurement, financial
           management, environment and social framework implementation, monitoring and evaluation, communications,
           and citizen and stakeholder engagement, to enable Project implementation.

  Summary of Assessment of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
  .


           21.      The Environmental and Social Risk Classification (ESRC) for the Project is Moderate. Civil works to be
           undertaken under the Project are related to the development of the broadband infrastructure, and specifically,
           deployment of OFC. Overall, OFC corridor will overlap or closely follow the existing linear infrastructure and will
           not have considerable additional environmental footprint. In any case, possible impacts will be confined to the
           Project sites and be typical for the construction and operation of small to medium-size infrastructure. Small and
           medium-scale civil works could have some impacts on people and communities, but these are expected to be
           limited in scope and time, predictable, and possible to manage during the Project. Social impacts would mostly
           be positive and will benefit communities who have so far not benefitted fully from affordable and reliable
           broadband coverage in their community. At the same time, the implementing entities have limited knowledge of
           World Bank environmental and social standards, and no prior track record of working with international finance
           institutions.

           E. Implementation

           Institutional and Implementation Arrangements

           22.      The Project will be implemented by Open Net NNLE over a five-year period, with the oversight of the
           MOESD. Open Net NNLE, chartered by GITA for the purpose of implementing the ON Program, will be the Project
           implementing entity (PIE) for the Project. It will jointly implement with MOESD and with ComCom for various
           parts of the Project. The MOESD Deputy Minister (responsible to oversee the ICT sector) will play a strategic role
           in ensuring the Project activities are implemented in a timely manner as well as supporting the overall
           achievement of Project outcomes. The MOESD Deputy Minister will also chair the proposed Project Coordination
           Group (PCG) that will coordinate project implementation across the implementing agencies.

           23.      Ensuring strong collaboration and transparency across implementation agencies is seen as critical for
           the successful implementation of the Project. These arrangements would be governed by the guidelines and
           procedures set out in a Project Operational Manual (POM) and applied through a Subsidiary Agreement between
           the MOF, MOESD, Open Net NNLE, and ComCom. The POM will be developed and adopted by Open Net NNLE
           with inputs from the other implementing agencies. The POM will reflect the financial management, procurement,
           ESF management, monitoring and evaluation, and reporting arrangements planned for the Project. It will include
           details on the implementation and action plans and approaches for Project activities. The Subsidiary Agreement
           will detail the relationships, roles, and responsibilities of the various agencies participating, implementing, and
           involved in the Project. Importantly, the POM will specify the exact roles of Open Net NNLE vis-à-vis the activities
           jointly implemented by ComCom, to avoid any perception of either organization influencing the outcomes of
           activities of the other.
