    I. PROJECT STATUS AND RATIONALE FOR RESTRUCTURING

    A. PROGRAM STATUS

1.       Implementation progress. Overall, the Program has made good progress with implementation of the policy actions
and other operational and technical measures to improve the financial viability, increase the reliability of electricity supply,
and strengthen corporate governance of the energy sector. Key areas of recent progress include (a) weighted average
end-user electricity tariffs increased by 64 percent since the Program launched in 2020; (b) restructuring of utility debt
through the amendment of 27 subsidiary agreements between the Ministry of Finance (MOF) and Barqi Tojik Open Stock
Holding Company (BT) which converted approximately TJS 13.5 billion (approximately US$1.24 billion) from debt to equity
on BT’s balance sheet1; (c) renegotiation of BTs commercial debts to reduce the interest rates from 23 percent to 20
percent in 2021 and to 14 percent (prevailing market terms) in 2023; and (d) launch of an escrow account as a cash
distribution mechanism of cash collected from electricity sales domestically to the three electricity utilities (BT, Shabakahoi
Intiqoli Barq – the national electricity transmission company (SIB), Shabakahoi Taqsimoti Barq – the national electricity
distribution company (STB)); and (e) improved governance and transparency through the establishment of the supervisory
boards (SBs) and specialized committees (audit and compensation committees) for each utility, disclosure of audited
financial statements for all three companies for 2021 and 2022, and the disclosure of key financial, commercial and
operational data for all three companies 2021-23.

2.       Despite this progress, the sector remains far from financial recovery levels (currently approximately 50 percent
cost recovery), as described in more detail below. This is driven in part by the macroeconomic impact of COVID-19 and
the spillovers from Russia’s invasion to Ukraine, but also by tariffs which continue to be below cost recovery, high levels
of aggregate technical, commercial and collection (ATC&C) losses, and high levels of debt. Further governance and
transparency improvements are needed. While the escrow account has been established, it is not fully functioning as per
the requirements of the escrow account rules, and while the SBs have been established in each company, they have not
met with adequate frequency and independent board members are yet to be appointed.

3.       Implementation of the Program Action Plan (PAP). As of December 2024, 9 out of 17 items of the PAP have been
completed, 4 are in progress because they require continuous implementation throughout the Program, and 4 are
delayed. Major activities from the PAP that have been completed include (i) hiring of internal auditors; (ii) revision of
subsidiary agreements between MOF and BT; (iii) hiring of environmental experts and consultants and development of
corporate environmental policies and standards for effective environmental management performance at the three
entities; (iv) preparation of capacity building and training plan. While the tariff unit at the Anti-Monopoly-Service (AMS)
has been established, it still requires external support to fulfill its long-term function as independent regulator. The
European Union (EU) recently launched a 4-year technical assistance program that, among other aspects, will provide
support to AMS to transition to a fully independent regulator. However, there are multiple areas where there has been
limited progress including procurement related activities, corporate environmental policy with key guidelines, and
launching of a Targeted Social Assistance (TSA) Program based on a poverty impacts assessment of planned annual tariff
increases.

4.      Overall Project Development Objective (PDO) and Implementation Progress (IP) ratings: as described below, the
financial performance of the sector has not improved despite successful implementation of several key activities under
the Program. Considering several external shocks that have impacted the sector and which are outside the control of the

5.       Disbursement under the Program: As of December 5, 2024, 22 out of the 96 disbursement-linked results (DLRs)

not been fully achieved2.

6.       Sector financial performance: Despite substantial reforms in recent years, the financial performance of the
electricity sector has worsened, marred by low tariffs, low collections from strategic customers such as Talco and water
pumping stations, high levels of transmission and distribution losses, lack of cost-controls, and burgeoning debt service
obligations and trade payables. Analyses of financial historical results show that BT is illiquid, is burdened by
unmanageably high liabilities and cannot meet expenses with current cash flows leading to burgeoning past dues (trade
payables and debt service). The compounded effect of years of below cost-recovery tariffs, past dues (the “stock”) reached
US$ 1.9 billion in 2023. BTs financial performance is further hampered by a lack of adequate cost controls including with
respect to inventory management and procurement practices. STB and SIB financials also indicated liquidity concerns and
constraints on required capex. While the Management Contractor at STB has delivered notable improvements in the
financial performance of the distribution segment, its efforts are hampered by ineffective collections from large strategic
state-owned entities including Talco, water pumping stations, and government organizations which combined represent
more than thirty percent of energy sales in the country. External shocks have also impacted the financial standing of
electricity sector. Revenues were substantially below forecast in 2020 and 2021 largely due to COVID-19 impacts leading
to lower electricity consumption as well as no tariff increases during the pandemic but recovered in 2022 and 2023 close
to original forecasts (Figure 1(a)). A larger than projected depreciation of the TJS caused by the macroeconomic impact of
COVID-19, and the spillovers from Russia’s invasion to Ukraine, which caused substantial foreign exchange impacts on
payments to the IPPs Sangtuda 1 and 2 are both made in US dollars as well as commercial debt payments, led to a sharp
increase in debt and liabilities in 2020-23, much more than expected (Figure 1(b)). MOF also continues to impose penalties
on BT due to not servicing debts, despite the fact that end-user tariffs remain significantly below cost recovery levels.

7.        As a result, the cash deficit incurred in the electricity sector has worsened and is estimated to be over US$1.9
billion in 20233 (TJS 21.3 billion, a significant increase on the cash deficit in 2021 estimated at TJS 18.5 billion, and above
the target of TJS 14 billion in DLR 4.3). Figure 2 provides a breakdown of the cash deficit in 2023 (the most recent year for
which audited financial statements are available for the three companies). In 2023, obligatory expenses, including
operating costs (wages and fuel), taxes and interest expenses, exceeded cash revenues, leaving no room to service accrued
liabilities including BT’s regulated combined heating and electricity plant in the capital Dushanbe is estimated to have cost
the company around US$50 million in 2023 and faces particularly disadvantageous tariffs estimated to be less than 5
percent cost recovery. In turn, debts have continued to rise. At the end of 2023, BT owed the Rogun OJSC more than
US$66 million for electricity supplied, and owed over US$600 million to Sangtuda 1 & 2. A more detailed analysis of
historical financial performance since the sector unbundling is provided in Annex 3.

    B. RATIONALE FOR RESTRUCTURING

8.      Accelerated transition to cost recovery by 2027 is urgently needed to underpin the financial viability of Rogun
Hydropower Plant (HPP). In the context of Rogun HPP, the Government has decided to accelerate the path to achieving
financial viability in the energy sector, from 2031 to 2027. The Government program has been updated to reflect this
accelerated timeline and was approved on March 30, 2024. A financially viable electricity sector is critical for the viability
of the Rogun HPP project (so that the project can service debt associated with Rogun HPP).

9.       The restructuring is required to enhance the focus of the Program. The current Program design resulted from a
previous expansion of the Program through an Additional Financing in 2022, which added two new DLIs on cost reduction
(DLI 10) and the introduction of the escrow account mechanism for cash distribution (DLI 11), and dropped DLIs 4 and 6.
While this led to a comprehensive design (consisting of 9 DLIs and 96 DLRs), all DLRs are rigid in their design being time
bound. The added complexity has also reduced the effectiveness of the Program, making it difficult to monitor progress
(since there has often been long lags between achievement and disbursement due to the lengthy verification process5)
and advance implementation, required frequent amendments to the financing agreement as deadlines are often missed,
and created a lack of flexibility for disbursement against partial achievement of DLRs. Furthermore, disbursements under
the AF are linked to full achievement of DLRs 11.1 and 11.2, thus representing an implementation bottleneck against the
achieved DLRs.

10.     The restructuring will reduce the number of DLIs to focus the Program on critical results with improved outcome
orientation, and with simpler verification and disbursement procedures. Key changes include:
    (a) Re-balancing of DLR disbursements. The revised Program will place a strong focus on DLI 1 (achievement of cost
        recovery tariffs by 2027 vs 2031), by increasing the total allocation for DLI 1 from US$25.1 million to US$56.3
        million, and a strong focus on cost reduction by increasing the allocation to DLI 10 from US18.6 million to US$ 27.5
        million. DLI 9 on improvement of financial transparency through the publication of key results will also increase
        from US$5.8 million to US$14.3 million. Overall, this rebalancing allows the Program to focus the incentives on
        addressing/resolving the key challenges to ensure timely achievement of the financial recovery of the sector,
        namely through cost-reflective tariffs, cost-effectiveness and good corporate governance.
    (b) Fewer DLIs. As the Program focuses on a smaller set of DLIs, the following DLIs will be dropped while the subject
        areas will remain in the broader Program framework:
             (i) DLI 3 (sector cash deficit): will be dropped (see next paragraph).
             (ii) DLI 7 (investment decision making following sound principles): will be dropped as a DLI and added as a
                  new action in the Program Action Plan, and publication of the Generation Expansion Plan (GEP) and
                  transmission masterplan will be moved as results to DLI 9 “Improved transparency”.
    (c) Fewer DLRs. DLI 8 will be simplified to reduce the number of DLRs from 11 to 3. The three new DLRs will separate
        the establishment of the SBs of the three utilities (DLR 8.1) from the requirement of having independent members
        that chair sub-committees (to become DLR 8.2) and later the entire SB (to become DLR 8.3). This is because the
        boards have already been established but the current structure still lacks independent directors, which may take
        some time to identify and attract, yet it is important to recognize the progress that has been made. The new DLRs
        under DLI 8 will focus on making the SBs more effective through the recruitment of independent directors who
        will help bring good international practice such as the approval of annual reports following good practice
        guidelines of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD). DLI 9 on publishing core
        operational and financial data will be simplified to reduce the number of DLRs from 11 to 5, with a new more

        tariff increase required to reach cost-recovery levels is high and hence continuous incremental annual increases
        are needed to achieve the objective of cost-recovery by 2027.

11.       Recognition of the limitations of the Program in addressing collection risks and the stock of liabilities. One key
aspect of the PDO relates to improving the financial viability of the energy sector entities. There are many contributing
factors to achieving sector financial viability, some of which are supported through the Program (cost-reflective tariffs,
some restructuring of debts, and cost reductions etc.). However, the Program does not address (i) collection risks,
particularly from large customers which are protected by the Government (such as Talco and water pumping stations);
and (ii) the stock of liabilities that have built up from years of underpricing (including trade payables such as Sangtuda 1
and 2). The updated financial analysis for the sector (Annex 3) demonstrates the critical importance of addressing
collections and the stock of liabilities. However, addressing those critical issues is outside the scope of the PforR and better
addressed through other instruments including the Sustainable Financing for Rogun HPP Project (P181029) and the DPO
series, both of which were launched since the PforR was approved. The DLI on cash deficit will be dropped accordingly,
and the intermediate indicator related to achieving cost recovery tariffs will replace the cash deficit PDO indictor. The
PDOs of the Program are achievable by the new closing date.

12.     Recognition of SIB and STB: SIB and STB play a key role in the success of several parts of the Program but to date
have not been implementing agencies or beneficiaries of the Program. To recognize their role, SIB and STB will, therefore,
be added as beneficiaries and receive disbursement under DLIs where they have a role to play, though the majority of
Program funds will remain with BT given its relatively weaker financial position. The fiduciary systems assessment of the
Program has been updated accordingly to take SIB and STB into account. Since the scope and boundaries of the Program
remain unchanged, the technical assessment and environmental and social assessments remain unchanged. While the
scope and boundaries of the Program have not changed, the Program Expenditure Framework will be amended to
recognize which investments are now being made by SIB and STB (rather than with BT prior to unbundling). Investments
in the transmission and distribution network are expected to improve service quality through reduction of network
outages, and to reduce aggregate technical, commercial and collection losses. The Program boundaries and expenditure
framework will be amended accordingly to reflect the role of SIB and STB.

The Program Action Plan will be updated to reflect the latest developments in the sector and ensure advancement of
the restructured Program towards meeting PDO. The deadline of several pending actions will be extended. New actions
are added on improved governance aspects e.g. establishment of a performance contract between BT and MOF with
establishment of the key performance indicators (KPI) for BT management, the objectives of former DLI 7 (MEWR, BT,
SIB, STB investment decisions) are added as new items to the PAP; preparation of a debt servicing plan for BT is added to
develop a credible plan for servicing BTs stock of debt. An audit of BTs inventory is added – identified as a line item with
potentially excessive operating costs - to inform DLI 10 on cost cutting measures.

13.      Several gender related actions are added to reflect the Program’s focus on promoting women into managerial
and technical positions. These are aligned with the Gender Action Plan developed by the ADB-financed Power Sector
Development Program (RRP TAJ 53315-001) to ensure collaboration and synergies across IFIs. These actions should
collectively lead to an increase in the number of women employed at BT, STB and SIB.

14.     The Results Framework will be updated to reflect the changes to the Program. The timing for results related to
achieving financial viability in the electricity sector will be advanced to 2027 to align with the Government’s accelerated
target. The PDO indicator related to investment decision making will be changed to an indicator that measures the
effectiveness in SBs taking decisions, to underline the importance of this process and to introduce a more objectively
measurable indicator. At the intermediate outcome level, the indicator on supervisory boards will be refined to use a
more concrete definition of board functionality to assure precise measurement. Thirdly, to reflect the Programs
engagement on gender, a target related to the share of women employed in management and technical roles at BT, SIB
and STB will be added.

15.     Legal covenant will be updated to reflect the inclusion of SIB and STB. The creation of STB and SIB as part of the
sector unbundling means that the financial recovery of the sector is no longer solely dependent on BT, but also on the
financial stability of STB and SIB. To assure that the debt service of both companies remains within the debt servicing
capabilities of each company, the legal covenant related to new debt will be amended to apply to SIB and STB.

16.     Closing dates will be adjusted to reflect the 2027 target for cost recovery tariffs. The closing date of the parent
project financing is proposed to be extended by 16 months - from August 30, 2026 to December 31, 2027 - to align with
the target of achieving cost recovery tariffs by 2027, while the closing date for the AF grant will be advanced by eight
months - from August 31, 2032 to December 31, 2031 - to be consistent with the timing of the Government program.

17.      In this context, MoF has submitted a request dated March 18, 2024, to the World Bank to restructure the
Program. The restructuring package represented in this restructuring paper was discussed extensively with the MoF and
project implementing entities prior to finalization.

18.     The restructuring of the Program includes the following: (a) change in implementing agency and institutional
arrangements; (b) changes in the Program Scope; (c) change in Results Framework; (d) reallocation between and/or
Change in DLI; (e) change in disbursement arrangements; (f) change in Disbursement Estimates; (g) change in closing
dates; (h) change in Systematic Operations Risk-Rating Tool; (i) change in Institutional Arrangements; (j) change in
fiduciary assessment; and (k) change in Program Action Plan;

19.      Change in Implementing Agency and Institutional Arrangements: SIB and STB are added as implementing
agencies. Program Agreements will be established with SIB and STB, complementing the implementation agreements
that already exist between the entities.

20.      Changes in the Program Scope. The Program boundaries are revised (i) reduction of sector costs moved from
Results Area 2 to Results Area 1; (ii) improved transparency on collection and distribiution of revenues moved to Results
Area 3; and (iii) invetsment decision making dropped since the corresponding DLI is dropped. The expenditure framework
is also updated to reflect the role of SIB and STB (Table 1). Note the expenditure framework remains the same as before
– the only change it has now are expenditures under SIB and STB.

Financial analysis of the revised Program

33.     Annex 3 provides detailed forecasts of financial performance (for the sector and by company), using an
updated financial model of the electricity sector to model the impacts of the actions under the government
program. Various scenarios are analyzed. The revised financial projections for the sector confirm that business as
usual will result in continued substantial deterioration of the financial performance of the secor. Moreover, there
is no single bullet solution. Establishing financial viability in the sector reqauires implementation of a
comprehensive package of reforms that will require careful planning, strong political will, and close monitoring to
effectively implement.

34.      The distinction between “stock” of liabilities (past due debt payments and trade payables such as Sangtuda
1 and 2) and expenditure flows is key when analyzing the sector’s financial performance. As mentioned above,
after years of underpricing and accumulated losses, the stock of past due liabilities is estimated at US$1.9 billion
in 2023 (including over US$700 million owed to IPPs). Scenario 2 shows that with annual increases of end-user
tariffs of 10 to 20 percent in nominal terms (as proposed through DLI 1), cost-cutting measures through reduced
operating costs and improved inventory management, and reduced ATC&C losses, the sector can cover the flow
of current expenses by 2027 (scenario 2). However, addressing stock of past due liabilities would require more
substantial tariff increases in the short-term which may not be affordable for consumers. Thus, more serious
actions will be necessary, which are presented through scenarios 3-5.

35.     The full financial viability scenario (scenario 5) includes additional measures included in the government
program and supported through the restructured Program (e.g. additional restructuring of the subsidiary
agreements between BT and the MOF (as proposed through the new DLR 2.10), more aggressive cost reductions
(as proposed through DLI 10). As discussed above, other actions in the Government program are supported
through other instruments (e.g. collection risks are supproted through the forthcoming DPO series, and through
the proposed Rogun financing project), and measures are supproted by other development partners e.g.
restructuring of the past due trade payables (negotiations ongoing).

                                               ANNEX 3: DETAILED FINANCIAL ANALYSIS

The objective of this annex is to provide a high-level financial analysis of each of BT, STB and SIB focused on a) historical
financial analysis including the evolution of their financial situation during the first 2 years of the sector unbundling and
the impact of recent tariff increases; and b) financial outlook and viability, i.e. ability to be financially sustainable on a
stand-alone basis.

The analysis demonstrated that the power sector in Tajikistan is facing several major issues which impede its financial
viability:
1. High level of indebtedness. A sector with annual sales of approximately US$430 million cannot support its operating
    costs plus the debt service associated with US$2.1 billion of debt and penalties for payment default.
2. Low tariffs. Consumer tariffs remain below cost-recovery. Tariff increases need to be complemented with cost
    reductions and improved efficiency.
3. Low collections. Higher tariffs become ineffective when customers do not pay for the electricity service. Instead, a
    tariff increase turns into a cross subsidy from paying customers (mostly residential and commercial) to defaulting
    customers, much like a tax on all electricity consumers.


Updated corporate structure for the sector

In 2021, the corporate structure of the electricity sector in Tajikistan changed from vertically integrated (with BT as the
single company) to unbundled into generation (BT), transmission (SIB) and distribution (STB), with BT as parent company
of the sector. In March of 2022, the corporate structure changed again to eliminate BT’s ownership in SIB and STB. All
Companies remain 100 percent owned by the Government.

Furthermore, in 2023 the sector began to apply an Escrow Account mechanism pursuant to which all domestic sector
revenues (proceeds from STB’s sales) are managed by an Agent which in turn allocates them to each of the Companies
according to specific/regulated percentages, for the Companies to apply according to specific/regulated priorities.

Consequently, there is no track record of the Companies’ operational and financial performance under the current
corporate structure and sector cash management system, with FY2023 being the first full year of operation/financial
reporting under the new structure, with all the challenges attached to it.

Historical financial analysis

The following financial analysis of BT, SIB and STB was performed on the basis of the companies’ audited financial
statements for FYE 2022 (restated) and 20236. All the information displayed in the summary tables was extracted from
the financial reports delivered by each of the Companies.

refers mostly to major line items.

Income statement
BT’s Revenues for 2023 increased by 12 percent when compared to 2022, reaching TJS 3.7 billion (US$334.8 million7)
(Table A3.1). This increase was mostly driven by higher volumes of electricity sold by BT to STB (17,512,801 MWh in 2022
vs 18,872,578 MWh in 2023).

Nonetheless, just as in FY2022, BT’s Revenues were insufficient to cover its Cost of Sales, let alone Opex and Non-
Operating Expenses, such as financial expenses. BT’s Cost of Sales increased by 9 percent YoY reaching TJS 3.8 billion
(US$344.5 million) - well above the Company’s Revenues - resulting in a Gross Loss of TJS 85 million (US$7.8 million), while
Operating Expenses decreased by 85 percent to TJS 60.9 million (US$5.6 million) mostly due to a TJS 157 million (US$14.3
million) reduction in taxes. As a result, EBITDA8 improved from a negative TJS 146.4 million (US$13.3 million) in FY2022
to a positive TJS 384.7 million (US$ 35 million) in 2023.

Accrued Financial Expenses amounted to TJS 2 billion (US$183.8 million) in FY 2023, which together with a Net Loss on
forex operations of TJS1.65 billion (US$ 150.7 million), led to a Net Operating Loss of TJS 3.7 billion (US$341.2 million), a
sharp and negative contrast with the Net Operating Income of TJS 61.4 million (US$5.6 million) of FY2022.

BT’s Balance Sheet shows values that evidence a further deterioration of an already critical situation:
(i)      BT is far from having the liquidity required to pay its short-term liabilities. Cash at the end of 2023 amounted to

(ii)    BT was unable to pay most of its financial obligations during FY2023. BT did not pay any of the Debt Service due

(iv)    Payments yet to be received from customers are not sufficient to cover payments due to BT’s suppliers. Although
        Trade Receivables reduced from TJS 604.7 million (US$55 million) in 2022 to TJS 451 million (US$41 million) in

        reflecting the negative performance during the year and reinforcing the need for a thorough financial
        restructuring of the Company. BT is essentially bankrupt.

The following financial ratios further illustrate the BT’s critical situation:

Conclusions on BT financial performance
BT’s financial situation worsened significantly during FY2023. Although the Company’s Sales increased, revenues were
insufficient to cover operating expenses. Furthermore, an inefficient use of cash resulted in the Company having to
increase the amount due for Trade Payables (i.e. BT failed to pay suppliers in order to pay debt service) and an increase in

which jeopardizes access to suppliers and a dislocated business cycle, and (iv) financial obligations that evidence an
indebtedness level far beyond the Company’s current payment capacity. A comprehensive restructuring of BT’s liabilities
portfolio (stock of debt, and past due trade payables) is a critical priority for the sector to achieve financial sustainability.

Financial Projections

The solutions to the issues described under the historical financial analysis above require a comprehensive program of
activities that includes achieving cost reflective tariffs, significant cost reductions, debt restructuring, restrictions on new
debt and a structural solution to invoice collection from Government owned/related consumers. Those solutions are
reflected in a financial model with the financial projections of each of the Companies.

The financial model and the resulting financial projections summarized in this report were prepared on the basis of:
    • Audited Financial Statements of each of the Companies for FYs 2021, 2022 and 2023 with their respective notes,
        and
    • A variety of reports and information related to BT’s generation assets, exports, sector demand, tariff projections
        and escrow account results, which are all cited as Sources in the relevant section of the model.

This section provides a summary of the financial outlook of BT, SIB, and STB under five different scenarios of performance,
which are intended to illustrate the actions and the magnitude of the changes required to achieve the financial viability of
the sector, and in particular, of BT.

Main Considerations & Assumptions
When analyzing the results, the following key considerations are important:

a) Capex requirements: are a key element to determining a company’s financial requirements and must be regarded as
   essential in any corporate financial projections. Detailed Capex plans were not available for any of the companies. The
   information in the model was extracted mostly from third party reports or from documents which included some
   short-term figures.

b) Receivables are a major area of concern for all the Companies. Despite the significant importance of this subject,
   formal plans or agreements to address the problem are not available. Scenario 2 assumes that STB’s collection rate
   starts at 89 percent in 2023 and increases by 2.5 percent annually to reach 99 percent in 2027.

c) Payables are another major area of concern for BT. Payables with electricity suppliers Sangtuda 1 & 2 and Rogun
   increased from TJS 6.2 billion (US$565 million) as at FYE2022 to TJS 7.3 billion (US$665 million) as at FYE2023. Formal
   plans to address the situation are not available. The financial model assumes that Past Due Payables are addressed
   with cash available after Debt Service (when/if there is any) therefore the “0” values for Cash Flow in some years.
   However, the current practice is to give payment priority to Debt Service related to EBRD and Orienbank loans above
   other payables; such arrangement will lead to serious operational problems for the Company which is now forced to
   postpone payment of invoices to suppliers of essential goods and services. In scenario 5, a restructuring of payables
   to Sangtuda 1 and 2 is modelled, in line with negotiations that are ongoing with each of them.

d) Financial Debt: as of the end of FY2023, the electricity sector in Tajikistan accrued financial debt in an amount
   equivalent to US$1.75 billion, 97.6% of which is owned/booked by BT, while the remaining 2.4% is owned by STB. The
   financial debt owned by BT was acquired prior to the sector unbundling and was applied to build, upgrade, etc.
   generation, transmission and distribution assets required to provide electricity services. As part of the unbundling,

     BT’s assets were distributed amongst the 3 companies, however the debt attached to them was not. Consequently,
     the fundamental financial principle whereby the debt acquired to build an asset should be repaid with the revenues
     produced by such asset, is not being applied in the sector, therefore creating a substantial financial imbalance amongst
     the companies, and a distortion of their individual financial reality. Table A3.11 provides a Summary of the Companies’

e) Escrow Account: The Escrow Account report for 2023 (when the account was launched) does not match the cash
   inflows reported in the Cash Flow Statements of the Companies. The model includes the cash transfer figures provided

   resulting from Escrow transfers. This is a critical issue which must be addressed as a matter of urgency.

f)   Tariffs: are one of the main drivers for the calculation of the various scenarios of performance in the financial model.

     Given that the tariff projection in the ETP is limited to the period 2023-2027, for purposes of the financial projections,
     and in order to preserve the principle of cost recovery (cost & expenses are assumed to increase annually by inflation),
     a basic inflation adjustment is applied to the tariff from 2028 onwards.

Scenario 5 – Financial Viability

This scenario identifies all the additional actions required for the sector to achieve financial viability (defined as achieving
positive cumulative cash deficit in the sector) by 2027. Additional measures factored into scenario five compared to
scenario four include:

    a) More aggressive cost reductions: the analysis of the historical financial statements indicates that there are several
       areas where the Companies have ample room for optimization. These measures are reflected in the revised DLR
       10.
    b) Inventory turnover optimization: historical figures indicate that the Companies are trapping large amounts of cash
       purchasing Inventory which does not rotate and instead is subject to major write downs (approximately 30%)
       every year. Consequently, the model includes parameters which reflect industry practice, consider local market
       reality and represent a significant release of cash for other purposes. These measures are reflected in the revised
       DLR 10.
    c) VAT over uncollected invoices: the model does not assume payment of VAT over uncollected invoices, which
       brings a cash benefit for STB.
    d) Taxation over all electricity purchased by STB, including losses: the current taxation practice results in financial
       loss for STB as they do not invoice customers for such electricity. Following the decree expected to be issued by
       the Government, the model does not tax technical losses, thus bringing a financial benefit to STB.
    e) Provision for Bad Debt: as discussed in various meetings with the Companies, this provision is currently equivalent
       to nearly 100% of the Receivables at year end without differentiating between current Receivables and those
       which are truly in arrears. This issue creates an unnecessary additional cost in the P&L which distorts the
       Companies’ results. The financial model is applying a 4% provision which better reflects the expected collection
       & bad debt for the sector.
    f) Recovery of Past Due Receivables by STB: the model assumes payment of arrears by TALCO and by Government
       organizations between 2025 and 2027. It also assumes that these entities will not accumulate new arrears and
       that they will pay their invoices when and as due.
    g) Write-off or freezing of all arrears with the MoF (for principal, interest and penalties) and with the Tax authority
       (penalties): these arrears which amounted to over TJS 19 billion as at FYE 2023. Arrears for interest and penalties
       with the MoF are written off as well as penalties with the Tax Authority are written off. Principal in arrears with
       the MoF is frozen throughout 2028 and paid from that year onwards according to the cash availability of the
       Companies.
    h) Payment of Debt Service with MoF: from 2025 all current Debt Service with the MoF will be paid on time.
    i) Payment of Receivables with suppliers: from 2025 all current payables with suppliers will be paid on time, while
       arrears are being amortized.
    j) No additional debt: although it can be anticipated that the Companies will invest in maintenance and growth
       Capex as their financial situation improves, and that those investments will be partially financed with new debt,
       the model does not include any additional debt due to lack of reliable long-term data.

Conclusions

The basic revenue/cost structure of the Companies, which can be seen in the summary tables, demonstrates a critical flaw
in the current financial fundamentals of the sector. Simply put, except for SIB, revenues are not enough to cover all the
costs and expenses of the Companies as they are now. In the case of SIB, they are sufficient with a narrow margin.

Several cost items merit detailed review (particularly inventory management, materials and services). In addition, taxes,
debt service and penalties (tax & debt-related), should be the focus of the analysis, if only, because they represent
obligations with the Government which is in turn the owner of the Companies.

The proportion of Total Debt to Total Sales provides evidence regarding the most important problem currently affecting
the sector i.e. over indebtedness, while the quantification of the tariff deficit per kWh demonstrates the impossibility of
addressing the problem solely through a tariff increase.

The main conclusion of the financial modelling exercise, which is summarized in Figure 1, is that the only scenario under
which the sector and in particular BT can achieve financial viability, understood as a situation where the Companies attain
a positive cash situation, is that in which various critical actions are taken simultaneously, to align the cash generation
capacity of the sector with its cash requirements.

Scenario 5 demonstrates that the way to achieve financial viability by 2027 is to relieve the sector from a substantial
amount of payment obligations. Consequently, the sector requires a comprehensive financial restructuring which must
include the following basic elements: i) consider re- allocation of financial debt across the Companies according to their
respective assets; ii) debt restructuring, to include partial forbearance, extended grace periods, renegotiation of
amortization schedules, etc. all intended to match the payment capacity of each of the Companies; iii) improvement of
invoice collection from government-related entities; (iv) elimination of tax and debt-related penalties; (v) recalculation of
projected tariffs to include all elements of cost of service, and (vi) a thorough review and rationalization of all costs &
expenses per Company.
