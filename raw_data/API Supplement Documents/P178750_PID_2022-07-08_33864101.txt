     B. Introduction and Context

     Country Context

 1.      The robust economic performance in Senegal pre-COVID-19 has failed to translate into substantive
 improvements of living standards or reduced inequality. Over the 2016-2019 period, Senegal’s average real Gross
 Domestic Product (GDP) growth reached 6.1 percent, outperforming the West African Economic and Monetary Union
 (Union Economique et Monétaire Ouest Africaine, UEMOA) by 0.7 percentage points (p.p.) and the Sub-Saharan Africa
 (SSA) average by 3.3 p.p. However, despite strong growth, poverty reduction remained modest, while the share of
 consumption of the bottom 40 percent of the population contracted during the same period, and the Gini coefficient
 stagnated at 0.35. New estimates indicate that poverty declined from 43 percent in 2011 to 37.8 percent in 2018-20191.
 With an average 1 p.p. reduction per year, Senegal’s track record falls short of the one achieved by the 15-best performing
 SSA countries, which saw their poverty rates decline by 1 to 3.4 p.p. per year2 . In terms of gender inequalities, the Gender
 Development Index (GDI) of Senegal, is 0.870, placing the country below the Sub-Saharan Africa average3. For the Gender
 Inequalities Index4 the country ranks 130 out of 162 due to lower educational attainment (with 10.3 percent of adult
 women having reached at least a secondary level of education compared to 26.5 percent of men), high prevalence of
 adolescent birth rates and lower female participation in the labor market (35.0 percent compared to 57.5 for men), among
 others.

 2.      The Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) outbreak halted years of strong economic performance. The combination
 of a COVID-19 induced global recession, disruptions in supply chains, and containment measures has taken a heavy toll on
 the economy. As a result, growth dropped from 4.4 percent in 2019 to 1.5 percent in 2020. This first recession since 1990
 was driven by weak activity in the service sector, particularly real estate, the hospitality and tourism industry, and
 information and communications technology. Inflation also increased from 1 percent in 2019 to 2.5 percent in 2020 due
 to higher transport and food prices stemming from confinement measures and supply chain disruptions. The social
 protection system is well established but is not yet leveraged as a platform to boost the equity and efficiency of shock
 responses and social spending across sectors. Further, low labor productivity is a significant barrier to equitable growth
 and inclusive structural transformation.

 3.      The Government of Senegal (GoS) has engaged in numerous efforts measures to mitigate socio-economic
 impacts of the pandemic and return to the initial growth trajectory facilitating an inclusive and resilient recovery. On
 top of containment measures, the GoS has put in place a comprehensive support and recovery plan, the Economic and
 Social Resilience Program (Programme de resilience économique et sociale – PRES), underpinned by a dedicated Solidarity
 Fund at the Central Bank of Western African States – FORCE-COVID-19. The main objectives of PRES are to upgrade the
 health system and mitigate the economic fallout, while providing targeted support to vulnerable households and firms. In
 particular, it allowed the GoS to pay for the electricity bills of the most vulnerable households of Senegal (975,000)

  Sectoral and Institutional Context

 4.      Senegal has one of the oldest higher education systems in Africa, with a well-established university culture that
 goes back more than a century. Created in 1957, the University of Cheikh Anta Diop (UCAD) of Dakar was the 18th French
 university while institutes and faculties that were bundled into the new university were established much earlier. Such is
 the case for the Ecole africaine de Médecine created in 1918 or the Institut Français d’Afrique noire created in 1936. Like
 Makerere University in Kampala created in 1922 or the University of Ibadan in Nigeria and the University of Ghana created
 both in 1948 that served neighboring English-speaking countries, the UCAD served the whole of French-speaking West
 African countries. The UCAD continues to attract, albeit on a smaller scale as countries have established their national
 universities, students, and academics from the entire region. To date, the UCAD remains by far the largest university with
 about 60,000 students.

 5.       Senegal’s labor market signals higher returns and better employment opportunities for higher level of skills.
 Over the last decades, university graduates have had lower unemployment rates, higher wage differentials and higher
 lifetime earnings than other categories of workers. The latest Public Expenditure Review (PER, 2021, Report No.
 AUS0002365) shows that, at the national level, the rate of return ranges from 25 percent for individuals with some primary
 education to 170 percent for individuals with higher education, relative to individuals with no education. Higher education
 is also the level that leads to remarkably high returns for female (170 percent). The average estimated earnings increases
 from about US$125 for the working age population with no formal education to about US$532 for those with some post-
 secondary education level which is more than four times of the income of an individual with no education.

 6.      Higher level of education is not only associated with higher wage earnings, but it also increases the likelihood
 of wage employment in sectors with higher returns and contracts. In Senegal, an additional year of education increases
 the probability of working in sectors with higher returns (industry and services) by 15 percent and 23 percent (PER, 2021).
 Additionally, the increase in the probability of finding wage employment ranges from 35 percent for individuals with some
 primary education to 321 percent for individuals with some post-secondary education, as compared to individuals with
 no education.

 7.       Due to a lack of capacity, tertiary education provision has diversified with an expanding private sector, the
 creation of public Tertiary Education Vocational Institutes (Institut Supérieur des Etudes Professionnelles, ISEP).
 Universities are for the most part autonomous institutions with the responsibility to hire staff, determine programs, design
 curricula, collect fees, select students, and manage budgets. There are approximately 90 private higher education
 institutions (HEIs) enrolling 27 percent of students mostly in accounting, business, tourism, communication, and ICT
 programs. Inadequate programs designed to equip the increasing numbers of unskilled youth for productive employment
 are a major constraint to the inclusiveness of growth of Senegal.

 Key challenges facing the tertiary education sub-sector

 8.      Unmet demand for tertiary education. Higher education is globally marked by considerable growth in student
 enrollment over the past two decades. This expansion is mainly attributed to an increased demand for higher levels of
 education and skills, which are essential for accessing the knowledge-based global economy. Following this global trend,
 enrollment in higher education in Senegal has also significantly increased over the past decade. Between 2010 and 2021,
 the number of students enrolled in public universities in Senegal increased at an average annual rate of 9 percent from
 66,373 to 170,748 students respectively. Women represent 48 percent of the total enrolment. Moreover, projections
 show that the gross enrollment rate (GER) in higher education will increase from 8 percent to 16 percent by 2030, and
 actual enrollment will more than double to over 422,037 students by 2030 (PER, 2021). The growth in student enrollment
 has serious financial implications on the public resources and the household contributions.

 9.        Mismatch between skills with high demand and the education provided by public universities. Skills constraints
 in all sectors of the economy are largely the product of insufficient education and training and/or low quality of education
 in Senegal. Supply chain analyses (GoS, 2009) for Senegal’s growth sectors project a continued strong demand for
 technical, managerial, and innovative skills in engineering, agro-industry, transportation, logistics, teaching, health care,
 ICT, marketing, entrepreneurship and finance--programs in short supply at universities where enrolment in arts and social
 sciences have boomed. At the same time, enrollments in science, applied science, technology and medicine have fallen
 during the past decade, while student demand for these programs exceeds places available. Only 17 percent of students
 are enrolled in science and technology programs, which is low when compared to 22 percent in SSA countries and 25
 percent in OECD countries. The ability of the labor market to absorb a growing labor force with tertiary education depends
 not only on economic and job growth but also on the type of skills made available to firms. The unemployment rate among
 the labor force with some higher education is significantly lower than for similar individuals with less education and a
 significant share of enterprises report skill shortages as an impediment to their business.

 10.      Low quality and relevance of tertiary education by all accounts. The expansion of tertiary education in Senegal
 was driven, in large part, by the need to respond to the growth in the demand for higher education emanating from the
 old tradition of automatic admission of all high school graduates. This uncontrolled growth in enrollments has resulted in
 a near exclusive focus on establishing physical infrastructure, with little attention paid to the most important elements of
 quality. The misallocation of resources and unchecked growth in enrollments, coupled with the lack of concomitant
 training of teaching staff, underfunding for academic inputs, curriculum content, and pedagogical methods have led to
 the deterioration in the quality of tertiary education provision and its disconnect from the evolution of knowledge and
 scientific practices.

 11.     Inefficiency of tertiary education spending and inequity. Senegal has invested a large share of public resources
 in education and in higher education in particular. In 2018, the Government had devoted a high share of its resources to
 its education sector, both in terms of GDP and in terms of total public spending, which was equivalent to 4.6 percent of
 the GDP and 21.5 percent of the total public expenditure. An increasing share of education expenditures (66 percent) has
 gone to higher education, which accounted for over a third of the education budget in 2018; the share allocated to primary
 and secondary hovers around 17 percent and 18 percent respectively. While the spending by level of education is
 proportional to the enrollment distribution in the respective level of education, most of the working-age population in
 Senegal has no formal education (58 percent) and a very small segment has post-basic education. At the tertiary level, the
 increase in spending is mainly driven by the increase in non-personnel recurrent spending that has more than doubled
 between 2010 and 2018. The non-personnel recurrent budget at the tertiary level increased from CFA 94 billion in 2010
 to CFA 175.6 billion in 2018 (PER, 2021). Non-personnel recurrent spending includes higher education scholarships
 spending with all incoming bacheliers provided with subsidies without any targeting of the neediest students.

 12.     Senegal spends significantly more on higher education, both as a share of GDP and as a share of total educational
 budget expenditure, than the average of Sub-Saharan African (SSA) countries. Further, they spend more on average than
 their structural and aspirational peers (such as Cote d’Ivoire, Ghana, and Kenya, Vietnam) . This underscores the
 government’s commitment to the sector but also points to potential inefficiencies in the system, as in the case of the
 scholarships noted above.

 13.      Although access to higher education remains limited, spending per student in Senegal is equivalent to 191 percent
 of GDP per capita. The high cost of a Senegalese student is explained less by educational expenses than by spending on
 scholarships and social works. The overall trend of public spending in education and particularly in tertiary education and
 scholarship provision, is pro-rich. Recent analysis of total public spending on education reveals that, overall, the poorest
 quintile (Q1) receives only 6 percent of the total education funds (14 percent less than its share of the population), while
 the richest quintile receives 53 percent of the total benefits (33 percent above its share of the population) (PER, 2021).
 The inequality in public spending is highest at the higher education level with 71 percent of resources devoted to students
 from the wealthiest households. At the higher education level, 86 percent of public spending went to students from
 households in the top two wealthiest quintiles.

 14.     The distribution of public spending on scholarships to new high school graduates (bacheliers) is more unequal
 than inequalities at the income distribution level. The distribution of public spending on university scholarships to new
 bacheliers is more regressive than income equality in general. Specifically, 20 percent of the higher education population
 uses more than 40 percent of university scholarships.

 15.      The awarding of scholarships is skewed toward fields outside of science, technology, engineering and mathematics
 related fields, which results in missed opportunities to incentive participation in these highly needed area for an emerging
 economy. Foreign scholarships have exploded, representing almost one-third of all scholarships or 13 percent of total
 higher education expenditures, including in programs readily available in Senegal. Only an estimated 5 percent of
 recipients of foreign scholarships have returned. While the government’s efforts to promote equity at the primary level
 are commendable and the scholarship policy is designed to promote equity, the regressive public spending pattern at the
 tertiary level underscores the importance of targeted programmatic actions to increase equity in higher education
 provision.

     Key sector achievements to build upon

 16.      The World Bank supported Senegal Tertiary Education Governance and Financing for Results Project (P123673,
 closed in 2018) has significantly strengthened the Government’s capacity to improve the management of the tertiary
 education system. The Project has supported relevant higher education governance bodies including: (i) the General
 Directorate of Higher Education which is in charge of overseeing the development of the higher education sector has been
 fully operationalized; (ii) the National Quality Assurance Authority (ANAQ) has been well established, and it has adopted
 international quality standards in teaching and research and evaluation criteria for higher education institutions (HEIs);
 and (iii) University Boards of Directors have been strengthened using incentives for better efficiency and university
 autonomy in public resources utilization through performance-based contracts (PBC). The Project has also established a
 new Tertiary Education and Vocational Institute in Thies to increase access to short-term tertiary education. Building on
 these key achievements, sustained investments are needed to address the key issues hampering the tertiary education
 sector for decades and to further lay solid foundations for a more resilient and competitive economy.

  Relationship to CPF

 17.     The World Bank Group’s Country Partnership Framework [(CPF) 2020 -2024, Report No. 14333-SN], identifies
 three focus areas for reducing extreme poverty and boosting shared prosperity: (i) build human capital to enhance
 productivity and ignite the demographic dividend; (ii) boost competitiveness and job creation through private sector-
 led growth; and (iii) increase resilience and sustainability in the context of growing risks. In addition, the CPF reflects
 Senegal’s strategic objective of becoming an equitable middle-income economy by 2035. The proposed project will
 contribute to the human capital focus area and may support efforts to boost competitiveness and job-creation. While all
 the sub-sectors of education and training are important, Higher education and TVET in particular can be powerful engines
 for economic restructuring and transformation.

 18.      The government's strategic Plan for an Emerging Senegal [(PES), 2012-2019, renewed 2019-2024], aim to
 reverse low economic growth by focusing on improvements in the business climate and the development of clusters of
 services and skills to raise the productivity and capacity of businesses and the labor force. The strong public-private
 collaboration lies at the core of development and maintenance of relevant skills training programs. A key success factor
 will be to support the development of a health, skilled and productive population that is well prepared to contribute to
 the growth process. The CPF is aligned with the government Plan (PES), particularly the three pillars which aim to :(i)
 transform the structure of the economy to support strong and sustainable growth; (ii) promote human capital
 development; and (iii) respond to the requirements of good governance.

 19.     The Bank's Systematic Country Diagnostic of Senegal - SCD (Report No. 130660, Dec. 2018) noted that, in the
 medium to long term, sustained economic growth and rising living standards can only be achieved through increases in
 productivity gained through strong human capital and innovation. The SCD is highly relevant to the current Country
 Partnership Framework (2020-2024), focus area 1, building human capital to enhance productivity and ignite the
 demographic dividend.

 20.     The project is aligned with the 2021-2025 Western and Central Africa Region (AFW) Strategy to Support a
 Resilient Recovery whose second and third targets are to: (i) increase access to education with a focus on girls; and (ii)
 expand access to job relevant TVET and higher education, thus strengthening human capital and women
 empowerment. In line with the AFW strategy, the project intends to strengthen the capabilities of workers to pursue new
 opportunities to access jobs and support the acquisition of job-relevant skills to prepare young men and women through
 vocational education and on-the-job training. Further, the project supports channels for educational institutions and the
 private sector to connect to ensure that students are acquiring the skills needed for jobs.

  D. Concept Description

 21.     In response to the Government’s request, the proposed project will support the ongoing and planned higher
 education reforms to enhance the capacity of HEIs in Senegal to produce high quality graduates with skills aligned to labor
 market needs. The Project will do so by improving the governance framework for higher education, while concurrently
 supporting the Government’s strategic policy options including: (i) expanding access to short-term tertiary vocational
 training programs; (ii) improving the quality and relevance of programs by strengthening quality assurance systems in
 place, performance requirements, and making courses more relevant to the labor market needs; (iii) strengthening
 internal and external efficiency; (iv) promoting greater use of information technology in tertiary education; (v)
 modernizing governance systems and management of the higher education system; and (vi) developing research and
 innovation.

 Component 1: Diversifying and increasing access to tertiary education for a greater professionalization of youth (US$78
 million)

 22.     The Project will directly respond to the unmet demand for tertiary education and address the critical challenges
 of low access to relevant short-term programs aligned with the labor market needs. This component consists of two sub-
 components, aligned with the Government’s priority focus areas.

  Subcomponent 1.1. Increasing Access through ISEP Construction

 23.     This sub-component will focus on expanding the supply of public short-term vocational programs at the tertiary
 level, which has been a central objective of the Government’s post-secondary education reform. It would respond to the
 following identified challenges hindering progress in the tertiary education sub-sector: (i) unmet demand for tertiary
 education; (ii) mismatch between skills with high demand and the education provided by public universities; and (iii)
 shortage and inadequate distribution of relevant public vocational education institutions.

 24.     Senegal’s ISEPs have succeeded in making training and job matching a reality in the sphere of tertiary education.
 The focus of the two-year programs has been on providing skilled manpower for some of the strategic sectors of the
 Senegalese economy, in particular: agriculture, water and energy, mining, ICT and telecommunications, and management.
 In addition, the programs were implemented to absorb the “ bacheliers�? and reduce the overcrowding at the two main
 universities, UCAD and the University Gaston Berger (UGB) of Saint-Louis. In 2021, a total of 1061 students graduated from
 ISEP, nearly 50 percent of which were female. ISEP graduate employment rates are high, with an estimated 90 percent of
 recent graduates able to find work, including an estimated 15 percent of whom are self-employed. Similarly, the ISEPs
 graduation rates are among the best in country, standing at 99 percent.

 25.      This sub-component would support the construction of several ISEPs across the country to complement the
 existing ISEPs financed by the World Bank, Korea International Cooperation Agency (KOICA), and German Development
 Bank (KfW). These would potentially include the two ISEPs in Richard Toll and Bignogna that were only legally established
 and initially agreed to be supported by the French Development Agency (AFD). Lessons learned in the implementation of
 the Higher Education Project have shown improvement in efficiency and relevance of the tertiary education system by
 reducing the overall number of years to get a degree with the increase of short-term tertiary education and the

 development of alternative ways to access tertiary education. The new ISEPs will provide two-year tertiary education
 programs linked with the economic needs, with a focus on key emerging priority sectors such as agriculture, agrobusiness,
 public works, electronics, mechanics, mining, petroleum, and information technology. At the agricultural focused centers,
 this would include training for youth in agricultural production, fisheries, and livestock with a focus on transformation and

 demand, equity, and private sector links.

     Subcomponent 1.2. Equipping ISEPs for pedagogical excellence

 26.      The sub-component will support a package of interventions focused on improved pedagogy for the growing
 network of ISEPs. This will include enhanced relevance of existing curriculum, new curriculum development where needed,
 training of teaching staff, and provision of pedagogical equipment to be determined in close consultation with the private
 sector. The project will also support enhanced ICT for the network of ISEPs, including: (i) the extension and improvement
 of the campus IT networks of tertiary institutions; and (ii) the training and capacity building for the staff managing such
 networks and equipment.

     Component 2: Strengthening the governance of tertiary education system

 27.    This component aims to improve the stewardship of the higher education system in Senegal and would directly
 address the low quality of tertiary education and the inefficiency of tertiary education spending through the
 implementation of key institutional reforms. The component consists of two sub-components.

     Subcomponent 2.1. Spurring Research and Innovation

 28.     The sub-component will also finance an open competitive fund for higher education to source and fund high-
 impact research. This pilot is meant to explore what works in terms of flexible funding to spur research and innovation,
 and to identify promising areas of research for further scale up.

     Subcomponent 2.2. Supporting key institutional reforms

 29.      This sub-component would support sub-sector-wide institutional reform package and its operational plan directed
 towards improving the overall governance, efficiency, and management of tertiary education. The project will achieve the
 governance objective through a pragmatic approach, supporting ongoing institutional reforms aimed at conferring more
 autonomy to HEIs while strengthening the planning, oversight, and information management of the Ministry of Higher
 Education (MoHE) (autonomy with accountability). This approach will be implemented in a two-pronged fashion. On the
 one hand, it will entail supporting stronger stewardship of the MoHE through continued strengthened quality assurance
 (QA), monitoring and evaluation (M&E) and the coordination and regulatory functions of systems and bodies. On the other
 hand, it will entail supporting the implementation of measures to improve governance in HEIs premised on best practices
 with the potential for scaling up. It will implement measures to empower HEIs with greater autonomy with regard to
 student admissions, teacher recruitment and budget management. This sub-component will also support greater lateral
 integration between the TVET centers and the ISEPs to (i) allow TVET institutions to benefit from system strengthening at
 the ISEP level by aligning with improved curriculum and quality standards set forth for trades; (ii) allow ISEPs to utilize

 30.      The project will have a strong focus on the effective engagement of the private sector for increased relevance and

 31.     As part of the reform package, the project will support an audit of the current scholarship approach, and propose

 participation in STEM fields, and better targeting of youth from the poorest households.

  Component 3: Strengthening Project Management and Monitoring and Evaluation (US$8 million)

 32.     This component will support the implementation of the project. It will finance the equipment, training of staff,
 the preparation of monitoring reports and technical assistance (TA) to the relevant implementing actors for the effective
 management and monitoring of the project. This component will also finance the necessary TA related to the support of
 a similar and well performing international tertiary education institution to accompany the development and the
 implementation of the ISEP, including a communications and branding campaign to situate ISEPs within the broader post-
 secondary landscape. These Institutions will be selected on a competitive basis for each ISEP.

 33.       It will also support the preparation and implementation of the environmental and social action plans. This sub-
 component will also finance: (i) training of staff in universities, Ministry of Finance, and MoHE in managing PBCs; (ii) TA;
 and (iii) equipment to monitor the implementation of PBCs.
