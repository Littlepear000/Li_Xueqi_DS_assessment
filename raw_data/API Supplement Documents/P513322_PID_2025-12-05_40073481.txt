B. Introduction and Context

Country Context

1.     Russia’s invasion of Ukraine has caused severe human suffering and triggered an unprecedented humanitarian
and economic crisis. Almost four years of active hostilities have resulted in significant civilian casualties, widespread
destruction of critical infrastructure, and massive population displacement that continues to reshape the country's
demographic landscape. As of October 2025, approximately 5.8 million Ukrainian refugees —14 percent of the
population—were recorded globally, including 5.2 million in Europe. The number of Internally Displaced Persons peaked
at 6.5 million in March 2022 and stabilized at approximately 3.7 million as of September 2025, which is 9 percent of the
population. The invasion has also led to a sharp increase in disability, with the number of people with disabilities rising
by over 10 percent to reach 3 million by early 2024. The invasion has reversed years of progress in poverty reduction and
dramatically worsened income inequality. The combination of sustained conflict, mass displacement, rising disability
prevalence, and deteriorating economic conditions has created compounding pressures on Ukraine's social protection
system, leading to an unprecedented level of demand for support.


Sectoral and Institutional Context

2.     Ukraine’s social protection system has shown remarkable resilience during the ongoing invasion. Overall, social
protection expenditures, which include pensions and social insurance, account for around 14.8 percent of the country’s
Gross Domestic Product (GDP). State budget spending on the social safety net (SSN) constitutes about 2 percent of GDP.
During the invasion, the Government of Ukraine (GoU) not only continues its SSN reform efforts but also strengthens
existing systems and programs, while developing long-term strategies to lay the foundations for sustainable recovery
when peace is restored. However, the system is simultaneously challenged by multiple crises: massive displacement,
surging poverty, large-scale disability, and millions of Ukrainians who need essential social support. These challenges are
particularly acute in three interconnected domains—social assistance, social services, and disability support.

3.      Social assistance provision needs to be enhanced by connecting beneficiaries to social services through
integrated case management. The centralized social assistance system lacks effective integration with decentralized
social services and provides no meaningful pathways out of welfare dependency. Currently, cash transfers significantly

    exceed expenditures for social services and support programs designed to help people exit difficult circumstances. To
    provide comprehensive support to the poor and vulnerable, the current phase of SSN reforms focuses on improving
    efficiency and targeting of social assistance, linking beneficiaries to social services to support their transition from welfare
    to self-sufficiency, introducing integrated case management, and advancing the digitalization of the delivery systems. A
    crucial aspect of this process is the Basic Social Assistance (BSA) reform, which improves Ukraine’s last-resort social
    assistance program, Guaranteed Minimum Income (GMI), and takes initial steps to address the fragmentation of the
    system by consolidating five benefits into a single “one-stop-shop�? program.

    4.      The financing mechanisms for social services need improvement to ensure equitable access. The current system
    tends to prioritize funding for maintaining residential facilities rather than focusing on assessing needs and delivering
    high-quality community-based services to the vulnerable. The decentralization reform transferred planning, organization,
    financing, and delivery responsibilities for the main social services to the community level, but local capacities and
    budgets are now severely strained due to the impacts of the invasion. Additionally, local authorities face challenges in
    service provision due to the lack of a marketplace and a well-established regulatory framework for procuring services
    from various providers, including non-governmental organizations (NGOs) and the private sector. As a result, access to
    services is inconsistent, and communities' capacity to provide services varies significantly. Effectively addressing these
    challenges requires dynamic central-local relations with aligned functions and financing capabilities, and a financing
    model that allocates state budget funds for priority services - to rebalance service delivery - and uses an effective service
    purchasing mechanism. As an initial step, the GoU is currently piloting the purchase of centrally funded social services.
    This initiative aims to test the new financing model and address existing supply gaps. The pilots are being implemented
    by the Fund for Social Protection of People with Disabilities.1

    5.      The disability support system is under immense pressure due to an influx of new cases since the invasion began.
    The system remains complex and requires better coordination, as disability policies are fragmented across multiple
    ministries—Health, Social Policy, Economy, and Veterans Affairs—operating without a unified framework that links
    assessment, benefit provision, rehabilitation, and employment support. Currently, only 16 percent of people with
    disabilities are employed, resulting in a 34 percentage-point employment gap compared to people living with disabilities
    in the European Union. To improve the disability support, Ukraine is transitioning from a medicalized disability
    assessment model to an integrated, person-centered approach aligned with the International Classification of
    Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) and is developing new systems and programs.

    6.      The GoU has requested a new social protection operation to support the implementation of a comprehensive
    reform package aimed at accelerating transformative changes across social assistance, social services, and disability
    support systems. The proposed Social Protection Project for Inclusion, Resilience, Innovation, and Transformation
    (SPIRIT) is a standalone Investment Project Financing operation, financed by an IBRD loan of US$500 million supported
    by bilateral guarantees and a grant of US$10 million from the Ukraine Relief, Recovery, Reconstruction, and Reform Trust
    Fund (URTF). The SPIRIT project will focus on three critical areas within the social protection system: (i) the full-scale
    implementation of the BSA program and the introduction of effective integrated case management to accelerate poverty
    reduction; (ii) the development of social services and related regulatory framework to introduce social services of national
    priority and establish a national purchasing agency for services; and (iii) the modernization of social support for people
    with disabilities, which includes improved disability assessment system and increased access to the labor market through
    supported employment services. The World Bank has been providing technical assistance to the GoU for the development
    and implementation of these reforms with financial support from the Foreign, Commonwealth, and Development Office

    Relationship to CPF

    7.     SPIRIT is part of the World Bank Group's (WBG) effort to support the people of Ukraine during the invasion,
    which has mobilized over US$82 billion since February 2022.3 The Bank's engagement in the country is guided by the
    Ukraine Country Program Update, presented to the Board in December 2024. This update identifies "ensuring the
    uninterrupted provision of core basic services" as one of its key priorities. Additionally, it emphasizes that the WBG will
    continue to support essential institutional reforms. The activities financed by SPIRIT align with these priorities by
    strengthening institutions and programs within Ukraine's social protection system and supporting reforms of social
    assistance and services for the most vulnerable.

    C. Proposed Development Objective(s)
    8.      Project Development Objective (PDO). The PDO is to enhance the integration, efficiency, and inclusiveness of the
    social protection system in Ukraine.

    D. Concept Description

    10.     The proposed project is designed to disburse based on the achievement of key results. The loan funds of US$500
    million will be disbursed against eligible expenditures on selected social assistance benefit programs upon the
    achievement of Performance-Based Conditions (PBCs). Specifically, disbursements will be triggered by the achievement
    of PBCs that support essential reforms in social assistance, social services, and the social protection of people with
    disabilities. Additionally, SPIRIT will provide US$10 million in grant funds to finance activities aimed at strengthening the
    institutional capacity of the Ministry of Social Policy, Family, and Unity (MoSPFU) and the FSPPD to implement reforms
    supported by this project, as well as developing community-based social services. The proposed project will have two
    components.

Component 1

11.    Component 1 - Improving Social Support for the Poor and Vulnerable. The project will allocate US$500 million in
loan funds under Component 1. These funds will be disbursed against eligible expenditures once specific PBCs are met.
The component will finance four selected social benefits envisaged by the state budget, including social assistance for
children from large families; temporary assistance for children whose parents do not pay alimony; social pensions, and
BSA, which is being implemented in a pilot phase. The SPIRIT project will reimburse expenditures for these existing
benefits until the full-scale implementation of the consolidated BSA program is enacted. From that point, the project will
finance the consolidated BSA program. The achievement of nine PBCs will trigger disbursements of funds under
Component 1. These disbursement-linked indicators should be considered as conditions that incentivize achieving
additional outcomes beyond what eligible expenditures alone can deliver.

12.    PBC 1 supports transformative social assistance reform by consolidating Ukraine's safety net, improving
targeting, and enhancing administrative efficiency to respond flexibly to socio-economic crises. The nationwide
implementation of the BSA program, which is currently under a pilot, advances four interconnected changes:

Consolidation of existing benefit programs. The BSA merges five existing benefits into a single "one-stop shop" anti-
poverty program. These programs include the Guaranteed Minimum Income (GMI); social assistance for single parents;
social assistance for children from large families; temporary assistance for children whose parents do not pay alimony;
and social assistance for individuals who do not qualify for an old-age pension and for people with disabilities (social
pension). This consolidation aims to enhance administrative efficiency by unifying programs’ rules and reducing the
administrative burden on both the system and beneficiaries.

Enhanced adequacy. The BSA will have a higher income eligibility threshold than the existing benefits it consolidates,
enabling more vulnerable households to access the support they need.

Modernized benefit calculation. The BSA introduces a new indicator of basic needs, the basic value, replacing the
Subsistence Minimum. This decoupling enhances the scalability of social assistance and allows benefit levels to respond
to actual vulnerability without triggering budget-straining recalculations across unrelated payment categories linked to
the Subsistence Minimum.

Improved administration and delivery infrastructure. The GoU is continuing its administrative reform of social assistance
by shifting responsibilities for client intake, benefits provision, and payments to the Pension Fund of Ukraine (PFU),
thereby transforming it into a unified payment agency. Currently, the PFU pays over 40 types of benefits and will
administer the BSA. Multi-channel enrollment—through the PFU offices, Administrative Service Centers, and the Diia
digital portal—will be refined based on pilot results to minimize inclusion/exclusion errors and ensure that the system
can effectively manage beneficiary caseloads.

13.     PBC 2 will support a significant shift towards an integrated model of benefits and services provision through a
case management approach. The World Bank team, in close collaboration with the MoSPFU, is developing an integrated
case management model to link BSA program beneficiaries to essential social services. This comprehensive approach
builds on best practices and experiences from similar models implemented with support from the World Bank in other
countries, such as Italy. The current centralized social assistance system lacks effective integration with decentralized
social services and provides no meaningful pathways out of welfare dependency. The new model under development is
expected to consolidate the efforts of various social protection and employment agencies at both the central and local
levels. It will focus on proactively identifying families in need, especially those in crises, and on preventing them from

14.     Through PBC 3 and PBC 4, the project will advance critical reforms in Ukraine's social services system . The GoU
is committed to building a system that expands coverage for vulnerable populations and delivers integrated, high-quality,
and user-friendly social services. To bridge existing coverage gaps, the GoU is developing a diversified financing model
for social services that incorporates funding from the state budget. As the first step, the GoU launched pilots to purchase
priority social services using state budget funds from a range of providers, including private-sector organizations and
NGOs. The next phase of the reform, supported by SPIRIT, will focus on two key policy measures: (i) establishing a
mechanism for prioritizing services to be funded by the state budget and (ii) the creation of a designated agency
responsible for purchasing social services.

15.     PBC 5 will support regulatory changes to establish centrally funded procurement of comprehensive
development and care services for children with disabilities to prevent their exclusion . This pilot will test procedures
for delivering these services to children with disabilities aged six and older. It aims to create a procurement mechanism
for the services contributing to: (i) the social integration and development of children who, due to health issues, cannot
attend mainstream secondary education full-time; (ii) integration of children with disabilities into mainstream
educational institutions; (iii) support for parents to help them re-enter the workforce; and (iv) prevention of the
placement of children in institutions by promoting family-based care.

16.     PBCs 6, 7, and 8 aim to support disability support system, moving from a medical model of disability to a
comprehensive, human rights-based approach. Building on initial reforms that replaced the substandard medical-social
expertise system with assessments of functioning, PBCs 6 and 7 will support the next transformative step by introducing
an assessment mechanism based on the biopsychosocial model and aligned with the World Health Organization’s ICF.
This shift is expected to guide the GoU’s strategies and programs in healthcare, social protection, education, employment,
and physical culture/sports sectors and expand access to rehabilitation, assistive technologies, and personalized services
for individuals with disabilities or functional limitations, including older adults. The mechanism will be implemented in
phases, beginning with the pilot supported by PBC 7. PBC 8 complements these activities by introducing Active Labor
Market Programs and targeted social services to enhance employability, earnings, and self-sufficiency for people with
disabilities.

Component 2

17.    Component 2 - Enhancing the Capacity of the Social Protection System and Developing Social Services.
Component 2, with a total amount of up to US$10 million, will finance investments to develop community-based social
services and enhance the institutional capacity of the MoSPFU and FSPPD to implement project-supported reforms.

18.     To implement the BSA reform and establish an integrated case management mechanism, the MoSPFU needs to
strengthen its Management Information System (MIS). A key initiative in Ukraine's efforts to modernize and digitize its
social protection system is the Unified Information System of the Social Sphere (UISSS). The UISSS is a comprehensive,
centralized digital environment that streamlines and simplifies the management of social benefits and services. The
SPIRIT project will finance hardware and application software to ensure that all components of the UISSS work
synergistically, including the subsystems and modules currently under development. This will be particularly important
during the full-scale implementation of the BSA and the introduction of integrated case management.

19.    The capacity of the FSPPD needs strengthening to enable the implementation of a new financing model.

Currently, the FSPPD is implementing pilots focused on social services financed by the state budget. These pilots are
testing the purchasing mechanism to identify potential bottlenecks, gather essential insights, and ensure timely
preparation for the full-scale implementation of a new financing model for social services. The SPIRIT project will finance
services and goods, including computer and office equipment and furniture, for the FSPPD.

20.    Component 2 will also invest in the development of community-based social services. The project seeks not only
to improve minimum income support and connect beneficiaries to essential services, but also to strengthen the
availability and quality of social services at the community level. Specifically, the project will help address gaps by
establishing priority social services tailored to local needs. SPIRIT will support the creation of local social service providers
through project-financed microprojects. The project will identify target beneficiary groups and the specific service options
required to address their challenging life circumstances through participatory needs assessments. The project will finance
goods (equipment) and services to help create community-based providers. The microprojects are not expected to
involve civil works. The project will develop a community selection methodology, with the selection criteria focused on
communities with high poverty levels, social vulnerability, and at-risk populations.
