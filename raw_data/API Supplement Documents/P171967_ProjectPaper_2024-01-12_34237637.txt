I. PROJECT STATUS AND RATIONALE FOR RESTRUCTURING



     1. The Regional Infrastructure Financing Facility (RIFF) Project was approved by World Bank’s Board of Executive
        Directors on June 30, 2020, and it became effective on October 5, 2020. The Project Development Objective is
        to expand long-term finance to private firms in selected infrastructure sectors in Eastern and Southern Africa.
        The Project is an Investment Project Financing (IPF), with a credit line and technical assistance (TA). The project
        has 3 components: component 1: Long-term Finance to Infrastructure Sub-projects (US$325 million),
        component 2: COVID-19 Infrastructure Sector Small and Medium-sized Enterprise (SME) Facility (US$75 million)
        and component 3: Capacity Building and Technical Assistance to TDB (US$15 million) and COMESA (15 million).
        TDB has borrowed US$400 million IDA SUF credit for Components 1 and 2, and a US$15 million IDA credit on
        regular terms (concessional credit) to finance its TA needs (Sub-component 3a). The COMESA Secretariat is the
        Recipient of the US$10 million equivalent grant for the TA (Sub-component 3b).

     2. As of October 30, 2023, $80.26 million or 18.89 percent of the project’s $425.0 Million has been disbursed. Of
        the total disbursements, $75M was disbursed to the credit lines and $5.26M to the TA of both COMESA and
        TDB. Disbursements have been slower than projected on account of (i) covid 19 which delayed the
        development of projects (ii) the slow nature of infrastructure project development and (iii) tranche
        disbursement which is characteristic of project financing. The project became effective on October 5, 2020,
        during COVID-19. COVID-19 severely weakened the ability of energy utilities to enter into power purchasing

     agreements with private investors, as a result several infrastructure sub-projects in the pipeline did not
     materialize or have been delayed. TDB has managed to add several new sub-projects to the pipeline
     and are optimistic that investor confidence in the sector is being restored and the pent-up demand will
     result in increased disbursement to the sub-projects in the pipeline. The infrastructure gaps in Eastern
     and Southern Africa persist and the urgency to fill these gaps remains. Hence the relevance of the
     objectives and activities of RIFF remains as strong as ever.

3.   The primary objective of the proposed restructuring is to continue to support the critical infrastructure
     financing gap while responding to the increased demand for financing under component 2 and the
     slower pace of sub-project finalizations under component 1. The other objectives of the proposed
     restructuring are as follows: (i) to allow for funding of SMEs with larger borrowing capacity beyond the
     $7 million cap included in the TDB’s definition of SMEs (ii) Given the change in relevance and focus on
     COVID, the restructuring proposes to widen the scope of component 2 to address other future crises;
     (iii) to add TDB’s wholly owned subsidiary, the Trade and Development Fund (TDF) as an implementing
     entity/recipient. The TDF, is now organized to manage the SME segment of the market, but while
     maintaining the governance and risk framework that applies to TDB lending. It is expected that going
     forward, the smaller loans i.e., the SME segment will be handled by the TDF, with the TDB directly
     handling the larger loan segment. The TDF is developing a robust pipeline of projects and it is important
     to ensure that the TDF pipeline can benefit from the competitive financing provided under RIFF. The
     restructuring also proposes to allow TDB to lend to financial intermediaries for further on lending to
     the private sector under both component 1 and 2 to widen the footprint of the TDB and to also enable
     mobilization of co-financing from such financial intermediaries. The focus on long term finance will still
     be maintained with the significant increase in the pipeline under component 1 which is expected to
     materialize over the course of the next 1-2 years. This restructuring also proposes extension of the
     current closing date of the project from September 26, 2025, by 12 months to September 26, 2026, to
     provide sufficient time for the currently robust but slow-moving pipeline under component 1 to
     develop and for sub-projects to close.

4. The proposed restructuring will include:

(i)     Reallocation between components 1 and 2: The restructuring proposes a reallocation of US $50 million
        from component 1 to component 2, over and above the current US$75 million allocation, taking the total
        allocation under component 2 to US$125M and the allocation under component 1 will be reduced from
        $325M to US$275M. Component 2 of RIFF has a current allocation of US$ 75 million all of which has been
        disbursed to four sub-projects (Table 1 in Annex 1. Currently, demand for component 2 already exceeds
        the existing financing allocation (based on the current pipeline), thereby necessitating the reallocation. It
        is observed that new deals under component 2 have relatively shorter periods from business
        development to closure. It is therefore expected that the additional pipeline that will develop for
        component 2 over the next 1-2 years will be sufficient to utilize the amount of US$50 million being
        allocated under this restructuring in addition to the existing pipeline of TDF mentioned above. In addition,
        there is a pipeline of projects with about 17-20 financial institutions in the region which is substantial (to
        the tune of US$40 million). The breakdown of the utilization of the US$50 million is expected to be as
        follows:
        o US$ 9.2 million: additional amount needed to satisfy the current approvals and pipeline for
             Component 2.
        o US$ 22 million: Pipeline sourced through TDF (see Table 2 in Annex 2); and.
        o US$ 18.8 million: to cater to new sub-projects (this is expected to come partially from TDB’s own
             business development and partially from the existing US$40 million pipeline with TDB’s partner
             financial institutions in the region mentioned above)


(ii)    Change in institutional and implementation arrangements to include in addition to the TDB, its wholly
        owned subsidiary, the Trade and Development Fund (the TDF) (see Annex 3). Implementation by TDF via
        a Subsidiary Agreement subject to the satisfactory assessment of its capacity. TDF is now leading on
        smaller transactions that are below TDB threshold (under US$10 million), while building a variety of other
        instruments, including guarantees, grants and capacity building to sustainably serve SMEs and other
        traditionally underserved groups, including women. The Project will leverage the combined strengths of
        TDB and TDF to enable financing for both larger and smaller companies and those in nascent markets and
        underserved groups, including female entrepreneurs. The Financing Agreement will be amended to
        reference TDB Group which would include TDB and TDF. The proposed language is as follows: “TDB shall
        cause TDF to carry out the implementation of some activities under Part 2 of the Project with due diligence
        and in conformance with appropriate technical, economic, financial, managerial, environmental and social
        standards and practices satisfactory to the Association, as well as in accordance with the Project
        Operations Manual, the ESMS (including, where applicable, the Guidance Note for lending to PFIs and TA
        activities) requirements and reporting, and provisions of the Anti-Corruption Guidelines, Anti-Money
        Laundering and Combatting the Financing of Terrorism Guidelines applicable to recipients of loan proceeds
        other than the Recipient.”

        TDB shall ensure that TDF provides financing to eligible SME through SME Sub-Credits that meet the
        requirements of Section I.D of the Annex 2. The TDB shall sign a Subsidiary Agreement between TDB and
        TDF to this effect that is legally binding. TDB retains the overall fiduciary responsibility of the project

           TDB cap of US$7 million to. In order to achieve the electrification targets and SDG7 we would need to

           several regional service providers of solar home systems that we can better target and service via the RIFF,
           but their loan request will be larger than the current size referenced in TDB’s SME definition.

(iv)       Removal of Covid 19 as a focus of component 2: Given the lower level of relevance of Covid 19, with the
           passage of time since the approval of the project, this restructuring proposes to remove the Covid 19 focus
           of component 2 of the project to widen it to include shock response & resilience to be able to respond to
           any other future exigencies, including those relating to the impacts of climate change. This is more so the
           case given there were no loan disbursements prior to the end of the pandemic, so it would be hard to
           claim that we responded to the pandemic at the end of the project.

(v)        TDB lending to FIs (financial intermediaries): The restructuring proposes to allow TDB to lend to financial
           intermediaries for further on lending to the private sector under both component 1 and 2, to further
           extend the footprint of the project and increase coverage. TDB has a working relationship with several FIs
           in the regions. Enabling lending to FIs with robust institutions through the RIFF with the ultimate risk being
           retained by TDB will enable TDB to disburse quickly building on the pipelines available with these FIs, while
           also ensuring no additional risk to the World Bank. As specified in (ii) above the participating financial
           institutions will adhere to Bank policies. In keeping with the World Bank fiduciary guidelines, a Financial
           intermediaries financing (FIF) assessment has been undertaken to ensure that TDB has robust criteria for
           the assessment of participating financial institutions which will include inter alia (i) meeting national
           supervisors legal and regulatory requirements (ii) portfolio quality and performance criteria; and at the
           transaction level (iii) ESF (Annex 4). This approach has the additional advantage of catalyzing additional
           funding by onboarding additional FIs to the off-grid sector including in local currency. In terms of
           proportion of financing that will go through the FIF route, the current estimation is approximately 20%.
           The restructuring has been discussed with TDB and follows a formal letter of request by TDB.

(vi)       Project extension: The restructuring proposed an extension of the current closing date of the project of
           September 26, 2025, by 12 months to September 26, 2026, to provide sufficient time for the currently
           robust but slow-moving pipeline under component 1 to develop and for deals to close.

5. This restructuring does not concern the US$10 million technical assistance component managed by COMESA
   which will remain unchanged.

6. The PDO will remain unchanged as the focus of the projects remains the same as earlier. All the other conditions
   remain unchanged.

7. The E&S requirements will remain the same.

    Background
    Trade and Development Fund (TDF), a wholly owned subsidiary of TDB, is a non-profit institution established to lead
    innovative, inclusive, and sustainable solutions through effective use of blended finance, special technical assistance
    programs and capacity building.
    TDF provides program management and capacity building services, thought leadership, and blended finance to clients in
    TDB Group Member States, with a special focus on financially underserved groups as final beneficiaries. It provides its
    services through various instruments such as grants, guarantees, equity, quasi equity and loans.
    In line with the Group’s overall mission, TDF intermediates capital from a variety of funders and through three
    workstreams, create and support economic growth through industry and infrastructure, increased access to education
    and knowledge and reduced economic and social inequalities.
    The TDF has an opportunity to participate in a global trend in intentional investment for development through its three
    workstreams: Incubation, Knowledge and Investment.

                         Annex 4: Financial Intermediary Financing (FIF) Review for RIFF Project
Introduction. A Financial intermediary financing (FIF) review was conducted for the purpose of supporting the
restructuring of the RIFF project to allow TDB to on-lend to other financial intermediaries in the infrastructure and energy
value chains. TDB will continue to do direct lending to the sponsors as well. The scope of the review included a detailed
assessment of TDBs risk management systems to vet, select and monitor financial intermediaries and considered TDB’s
track record in working with Financial Intermediaries (FIs). The key benefits of allowing financial intermediary lending
through the RIFF are as follows:

    1. TDB has gained some experience in lending to the distributed renewable energy sector and infrastructure
       development under the RIFF and will expand to work with other financial institutions through co-financing and
       on-lending arrangements and capacity building. This will crowd in FIs to participate in TDB-led project and
       infrastructure transactions in TDB member states through risk sharing syndications, Co-Financing and on-lending.
       FIF support better risk management where the risks for specific transactions are shared between TDB and the FI.
    2. To support the acceleration of energy access since half of the population still live without access to electricity; and
       to work towards closing the infrastructure gaps within the East and Southern region by having more financial
       institutions increase their capacity to lend.
    3. The FIF strategy will help to create greater leverage for the RIFF funds, as the Financial Intermediaries will co-
       finance the projects, thereby allowing the RIFF funds to finance much more projects. For example, for every $1 of
       RIFF funding, it should be able to unlock multiple times lending given the sponsor will put in equity and the other
       financial institution will contribute to lending thereby reducing TDB’s exposure to any one project and leaving
       funds to invest in other projects. Much lower leverage is created with direct lending only by TDB.
    4. Access to local currency financing has been a major challenge for infrastructure developers and given TDB’s
       portfolio and the RIFF, are denominated in USD, TDB has not been able to meet the demand for local currency.
       The lack of local currency financing exposes the project sponsor to excessive foreign exchange risks given the
       volatility of local currencies in the region to the USD. The FIF strategy will help to unlock much needed local
       currency funding for infrastructure and energy development.
    5. FIF will set the stage for ASCENT MPA project (pipeline) which will mobilize US$250 million of non-concessional
       financing from the IDA20 Regular SUW, to lend to TDB to provide debt financing to distributed renewable energy
       (DRE) companies operating in the AFE region, while mobilizing additional private capital, in order to unlock
       financing for the sector’s growth. The TDB platform will allow for FIF and will link individual country operations
       into a cohesive regional framework by mobilizing and providing financing for distributed renewable energy (DRE)
       at the regional scale, thereby building larger regional DRE markets and contributing to regional integration
       outcomes. DRE products include solar systems, mini-grids, clean cooking equipment and other renewable energy
       investments. The first phase of the MPA will establish the unit within TDB to offer a menu of financial products,
       from lending to de-risking for DRE in the region.

Special FIF features. The project has four features that were considered in the FIF review. First, TDB has worked and
continue to work extensively through financial institutions for its trade finance portfolio. As such, TDB has developed the
capacity to work with financial institutions and have developed solid track records and relationships with the FIs which
would be leveraged for project financing under the RIFF project. This expertise will enable TDB and FIs to price the
products based on risks and increase the technical capacity in the energy and infrastructure space. Second, TDB may act
as both an apex for national level project finance and a direct retailer for large regional projects or those where no national
participating financial institution expresses interest. Third, the RIFF project will continue to only finance private sector
investments. Finally, TDB is a supranational financial institution and does not fall under any national financial supervisory
or regulatory body (see supervision discussion below).

The FIF reviewed considered inter alia TDB’s financial strength; TDB’s Risk Management and Supervision, and TDB’s
capacity to (i) assess the macroeconomic environment of its member countries (ii) due diligence system for Financial
Institutions (iii) transaction level due diligence.

    1. TDB Financial Strength. TDB is a sound, profitable and well-managed commercial financial institution with the
       following performance highlights as of December 2022:
             Assets of US$8.4 billion, US$ 6.4 billion in total liabilities; and US$ 1.9 billion in total equity. Assets
               increased by 5% in 2022, despite exogenous regional and international shocks.
             Liquidity maintained at over 20% of total assets.
             Liabilities balanced between short-term (43%) and long-term borrowings (31%), a reflection of the balance
               between trade finance and longer-term project finance.
             International ratings in BB+ range with positive outlook (Fitch, as of March 2023).
             TDB's gross non-performing loan (NPL) ratio was 2.93% in December 2022.

    2. Risk management and supervision. As a supranational financial institution, TDB does not come under the
       jurisdiction of any national supervisory authority or regulatory body. However, TDB has a highly developed
       independent risk management system that is in line with international best practices and international supervisory
       and regulatory standards which is bolstered by annual independent external audits. As part of its risk
       management TDB actively manages exposure and concentration risks by financing activities across its 24 member
       countries. TDB’s active risk management approach covers portfolio risk, currency risk, maturity mismatch risk,
       environmental and social risk, and concentration of exposure risk. TDB has a robust governance framework that
       includes: (i) a detailed credit risk management system including project level analysis of financial, environmental,
       and social risks, (ii) use of good practices of Basel international standards, (iii) annual external audits, and (iv) and
       outsourced internal audit function with an international audit firm reporting directly to the Board of Directors.

    3. Surveillance of the Macroeconomic Environment of Member Countries. TDB’s decision to lend directly to a
       project or through a financial intermediary will consider the country’s macroeconomic situation. TDB already has
       the capacity to carry out such reviews efficiently, as demonstrated under the RIFF project. Further as part of TDB’s
       risk management and ongoing surveillance it monitors the macroeconomic environment of its member countries,
       which feeds into TDB setting maximum exposure limits for countries.

4. Financial Institutions due diligence selection system. TDB has a robust due diligence system established and
   staffed with an adequate number of experienced financial analysts for expansion of the FI network. This system
   includes all the key areas of financial and risk analysis, based on interviews, three-year trend analysis of standard
   financial ratios (using audited financial statements), and Central Bank information. The analysis covers the
   following: (i) organizational structure and Board composition, (ii) business lines, (iii) assets, especially the
   composition of the loan portfolio (maturity, sectoral composition, top 20 loans assessment, concentration and
   related party transactions, write-offs, loan recovery rates), (iv) liquidity ratios (deposits, buffers, contingencies
   and Government security holdings), (v) Liabilities (costs, maturities, leverage, credit lines, contingent liabilities),
   and (vi) capital (risk weighted capital adequacy, and actual or projected capital shortfalls). From the income
   statement, TDB assesses (i) interest income (sources, growth, and sustainability), (ii) average cost of capital, and
   (iii) average annual returns on equity and assets. Finally, from a risk management perspective, TDB reviews policies
   for (i) credit review (see transaction due diligence below), (ii) rescheduling and restructuring policies and practices,
   (iii) staffing, (iv) collateral policy, (v) provisioning and write-offs, and (vi) the emergency response system in place
   for liquidity shortfalls and other potential threats. Finally, TDB reviews the FI’s core banking system, anti-money
   laundering practices and any pending legal issues, as well as the environmental and social risk management
   system in place. The various perspectives are brought together with the FI’s strategic plan, including market
   analysis, financial projections, a strengths/weaknesses analysis, and a review of competitors in the same sectors.

   risk and operational considerations in that review process.

    experience can be adapted to project finance under the RIFF. TDB’s success in financial intermediary lending is
    evidenced by strong loan portfolio growth, active management of portfolio and other risks, and a low level of non-
