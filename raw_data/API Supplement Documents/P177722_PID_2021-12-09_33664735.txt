     B. Introduction and Context

     Country Context

 1.        Tajikistan remains the poorest among the former Soviet countries despite decent economic growth in the past
 two decades. Tajikistan had a population of 9.57 million and GDP per capita of US$859 in 2020. Between 2000 and 2019,
 the poverty rate fell from 83 to 26 percent of the population 1, while the economy grew at an average rate of seven percent
 per year. The growth rate slowed down to 4.5 percent in 2020 due to the COVID-19 crisis but rebounded in 2021.2
 However, the country remains the poorest among the former Soviet countries. The country’s economic outcomes reflect:
 (i) the legacy of the 1992–97 civil war, (ii) a centralized, state-led approach to economic management, (iii) low domestic
 productivity, with wages that leave most households exposed to seasonal poverty risks, and (iv) the “low-level
 equilibrium�? of a remittance-financed, import-reliant, indirect tax-based economic model that has provided little space
 for and support to the private sector.
 2.        The country lacks economic opportunities for its young and growing population, leading to high unemployment
 and large labor out-migration. The working-age population (aged 15 to 64) rose from 3.3 million in 2000 to 5.7 million in
 2020, of which only 41 percent were part of the labor force. With over a third of the population currently under 14 years
 old, Tajikistan’s labor force is set to rise over the long term. However, Tajikistan lacks enough jobs to absorb its working -
 age population.3 Even before the COVID-19 crisis, more than one in three young people and almost nine in ten female
 youths were not in employment, education, or training (NEET). The lack of job opportunities drives many Tajiks to seek
 employment abroad. Before the COVID-19 crisis, between 600,000 and one million prime-age Tajiks (mostly men) worked
 overseas, principally in Russia, sending remittances to their families. Migrant transfers in 2013 totaled more than US$3.4
 billion, equivalent to 40 percent of GDP, making Tajikistan one of the world’s most remittance-dependent countries.4
 3.      Risks related to fragility, conflict, and violence (FCV) constrain Tajikistan’s development progress. Since many
 people in Tajikistan (particularly youth, women, and returning migrants) are either unemployed or work in predominantly
 insecure, low quality, and low-wage jobs in the informal sector, their livelihood is vulnerable to external shocks. The recent
 COVID-19 crisis has caused severe damage to local livelihood. For example, the crisis led to a sudden stop to tourism
 inflows, depriving a key source of livelihood for the population of the Gorno-Badakhshan Autonomous Oblast (GBAO).5
 The vulnerability has further increased following the developments in neighboring Afghanistan, as the two countries share
 a porous 1,357 km (843 miles) border. The population of the bordering regions of Khatlon and GBAO is more conducive
 to radicalization as these are lagging regions with fewer economic opportunities.

     Sectoral and Institutional Context

 Private sector development can help Tajikistan create more and better jobs.
 4.      Tajikistan is primarily an agriculture economy, with the majority of the population working in low-productivity
 agriculture. More than 70 percent of the workforce is in agriculture, which pays three times lower wages than the non-
 agriculture sector (including manufacturing and service activities). As a result of the low productivity, the agriculture sector
 accounts for 22 percent of the gross domestic product (GDP) despite hiring nearly three-quarters of the workforce. The
 industry sector accounts for another 20 percent of the GDP, while the remaining 59 percent comes from services.6
 5.      The development of the formal non-agriculture private sector may create more and better jobs. While currently
 employing a small share of the workforce, the private sector segment could absorb surplus labor from the agriculture
 sector and offers an opportunity to create more and better jobs. However, the sector is yet to reach its potential fully.
 Improving financial intermediation can help channel resources for private sector-led growth.
 6.       A low level of financial intermediation remains a key obstacle for private sector development. The credit to
 private sector in Tajikistan stood at 13 percent of GDP by the end of 2020, significantly lower than the average of 42
 percent for lower middle-income countries and 57 percent for countries in Europe and Central Asia (ECA).7 The low level
 of credit intermediation made it difficult for private firms to finance investment and working capital using external sources
 - only 8 percent of private firms reported using banks to finance investment, compared with 26 percent in ECA, and only
 13 percent of private firms reported to use banks to finance working capital, compared with 32 percent in ECA.8
 7.       A lack of confidence in the banking sector contributes to low deposit mobilization. The deposits as a share of
 GDP were at 12 percent in 2020, much lower than neighboring countries such as Uzbekistan (20 percent) and Kyrgyz
 Republic (30 percent), and the lowest among lower-middle income countries.9 Only 2.5 percent of adults have an account
 with a formal financial institution, and just 0.3 percent have formal savings. Cash held outside the financial system is
 significant. Tajik retail depositors deposit only TJS 930 (US$82) in bank accounts on average, or 10 percent of the country’s
 per capita income - a considerably low volume especially given that households lack alternative means of saving other
 than bank deposits. In general, a quarter of the population doesn’t have accounts at local banks for lack of trust in financial
 institutions. The role of confidence plays a critical role in mobilizing savings, influenced by the stability of the economy
 and economic policies, and the soundness of institutions and instruments in which savings are held.
 8.      A credible deposit insurance scheme can ameliorate the lack of trust in banks, but the Individual Deposit
 Insurance Fund (IDIF) faces funding and operational weaknesses. A sustained rise in the long-term savings will require
 improvements in several dimensions. A deposit insurance scheme with sufficient reserves and efficient payout procedures
 can prevent contagious bank runs and instill depositor confidence. Nevertheless, depositor confidence deteriorated due
 to recent restrictions in accessing funds at Tojiksodirobonk (TSB) and Agroinvestbonk (AIB). The liquidations of the two
 banks were initiated in May 2021, and the IDIF was able to initiate the payout of depositors shortly thereafter.10 However,
 the payouts left IDIF undercapitalized. The insured depositors’ payouts substantially depleted IDIF’s reserves to around
 100mln somoni, far below the target level in the legislation of 7 percent of individual deposits or 340 mln somoni. Despite
 high premium levels, it would take over ten years for IDIF to reach this target. Closing the funding gap will need to be
 supported by reforms to enhance IDIF’s sustainability. This includes increasing its recovery ratios in future payouts and

 9.       Banks' intermediation to the private sector is poor, particularly impacting access to finance for MSMEs. Banks
 dominate the financial sector and have a long history of weak performance due to poor lending practices. Historically,
 some banks have been subjected to extensive directed lending at subsidized rates for priority projects, politically
 connected borrowers, and loss-making SOEs.11 These practices have discouraged adequate risk assessment and made it
 costly for private sector firms to access bank credits. In 2019, only 18 percent of the firms had a bank loan or line of credit,
 compared with an average of 39 percent in countries of ECA. Only 8 percent of firms finance investments using bank loans,
 compared with 26 percent in ECA. Among those private-sector SMEs that wanted to apply for credit but did not apply in
 2019, most cite high rates and high collateral as the main reason.12 The level of collateral needed to obtain a loan stands
 at 125 percent.
 10.      Partial credit guarantees can support access to finance for MSMEs (including female-headed or female-owned
 firms). These schemes can support MSMEs facing collateral constraints in normal times and the higher risk aversion of
 banks during the COVID-19 crisis. Zamonat and the Credit Guarantee Fund Tajikistan (CGFT) have demonstrated high
 demand in Tajikistan, albeit with limited results due to constrained financial resources and, potentially, design issues. The
 Ministry of Finance (MoF) has expressed interest in scaling up the use of guarantees. The WB is currently undertaking an
 assessment with consideration to operational, financial, and governance parameters. In addition, the National Bank of
 Tajikistan (NBT) requested technical assistance for upgrades to its regulatory framework on credit guarantees, which the
 WB will provide through its technical assistance program or the proposed Project. Annex 2 provides more information.
 Strengthening MSMEs’ market linkages in value chains can kickstart private sector-led growth
 11.      Tajikistan’s private sector has limited market linkages in value chains and specializes in the lower -value-addition
 sections. Connectivity to the international market is low in Tajikistan, especially for SMEs. Only 1.6 percent of small firms
 and 4.6 percent of medium sized firms exported directly in 2019, compared with an average of 14 percent for small firms
 and 25 percent for medium sized firms in ECA. Firms that connect to the international market (typically large ones)
 specialize in producing and exporting raw materials and commodities. Out of Tajikistan’s entire US$1,120 million export
 portfolio in 2019, 65 percent was commodities, including minerals (24 percent), stone (17 percent), metals (14 percent),
 and cotton (11 percent). These raw extractive sectors typically do not create well-paid jobs and are at the lower end of
 global value chains. By creating market linkages and moving up the private sector value chains, especially for SMEs in
 tradable sectors, Tajikistan could take advantage of its demographic dividend to create more jobs and accelerate economic
 growth and poverty reduction.
 12.     The capacity of the non-agriculture private sector needs to be improved to better connect to and move up the
 value chains. In 2019, only 2.2 percent of firms in Tajikistan had an internationally recognized quality certification,
 compared with 22 percent in ECA. The firms in Tajikistan are much less likely to develop new products/services (only 19
 percent introduced a new product/service in 2019, as opposed to 37 percent in ECA), introduce new process innovations
 (only 10 percent introduced new process innovations in 2019, as opposed to 30 percent in ECA), use digital means to do
 business (only 28 percent have own website, as opposed to 50 percent in ECA), or spend on research and development (9
 percent spend on R&D in 2019, as opposed to 14 percent in ECA).13
 13.     Tajikistan has several sectors with potential comparative advantage but lacks an agency to improve SMEs'
 competitiveness, integration into value chains, and international market linkages. There are several sectors with
 potential comparative advantage for enhanced connectivity into value chains, including inter alia: (i) high-value-added

  Relationship to CPF

 14.      The Project is aligned with the World Bank Group’s Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for Tajikistan for
 FY19–23. The Project’s interventions will enable the private sector to create more and better jobs, and thus contribute to
 private-sector led growth - aligning with CPF’s Pillar I, “Strengthening the role of the private sector�?. In addition, the Project
 will allow MSMEs to better connect with firms in regional value chains - contributing to the CPF’s Pillar III, “Promoting
 regional connectivity�?.

  C. Proposed Development Objective(s)


  The Project Development Objective is to strengthen financial intermediation to and market integration of MSMEs.

  Key Results (From PCN)

 15.     The PDO indicators are: (i) Deposit Insurance Fund (DIF): Share of reserves of the IDIF / total deposits; (ii) Portfolio
 Risk Sharing Facility (PRSF): Number of MSMEs receiving financing guaranteed by the PRSF; (iii) Supplier Development
 Program (SDP): Number of SMEs who become accredited suppliers to large companies under the SDP.
 16.     The intermediate result indicators are: (i) Improved capacity by IDIF to conduct payout of insured depositors (as
 measured by payout simulation exercises); (ii) Improved awareness of IDIF’s deposit insurance (as measured by periodic
 surveys); (iii) Number of PFIs under the PRSF; (iv) Volume of MSME credits guaranteed under the PRSF; (v) Volume of
 guarantees called under the PRSF (for monitoring purposes, no target), (vi) Number of female-headed or female-owned
 MSMEs that benefited from the PRSF; (vii) Number of beneficiaries of the SDP’s capacity building; (viii) Number and
 volume of SDP’s partial reimbursement grants; (ix) Number of female-headed or female-owned SMEs that benefited from
 the SDP; (x) Percentage of participating MSMEs reporting satisfaction for the PRSF and SDP support (as measured by
 surveys).

  D. Concept Description

 17.   The proposed Project is a US$50 million Investment Project, with the MoF as the Borrower and primary
 implementing agency. It includes four components:
 18.    Component 1: Deposit Insurance Fund. The component will fund the IDIF and finance payout automation software
 and public awareness campaigns, in order to reduce bank runs and improve depositor trust.
 19.     Component 2: Portfolio Risk-Sharing Facility. The component will finance a portfolio risk-sharing facility (PRSF)
 and related capacity building to share the credit risk of eligible MSME loans with participating financial institutions (PFIs),
 in order to support MSMEs facing collateral constraints in normal times and the higher risk aversion of banks during the
 COVID-19 crisis.

 21.      Component 4: Project implementation, coordination, and management Component 4 will finance overall project

 implementation costs for staff and operating expenses, and commission fees to agencies and Government entities in
 project implementation.

     Summary of Screening of Environmental and Social Risks and Impacts
     .


 22.       Both environmental and social risks are rated Moderate for the proposed project; therefore, the overall ESF risk
 is also Moderate. These risks are covered by ESS 1, ESS 2, ESS 9, and ESS 10. Environmental risks are limited as Component
 1 will not finance any civil works and, under Component 2 and 3, MSMEs that involve substantial, or high environmental
 or social risk will not be eligible for guarantees and loans. On the social front, the following risks are to be addressed: (i)
 managing exclusion/inclusion errors; (ii) addressing the limited environment and social risk management capacity of PFIs;
 (iii) labor management, including occupational health and safety considerations; and (iv) outreach - through an effective
 and inclusive Information, Education, and Communication Campaign (IEC).
